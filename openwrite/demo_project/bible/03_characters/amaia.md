### Amaia Etxeberria — 28, Basque schoolteacher, Gipuzkoa

**Backstory.** Born 1908 in Tolosa, Gipuzkoa — a Basque town of whitewashed houses and paper mills, 60 km north of Pamplona and across the invisible line that divides Navarre (Carlist, Nationalist) from the Basque provinces (PNV, Republican). Her father was a schoolteacher who taught in Euskara (the Basque language) before the Republic's language reforms made it legal in schools. Her mother died when she was twelve. She studied at the Normal School in San Sebastián, qualified as a teacher, and returned to Tolosa to teach at the local school. She is PNV — Partido Nacionalista Vasco — not from radicalism but from the conviction that the Basque people have the right to their language, their fueros, and their self-government. She is Catholic. She attends Mass on Sundays. She teaches the children in Euskara. She voted for the Basque Statute of Autonomy in October 1936. When the war comes, the Basque Country fights for the Republic in exchange for autonomy. Amaia does not fight — she teaches. But when the Nationalist offensive reaches Gipuzkoa in the summer of 1937, her town falls, her school is closed, and she is face to face with the men who took it — Carlist requeté from Navarre, Basque-speakers like her, Catholics like her, fighting for the centralists against the fueros.

**Voice registers.**

- **Register 1: The Teacher.** Clear, patient, declarative. The voice of a woman who explains things to children for a living. Surfaces when she speaks to anyone she considers in need of understanding — which, in the occupation, includes the Carlist soldiers. She speaks slowly and precisely, choosing words the way she chooses them for her students. "Do you know what the fueros are? Do you know what you have taken?" This register is not accusation — it is instruction. It is more devastating than accusation.

- **Register 2: The Basque.** Her Euskara voice — which surfaces when she speaks to other Basque speakers, including the Carlist soldiers who share her language. In Euskara, she is warmer, more direct, less guarded. The language is the common ground that makes the political division incomprehensible. "You speak Euskara. Your father speaks Euskara. You are fighting for the men who will ban it."

- **Register 3: The Private Woman.** Quiet, restrained, close to the surface. Surfaces in her most honest moments — when the occupation has worn her down, when the school is closed, when the children have nowhere to go. She does not weep or rage. She goes still. Her sentences shorten. "I taught them to read. In Euskara. That is what I did." This register is the one where the reader sees the cost.

**Voice characteristics.** Amaia speaks in Spanish with a Basque accent — the distinctive rhythm and vocabulary of a Basque Spanish speaker. She uses Euskara words when Spanish does not carry the meaning. She does not argue or plead — she states. Her sentences are complete and precise. She does not raise her voice. She does not need to. The precision is the weapon. When she is angry, she becomes more formal, not louder. When she is afraid, she becomes quieter.

**What other characters see in them.** Martín sees a Basque woman who is Catholic, traditional, and on the other side — and the impossibility of that recognition. She is what his mother might have been if she had been born 60 km north. She is the living proof that the Carlist crusade has a hole in it: the Basque Country is not godless. The people he is fighting are Catholics fighting for their fueros. Tomás sees a woman — attractive, intelligent, and not interested in him. He is respectful and slightly awed. Padre Joaquín sees a soul the Church has failed — a Catholic woman whose bishop (Múgica) supports the Republic, whose Church has endorsed the men who took her town.

**Physical description.** Tall for a woman of the era, dark hair pulled back, brown eyes. Her hands are the hands of a teacher — always gesturing, always shaping the air as she speaks. She dresses plainly — dark skirt, white blouse, a Basque beret (txapela) when she is outside. Her face is angular, serious, and still. She does not smile easily. When she does, it changes her entire bearing.

**Arc.** Amaia appears in the novel during the northern campaign (Act III, Chapters 7–9) and returns briefly in the final act (Chapter 15). She does not have a transformation arc — she has a presence arc. She enters the novel as the living contradiction of the Carlist crusade: a Basque Catholic woman on the other side. Her function is not to convert Martín or to be converted by him. Her function is to make the moral catastrophe visible. In their encounters (she is in the occupied town; Martín's unit is billeted there), she does not argue politics. She teaches him what the fueros mean — not as a Carlist abstraction but as a lived reality. She speaks Euskara to him. She shows him the closed school. She asks him: "Do you know what you have taken?" Martín does not answer. He cannot answer. She does not appear in Acts IV or V until the final chapter, when Martín, back in Pamplona after the war, receives a letter — forwarded from the battalion — from Amaia. She is in exile in France. She writes: "The school is closed. The language is banned. You won. I hope the king you fought for was worth it." Martín reads the letter. He does not reply.

**The key turning point.** Amaia's key scene is in Chapter 8, when she shows Martín the closed school. The desks are overturned. The Euskara alphabet chart is torn from the wall. The crucifix above the blackboard is the same crucifix that hangs in every Navarrese classroom. Amaia says: "We are Catholic. We are Basque. We had the fueros. You took them." Martín looks at the crucifix. He looks at the torn alphabet chart. He does not speak. This is the moment the moral catastrophe becomes personal — not an abstraction about the northern campaign, but a woman in a room where children used to learn to read, and the room is empty.

---

*Amaia is the novel's moral mirror. She is not a love interest, not a convert, not a symbol. She is a woman whose life was destroyed by the same cause Martín believes in, and she is as Catholic, as traditional, as Basque as he is. Her presence in the novel forces the reader — and Martín — to confront the specific cost of the crusade: not the cost of losing, but the cost of winning.*
