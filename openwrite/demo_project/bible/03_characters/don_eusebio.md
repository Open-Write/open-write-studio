### Don Eusebio Echeverría — 58, Carlist elder, Pamplona patriarch

**Backstory.** Born 1878 in Pamplona. His father, Javier Echeverría, fought in the Third Carlist War (1872–1876) as a young requeté and brought back a wooden crucifix that now hangs above the dining table. Don Eusebio grew up in the shadow of that crucifix and the stories his father told — the ambushes, the prayers before battle, the king who never came. He enlisted in the Requeté himself in the 1900s, during the Carlist mobilizations of the Restoration period. He never saw combat — the Third War was the last real Carlist war — but he wore the red beret, drilled on Sundays, and attended the annual commemorations. He married Pilar Larrañaga in 1905. She died of influenza in 1918, during the pandemic. He never remarried. He raised his two sons — Ignacio (born 1908) and Martín (born 1914) — in the Carlist tradition. Ignacio died of tuberculosis in 1932. Martín is all that remains. Don Eusebio runs the hardware store on the Calle Mayor. He is a member of the Carlist Communion's local junta. He speaks at the annual requeté commemorations. He is not a man of words — he is a man of objects: the crucifix, the red beret, the store, the stone house. His authority comes from what he has endured, not from what he says.

**Voice registers.**

- **Register 1: The Patriarch.** Measured, declarative, final. Short sentences that close rather than open. Surfaces in public — at the junta, at the commemorations, in the store. "The fueros are not negotiable. The king is the king. We have fought for a century and we will fight again." This register has the weight of repetition behind it — these are sentences that have been said before, by his father, by the men of 1872.

- **Register 2: The Father.** Softer, more tentative, longer sentences. Surfaces in private with Martín — and only with Martín. The subjunctive appears. "I would ask you to remember. I would hope that you carry this." This register is the only one that reveals doubt, and it does so through indirection — what Don Eusebio does not say is more revealing than what he does.

- **Register 3: The Silence.** Not a voice but an absence. Surfaces when the news is too heavy for words. Don Eusebio does not rage, weep, or explain. He goes quiet. He stands at the window. He touches the crucifix. This register is most powerful in the final act, when the king does not come and the fueros are abolished and Don Eusebio says nothing.

**Voice characteristics.** Don Eusebio speaks in the Carlist register — formal, Catholic, Carlist invocations woven into everyday speech. "God willing." "If Christ the King permits." "As my father said." He does not use contractions or colloquialisms. He addresses Martín as "tú" but with the gravity of a man who uses "usted" in his head. He is not loquacious. His letters to Martín at the front are short — a few sentences about the store, the weather, the town. The Carlist content is between the lines.

**What other characters see in them.** Martín sees the man who gave him the red beret and the crucifix — the man whose faith he is trying to live up to. After the unification, Martín begins to see the man who sent him to fight for something that will never arrive. Padre Joaquín sees a Carlist whose faith is political as much as theological — a man who believes in the fueros as much as in the sacraments. Salazar sees a relic — a man of the old order who must be absorbed into the new.

**Physical description.** Tall, stooped, white hair cropped close. Hands that are large and scarred from the hardware store. He wears a dark jacket and a white shirt every day — the dress of a Pamplona bourgeois, unchanged since the 1900s. He walks slowly but with the economy of a man who has walked the same streets for sixty years. His face is long, lined, and still. He does not gesture when he speaks.

**Arc.** Don Eusebio begins as the Carlist patriarch — the man who represents the tradition, the faith, the century of sacrifice. He sends Martín to war with the wooden crucifix and the red beret, and he believes — has believed all his life — that this war will deliver the Carlist king. The unification decree is the first blow: the movement his father fought for is absorbed into a single party. He writes bitter letters to Martín — short, tight, controlled. The northern campaign is the second blow: Carlists killing Basque Catholics. He does not write about this. Teruel and the Ebro are the slow erosion — Martín's letters grow shorter, less certain. Don Eusebio reads them and says nothing. The war's end is the final silence: Franco enters Madrid. There is no Carlist king. The fueros are abolished. The Church praises the Caudillo. Don Eusebio hangs the crucifix on the wall and says nothing. His arc is not a journey — it is a contraction. The patriarch becomes a man in a room with a crucifix, waiting for a grandson who may or may not carry the tradition forward.

**The key turning point.** The unification decree (February 17, 1937). Don Eusebio receives the news in Pamplona — not from a letter but from the newspapers, from the flags, from the yoke-and-arrows appearing alongside the red beret. He attends the junta meeting. The elders argue. Some resist. Some comply. Don Eusebio says: "My father fought for the king. I sent my son to fight for the king. The king was not consulted." He leaves the meeting. He goes home. He stands at the window. This is the moment the century-long faith begins to crack.

---

*Don Eusebio is the Carlist tradition made flesh. His silence in the final scene carries the weight of three wars, a dead wife, a dead elder son, and a younger son who has been hollowed out by the victory Don Eusebio spent his life believing in.*
