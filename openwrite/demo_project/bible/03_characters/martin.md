### Martín Javier Echeverría — 22, Carlist requeté volunteer, protagonist

**Backstory.** Born 1914 in Pamplona. Second son of Don Eusebio Echeverría, a Carlist elder and former requeté. Grew up in a stone house on the Calle Mayor, three blocks from the Plaza del Castillo. His childhood was shaped by two objects: the red beret his father wore in the 1920s campaigns, hanging on a hook in the hallway, and the wooden crucifix from his grandfather who fought in the Third Carlist War (1872–1876), mounted above the dining table. He studied at the seminary for two years (ages 16–18) before leaving — not from a crisis of faith but from the discovery that he was not called to the priesthood. He worked in his father's hardware store. He played pelota on Sundays. He attended Mass every morning. He was not political in the sense of speeches and pamphlets — he was political in the sense that his faith, his family, and his town were Carlist, and Carlism was not an opinion but a condition of being. He enlisted in the Requeté on July 18, 1936, the day the mobilization order came. He did not hesitate. He did not need to be persuaded. This was what the red beret was for.

**Voice registers.**

- **Register 1: The Catechism.** Clear, declarative sentences. The voice of conviction. Surfaces when Martín speaks about the cause — God, king, the fueros. Short sentences. No hedging. The syntax of a man who has been taught the answers and has not yet been tested on them. "We fight for the king. We fight for the fueros. We fight for Christ the King." This register fades as the war progresses, replaced by something harder and less certain.

- **Register 2: The Son.** Quieter, deferential, slightly formal. Surfaces in the presence of his father, Padre Joaquín, or anyone he recognizes as an elder. Subjunctive constructions — "I would hope," "It seems to me." The syntax of a young man who knows his place in a hierarchy. This register cracks in Act III when Martín begins to see that the elders do not have the answers.

- **Register 3: The Soldier.** Concrete, physical, present-tense in its attention. Surfaces in combat and in the company of Tomás and the other requeté. Short observations. Sensory. No abstraction. "The dust is red. The road is long. Tomás is sleeping." This register strengthens as the war progresses — the body's language replacing the catechism's language.

- **Register 4: The Fracture.** Fragments. Questions without answers. The syntax of a man whose framework has collapsed. Surfaces in the final two acts. "The beret. The crucifix. The king who did not come." This register is not despair — it is the space where certainty used to be. It surfaces only in private moments, never in front of the men.

**Voice characteristics.** Martín speaks in complete sentences when he is certain and in fragments when he is not. He does not use profanity — not from prudishness but from the Carlist register, where blasphemy is a sin, not an emphasis. He says "Dios" and "Jesús" as invocations, not expletives. He is not eloquent. He is precise. He reaches for concrete nouns — the road, the beret, the rifle, the crucifix — not abstract ones. He deflects personal questions with short answers. He does not explain himself. When he is uncomfortable, he adjusts his beret.

**What other characters see in them.** Don Eusebio sees the son who will carry the Carlist standard — and, later, the son who is being hollowed out by the war the father sent him to. Tomás sees a good soldier, a little too serious, a little too pious, but someone you want beside you in a trench. Padre Joaquín sees a soul in danger — the danger of faith that has not been tested, and later, the danger of faith that has been tested and found wanting. Salazar sees a Carlist who needs to be integrated — and, later, a man whose specific tradition is being absorbed and who knows it. Amaia sees an enemy who is also Catholic, also Basque-speaking, also fighting for the fueros — and the impossibility of that recognition.

**Physical description.** Medium height, lean, dark hair cut short. Brown eyes. Hands that are calloused from the hardware store, not from farming. He moves with the economy of a man who is not tall enough to waste motion. The red beret is always on his head or in his hand — it is the first thing he puts on in the morning and the last thing he takes off. His posture changes over the course of the novel: straight-backed and certain in Act I, progressively curved in Acts III–IV, and finally still in Act V — not collapsed, but emptied.

**Arc.** Martín begins as a believer — not naive, but certain. His faith is specific: God, king, the fueros. He enlists without hesitation because the crusade is what the red beret is for. He marches south, fights at Madrid, survives Guadalajara. He is not disillusioned by combat — combat is what he expected. The turn is the unification decree (February 1937): Franco merges the Carlists into the Falange. The red beret must now bear the Falangist yoke-and-arrows. This is the first crack. The northern campaign (June–October 1937) widens it: Martín fights Basque Nationalists — Catholic men like him, fighting for the same fueros from the other side. The moral catastrophe is unspeakable and unspoken. Teruel (December 1937–February 1938) grinds him down. The Ebro (July–November 1938) takes Tomás and the last of his certainty. By the war's end (March–April 1939), Martín is alive, victorious, and emptied. He returns to Pamplona. The red beret hangs on the wall. The crucifix remains. The king did not come. The fueros are abolished. The Church praises Franco. Martín does not denounce the crusade — he does not have the language for denunciation. He does not embrace it — he does not have the faith for embrace. He stands in the space between, and the novel ends there.

**The key turning point.** The unification decree (February 17, 1937). This is the moment the crusade begins to hollow out. Not a battle, not a death — a bureaucratic act. A decree. The red beret must bear a new symbol. The Carlist king is not consulted. The fueros are not mentioned. The movement that Martín's family has fought for across three generations is absorbed into a single party controlled by a man who wants power, not a kingdom. This is the wound that never heals.

---

*The Carlist calendar marks Martín's journey: the feast of Santiago (July 25) at the start, the feast of the Virgen del Pilar (October 12) during the march south, the feast of San Fermín (July 7) during the northern campaign — the same saint whose festival he celebrated as a child in Pamplona, now a date on a military calendar in enemy territory.*
