### Padre Joaquín — 50, Carlist military chaplain, Requeté battalion

**Backstory.** Born 1886 in Estella (Lizarra), Navarre — the Carlist capital, where the Pretender Don Carlos held his court during the Third Carlist War. Ordained in 1910. Served as a parish priest in small Navarrese villages — Aberin, Los Arcos, Puente la Reina — for twenty years. His theology is Carlist theology: the faith is not separate from the state; the state is the faith's instrument. The king rules by divine right. The fueros are God's gift to the Basque-Navarrese people. The Church is not one institution among many — it is the foundation of all institutions. He volunteered as a military chaplain in July 1936, not because he wanted to go to war but because the Requeté needed a priest and he was available. He blessed the red berets. He heard confessions before the first mobilization. He said Mass in the Pamplona barracks on the morning the battalion marched south. He has been with the unit ever since — through Madrid, the northern campaign, Teruel, the Ebro. He has given last rites to more men than he can count. He has told them God is with them. He is no longer sure God is with anyone.

**Voice registers.**

- **Register 1: The Pastor.** Warm, measured, pastoral. The voice of a man who has spent thirty years in the confessional. Full sentences, gentle cadences, the rhythm of homily. Surfaces in his official capacity — saying Mass, blessing the men, writing letters to families. "God sees your sacrifice. God knows your heart. The crusade is His work, and you are His instruments." This register has not changed since the war began. Its constancy is both a comfort and, increasingly, a lie.

- **Register 2: The Confessor.** Quiet, close, almost whispered. The voice of the man behind the screen. Surfaces in private conversations — with Martín, with the dying. Short sentences. Questions rather than declarations. "When you pulled the trigger. What did you feel. Tell me." This register is the one where Padre Joaquín does his real work — not the work of the Church, but the work of one man listening to another. It is also the register where his doubt leaks through.

- **Register 3: The Doubt.** Halting, unfinished. The syntax of a man whose framework is cracking. Surfaces in his most private moments — alone in his tent, writing in his journal, or in the single conversation with Martín where the doubt becomes explicit. "I blessed the bullet. I blessed the hand that fired it. I blessed the man who died from it. I am not sure these are the same blessing." This register appears only in the final third of the novel. Its rarity is the point.

**Voice characteristics.** Padre Joaquín speaks in the register of the Navarrese priesthood — formal, Catholic, with the cadences of liturgical Spanish. He uses "hijo" (son) when addressing the requeté, not "soldado" (soldier). He quotes Scripture naturally, not as display but as habit. He does not argue or debate. He asks questions. His sentences grow shorter as the war progresses — the pastoral fluency eroding, replaced by pauses and incomplete thoughts. He blesses everything: the rifles, the berets, the water, the dead. The blessings become less certain.

**What other characters see in them.** Martín sees the spiritual authority — the man who represents God's presence in the battalion. In the early acts, this is comforting. In the later acts, it becomes a question: if Padre Joaquín doubts, what does Martín have left? Don Eusebio sees a good priest, a Carlist priest, the kind of man who should be blessing the requeté. The other soldiers see a man who is always there — in the trench, in the field hospital, at the grave. They do not see his doubt. They see his presence.

**Physical description.** Short, slight, tonsure visible through thinning hair. He wears the black cassock with a military blanket over his shoulders — the hybrid dress of a priest at the front. His face is narrow, lined, deeply tanned. His hands are small and always moving — blessing, touching, adjusting his stole. He carries a leather pouch with communion wafers, a small bottle of wine, and a prayer book. He walks with a slight limp from a childhood injury.

**Arc.** Padre Joaquín begins as the crusade's spiritual anchor — the man who gives the war its theological meaning. He blesses the bullets because the cause is just. He hears the confessions because the men are doing God's work. He tells them God is with them because he believes it. The unification decree (February 1937) is the first theological problem: the Church's endorsement of Franco does not distinguish between Carlist and Falangist. The fueros are not mentioned. The Carlist king is not crowned. Padre Joaquín blesses the yoke-and-arrows alongside the red beret, but the blessing tastes different. The northern campaign (June–October 1937) is the moral catastrophe: he gives last rites to requeté who killed Basque Nationalists — and he gives last rites to the Basque dead when no Basque priest is available. He administers the same sacrament to both sides. He begins to ask: if God is with us, is God also with them? Teruel grinds him down. The Ebro breaks him. By the final act, he is a man who still says Mass, still hears confessions, still blesses — but the blessings are rote, the homilies are shorter, and the doubt has moved from his private register to his pastoral one. The men notice. They say nothing.

**The key turning point.** The northern campaign, specifically the moment when Padre Joaquín administers last rites to a dying Basque Nationalist soldier. The man is Catholic. He is praying. He asks for absolution. Padre Joaquín gives it. The man dies. Padre Joaquín stands up and walks to the requeté lines and says nothing. This is the moment the theological framework cracks — not because the man was an enemy, but because the man was not an enemy. He was a Catholic dying in a ditch, and Padre Joaquín blessed him, and the blessing was the same blessing he gives to the requeté.

---

*Padre Joaquín is the novel's theological conscience. His doubt does not resolve into atheism or heresy — it remains within the Church, within the faith, within the Carlist tradition. He does not stop believing. He stops being certain. The difference is the novel's central spiritual question: can faith survive the collapse of certainty? Can God exist in the space where the crusade used to be?*
