### Tomás Eguía — 24, Carlist requeté, Martín's closest companion

**Backstory.** Born 1912 in Aoiz (Agoitz), a small town in the Navarrese valleys, 30 km south of Pamplona. His father is a shepherd. His mother runs the family's smallholding — a few hectares of wheat and sheep on the hillside above the town. Tomás is the fourth of six children. He did not attend the seminary, did not study law, did not read the Carlist manifestos. He enlisted in the Requeté because his three older brothers enlisted and his father expected him to follow. His Carlist faith is not theological — it is tribal. The red beret is what men in his family wear. The crucifix is above the door. The fueros are what the Navarrese have always had. He does not argue about the king or the theocratic monarchy. He fights because his brothers fight and his father fought and the men of Aoiz have always fought. He is practical, physical, good with his hands. He can fix a rifle, dig a trench, and cook rice over a wood fire. He is not pious. He does not attend daily Mass. But he carries a scapular his mother sewed, and he touches it before battle.

**Voice registers.**

- **Register 1: The Companion.** Easy, colloquial, half-sentences. The voice of a man comfortable in his own skin. Surfaces in camp, on the march, in the trenches with Martín. He speaks in Navarrese dialect — Basque-inflected Spanish with dropped consonants and local idioms. "Vamos, tío. It's not so bad. At least the bread is fresh today." He deflects seriousness with humor and discomfort with silence. This register is the one Martín knows best.

- **Register 2: The Brother.** Blunt, direct, no decoration. Surfaces when Tomás is angry or afraid. Short sentences. No dialect — the fear strips the accent. "They're coming. Get down. Don't move." This register appears in combat and in the moments before combat, when the joking stops and the body takes over.

- **Register 3: The Son.** Quiet, hesitant, almost inaudible. Surfaces in his letters home — which he dictates to Martín because his writing is slow. "Tell my mother I am well. Tell her the food is not so bad. Tell her I will come home." This register is the closest Tomás comes to vulnerability, and it surfaces only when he is away from the other men.

**Voice characteristics.** Tomás speaks in fragments and contractions. He does not complete sentences when the meaning is clear. He uses dialect freely and without self-consciousness. He swears — not blasphemy (that is Carlist territory) but soldier's profanity — "mierda," "hostias" (used as expletive, not invocation), "joder." He deflects with humor. He does not talk about the cause. He talks about food, sleep, women, home. When Martín speaks about God and king, Tomás listens and says nothing — not from disapproval but from the recognition that these are not his words. He calls Martín "cura" (priest) as a nickname, gently mocking Martín's seminary past.

**What other characters see in them.** Martín sees the companion — the man beside him in the trench, the one who keeps him human when the crusade becomes a machine. Tomás is the antidote to ideology: he fights because he is here, not because of a doctrine. Martín loves him without saying so. Padre Joaquín sees a good man who is not particularly religious — the kind of soul the Church claims but has not shaped. Don Eusebio sees a common man, not a Carlist of the old school — but a loyal one. Salazar sees the base material of the army: the men who fight without understanding the ideology, which makes them useful and disposable.

**Physical description.** Stocky, broad-shouldered, dark hair that grows fast and is always slightly too long. A round face that is quick to smile. Hands that are large and scarred from shepherding. He moves with the ease of a man who is comfortable in his body — rolling his shoulders, cracking his knuckles, stretching in the morning like a cat. He carries more than his share of the unit's supplies because he is strong and does not complain.

**Arc.** Tomás does not have a transformation arc. He is the same man at the end as at the beginning — practical, loyal, unideological. His arc is the arc of what happens to that man in a war that consumes everything. In Act I, he is the companion — the human counterweight to Martín's crusade fervor. In Act II, he is the trench mate — the man who shares food, keeps watch, and does not ask why they are fighting. In Act III, he follows Martín through the northern campaign without comment — he fights Basque Nationalists because the orders say to, not because he understands the fueros. In Act IV, he is killed at the Ebro. His death is not heroic. It is not meaningful. It is a mortar round that lands in the wrong place at the wrong time. Martín holds him. Tomás does not say anything profound. He says: "Tell my mother." He does not finish the sentence.

**The key turning point.** Tomás's death at the Ebro (Chapter 12). It is the novel's emotional center — the moment when the war's cost becomes personal and irreversible. Tomás is not a symbol. He is a man who wanted to go home. His death does not serve the crusade or the cause or the narrative. It serves nothing. It is waste. And Martín, holding him, understands for the first time that the crusade has consumed the very men it was supposed to save.

---

*Tomás is the novel's heart. His death is the wound from which Martín does not recover. The reader must love Tomás — not for what he represents but for what he is: a man who fought because his family expected him to, who died because a mortar round does not distinguish between believers and non-believers, and whose last word was an unfinished sentence to a mother who will wait for a letter that will not come.*
