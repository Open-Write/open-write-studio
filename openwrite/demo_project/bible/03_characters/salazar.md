### Comandante Ignacio Salazar — 35, Falangist officer, integration overseer

**Backstory.** Born 1901 in Valladolid. Son of a Castilian railway official — neither aristocratic nor working-class, but the bureaucratic middle that Spain's political convulsions always overlook. Studied law at the University of Valladolid. Read José Antonio Primo de Rivera's early speeches in 1933 and recognized in them something he had been waiting for: a vision of Spain that was neither the liberal Republic's secular individualism nor the Carlists' backward-looking monarchism, but something new — national syndicalism, a state built on workers' syndicates, national pride, and spiritual purpose without the Church's institutional weight. He joined the Falange in 1934. He organized cells in Valladolid. He was arrested briefly in 1935, released, and resumed. When the uprising came in July 1936, he was among the first to mobilize. He fought at the front in the early months — not as a soldier but as a political officer, organizing morale, distributing José Antonio's writings, building the Falangist vision from the ground up. José Antonio's execution (November 20, 1936) transformed him from an organizer into a zealot. The martyr's death made the cause sacred. Then came the unification decree (February 17, 1937): Franco merged the Falange and the Carlist Communion into a single party — Falange Española Tradicionalista y de las JONS. Salazar was assigned to integrate Carlist units. He believes the unification will strengthen the Nationalist cause. He does not yet understand that Franco has used the unification to neuter both the Falange and the Carlists — burying José Antonio's radicalism under the Caudillo's conservatism. He is the instrument of Carlist subordination, and he is himself being subordinated.

**Voice registers.**

- **Register 1: The Falangist.** Precise, ideological, rhythmic. Longer sentences than the Carlists use — the syntax of a man who has read manifestos and written pamphlets. Vocabulary drawn from José Antonio's speeches: "new Spain," "national syndicalism," "spiritual revolution." Surfaces in public meetings, in the integration sessions, in his initial encounters with Martín. "The unification is not the death of your tradition. It is the birth of a greater one. The red beret and the yoke-and-arrows are one."

- **Register 2: The Pragmatist.** Sharper, more tactical, shorter sentences. Surfaces when Salazar is under pressure — when the Carlists resist, when the military overrides him, when the gap between the Falangist vision and Franco's reality becomes visible. "The war needs unity. Your king understands this. Your junta understands this. You should understand this." This register is the one that reveals the cracks — the moments when Salazar is enforcing an order he did not choose.

- **Register 3: The Private Man.** Quiet, reflective, almost conversational. Surfaces in rare moments of honesty — with Martín, late at night, after the integration sessions have failed. The ideology falls away and what remains is a man who believed in something beautiful and is watching it be buried. "José Antonio said we would build a new Spain. He did not say the new Spain would look like this." This register appears only twice in the novel — once in Act III and once in Act V — and its rarity is the point.

**Voice characteristics.** Salazar speaks in complete sentences. He does not use fragments or colloquialisms. His Castilian accent marks him as an outsider in Navarre — his Spanish is flatter, more bureaucratic, less inflected than the Navarrese speech of the Carlists. He uses the formal "usted" with everyone, including Martín — a Falangist habit, emphasizing equality through formality. He quotes José Antonio without attribution, as if the founder's words are scripture. He is articulate and controlled. When he is angry, he becomes more precise, not louder.

**What other characters see in them.** Martín sees the man who is taking the red beret away — the Falangist who arrives with a decree and a new symbol. He resists Salazar instinctively. Over time, he begins to see that Salazar is also being taken from — that the Falangist vision is being buried alongside the Carlist one. Don Eusebio sees a functionary of the regime that betrayed the Carlist cause. Padre Joaquín sees a man without God — a political animal who substitutes ideology for faith. Teniente Vidal sees a useful political officer who keeps the Carlists in line.

**Physical description.** Medium height, compact, dark hair parted neatly. Clean-shaven. He wears the Falangist blue shirt with an officer's jacket — the hybrid uniform of the unified party. His movements are precise and controlled. He does not smoke or drink. His hands are smooth — a desk man's hands, not a soldier's. He carries a leather briefcase with Falangist documents and a copy of José Antonio's collected speeches.

**Arc.** Salazar begins as a true believer in the Falangist vision — not Franco's Falange, but José Antonio's original radicalism. He is assigned to the Carlist integration with genuine conviction: the unification will build a new Spain. He arrives in Martín's battalion and finds resistance — the Carlists do not want to be unified. He persists. He is articulate, patient, and firm. The cracks appear in Act III: the military overrides his political decisions. Franco's conservatism buries José Antonio's radicalism. The unified party becomes a rubber stamp. Salazar continues to enforce the integration, but his sentences grow shorter, his certainty less visible. By the Ebro (Act IV), he is a functionary — doing his job, not building a vision. In the final act, after the war, he is in Pamplona for the victory parade. He sees Martín. They exchange a few words. Salazar says: "We built a new Spain." Martín says nothing. Salazar walks away. The reader understands: the new Spain is Franco's Spain, not José Antonio's, not the Carlists'. Salazar's vision has been buried alongside the one he helped bury.

**The key turning point.** The unification decree is Salazar's opportunity — the moment when the Falangist vision seems to be winning. But the real turn comes later, in Act III, when Salazar realizes that Franco has used the unification to neuter both movements. He does not say this aloud. He does not rebel. He continues to enforce the integration. But his private register surfaces for the first time: "José Antonio said we would build a new Spain. He did not say the new Spain would look like this." This is the moment the reader understands that Salazar is not a villain — he is another kind of casualty.

---

*Salazar is the novel's most important non-Carlist character. He represents the Falangist alternative — a genuine political vision that is also consumed by Franco. The reader must be able to hear his argument and feel its pull. The novel fails if Salazar is a foil. The novel succeeds if the reader recognizes that Salazar and Martín are on the same side of a wall they cannot see — both believing in something specific and beautiful, both watching Franco bury it.*
