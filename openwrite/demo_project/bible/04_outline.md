## IV. STORY OUTLINE

**Overall structure.** Five acts, three chapters each, fifteen chapters total. The structure mirrors the war's arc — but the war is the vehicle, not the subject. Each act corresponds to a phase of Martín's arc: conviction, advance, erosion, attrition, and the silence of victory.

**The Carlist calendar.** Each act opens with a reference to a Carlist feast day or historical anniversary, connecting the present war to the three Carlist wars of the 19th century. This device is structural, not decorative — it reminds the reader (and Martín) that the tradition being destroyed is a century old.

**Timeline.** Linear. No flashbacks. The war's chronology is the novel's chronology. The structure enforces inevitability — the reader moves forward with Martín toward a victory that is also a defeat.

**POV.** Third-person limited, Martín's perspective exclusively. The reader knows only what Martín knows, sees only what he sees. Other characters' interiorities are visible only through their behavior, their voices, and what Martín can read in their faces.

### ACT ONE — THE CRUSADE  (Chapters 1–3)

*July – October 1936. The Carlist mobilization. Martín marches south. The crusade begins.*

**1. The Red Beret.** July 18, 1936. Pamplona. The mobilization order comes. Martín is in the hardware store with his father, Don Eusebio. The Carlist Communion's runners bring the word: Mola has risen. The Requeté assembles at the cuartel. Don Eusebio gives Martín the wooden crucifix — the one from his grandfather who fought in the Third Carlist War. "Carry this. It has been in three wars." Martín puts on the red beret. The chapter is set in the hours before departure — the streets of Pamplona filling with men in red berets, the church bells, the Plaza del Castillo. Padre Joaquín blesses the assembled requeté. Don Eusebio watches from the edge of the plaza. He does not embrace Martín. He adjusts the beret on his son's head and steps back. Tomás Eguía appears — a stocky man from Aoiz, already carrying more than his share. "I'm Tomás. I'll be beside you." They march south.

*Emotional palette: [Conviction], [Farewell]. The reader should feel the weight of the tradition being carried forward — and the first, faint edge of what it will cost.*

**2. The March South.** July–August 1936. The requeté battalion moves south through Castile by train and on foot. Martín sees the meseta for the first time — flat, brown, endless. The Castilian landscape is alien to a man from the green valleys of Navarre. He meets Teniente Vidal, the professional Army officer assigned to command the regular soldiers in the battalion. Vidal is efficient, unideological, and respected. He organizes the requeté's raw courage into something militarily functional. Martín is billeted with Tomás and a dozen other volunteers in a Castilian village. The villagers are friendly — they are Nationalist, Catholic, conservative. But they are not Carlist. They do not know what the fueros are. Martín begins to understand that the crusade means different things to different people. Tomás is unconcerned. "We fight. We win. We go home." Martín writes to his father. The letter is short: "We are well. The men are ready."

*Emotional palette: [Discovery], [Unease]. The reader should feel the breadth of the Nationalist coalition — and the first hint that the Carlist cause is one voice among many, not the voice.*

**3. First Blood.** September–October 1936. The battalion's first engagement. Not Madrid — a smaller action, a Republican-held village in the advance toward the capital. The requeté go in with the red berets and the prayers. Padre Joaquín says Mass before the attack. Martín kills a man for the first time. The man is running. Martín fires. The man falls. The chapter does not render this as a dramatic revelation — it renders it as a fact. Martín's hands shake afterward. Tomás gives him water. They dig in. That night, Martín holds the crucifix and prays. The prayer is not articulate. It is the body's prayer — the hands, the breath, the dark. The chapter ends with the battalion receiving news: they are being moved to the Madrid front. The war is about to become real.

*Emotional palette: [Terror], [Numbness]. The reader should feel the body's response to killing — and the gap between the crusade's rhetoric and the physical reality of a man falling.*

**END OF ACT ONE.**

### ACT TWO — THE ADVANCE  (Chapters 4–6)

*November 1936 – February 1937. Madrid. The unification decree. The crusade cracks.*

**4. The Siege.** November 1936. The battalion reaches the outskirts of Madrid. The Nationalist assault is underway — Army of Africa troops, Regulares, Legionnaires. The requeté are held in reserve. Martín hears the sounds of the battle — artillery, machine guns, the International Brigades entering the city. The assault fails. The Nationalists are thrown back. Martín watches from the reserve line as the dream of a quick victory dies. He sees the Army of Africa for the first time — Moorish Regulares, Legionnaires. Men who fight differently than the Carlist volunteers. Professional, brutal, efficient. Martín is awed and disturbed. Tomás is pragmatic: "At least they're on our side." Padre Joaquín blesses the dead from the assault. The requeté are not yet in the worst of it. But the war has changed scale. This is not a village. This is a capital.

*Emotional palette: [Awe], [Dread]. The reader should feel the war's scale — and the first crack in the crusade's narrative of quick, righteous victory.*

**5. The Yoke and the Arrows.** February 17, 1937. The unification decree. Franco merges the Falange and the Carlist Communion into a single party: Falange Española Tradicionalista y de las JONS. The requeté are told to wear the Falangist yoke-and-arrows symbol alongside the red beret. Comandante Salazar arrives to oversee the integration. He is articulate, disciplined, a true believer in the Falangist vision — not Franco's version, but José Antonio's original radicalism. He addresses the battalion: "The unification is not the death of your tradition. It is the birth of a greater one." Don Eusebio's letters from home grow bitter: "The king was not consulted. The junta is divided. Your grandfather's crucifix has been in three wars. This is not what he fought for." Martín wears the yoke-and-arrows. Tomás says: "It's a pin. It doesn't change anything." Martín is not sure.

*Emotional palette: [Betrayal], [Confusion]. The reader should feel the first wound — the bureaucratic act that begins to hollow out the crusade. This is not a battle. It is a decree. And it is worse.*

**MIDPOINT.** The unification decree (Chapter 5) is the novel's structural midpoint and its hinge. Everything before it is the crusade. Everything after it is the crusade's erosion. The reader has been watching a man march toward his own subordination and calling it heroism.

**6. The Field of Jarama.** February 1937. The battalion is moved to the Jarama front — south of Madrid, where the Nationalists are trying to cut the Madrid-Valencia road. The fighting is intense. The requeté hold a section of the line. Martín and Tomás share a trench. The International Brigades are across the line — German volunteers, British volunteers, men from fifty countries. The requeté are fighting not Spaniards but foreigners. The propaganda said the war was against godless Reds. The men across the line are not godless — some are Catholic, some are socialist, some are just volunteers who came to fight fascism. The trench warfare is brutal — snipers, mortar fire, the constant noise. Padre Joaquín hears confessions at night. The confessions grow longer. The chapter ends with a stalemate — neither side advances. The requeté hold. The cost is seventeen dead.

*Emotional palette: [Weariness], [Doubt]. The reader should feel the war's grind — and the gap between the crusade's rhetoric and the reality of men in trenches fighting men they do not know.*

**END OF ACT TWO.**

### ACT THREE — THE NORTHERN CAMPAIGN  (Chapters 7–9)

*June – October 1937. The moral catastrophe. Carlists fight Basque Catholics. The crusade collapses.*

**7. The Basque Country.** June 1937. After the fall of Bilbao (June 19), the requeté battalion is moved north for the campaign through Gipuzkoa and into the remaining Basque territory. The landscape changes — from the brown meseta to the green mountains. Martín recognizes the terrain. It looks like Navarre. It looks like home. The battalion enters a Basque town — Catholic, traditional, with a church and a plaza and a school. The town has been held by Basque Nationalists. The requeté take it. The townspeople are silent. They do not cheer. They do not resist. They watch from doorways as the men in red berets walk their streets. Martín sees a crucifix in the church — the same crucifix that hangs in every Navarrese church. He sees an Euskara alphabet chart in the school. He sees a Basque flag (ikurriña) that has been torn down and left in the street. The moral catastrophe begins here: these people are Catholic. They are Basque. They had the fueros. And the Carlists have taken them.

*Emotional palette: [Recognition], [Horror]. The reader should feel the moral ground shift — the moment when the enemy is revealed as a mirror.*

**8. The Schoolteacher.** July 1937. The battalion is billeted in the occupied town. Martín meets Amaia Etxeberria — a Basque schoolteacher whose school has been closed by the occupation. She is 28, Catholic, PNV, a speaker of Euskara. She does not argue politics. She teaches. She shows Martín the closed school. The desks are overturned. The Euskara alphabet chart is torn from the wall. The crucifix above the blackboard is the same crucifix that hangs in every Navarrese classroom. "We are Catholic. We are Basque. We had the fueros. You took them." Martín does not answer. He cannot answer. Amaia speaks Euskara to him — the same language his grandmother spoke. The common ground makes the political division incomprehensible. Tomás sees the encounter and says nothing. Padre Joaquín, who has been hearing confessions from the occupation, administers last rites to a dying Basque Nationalist soldier — a Catholic man praying for absolution. The same sacrament. The same God.

*Emotional palette: [Moral vertigo], [Grief]. The reader should feel the ground give way — the realization that the crusade has a hole in it that cannot be patched.*

**9. The Fallen Oak.** August–October 1937. The northern campaign continues — Santander falls (August), Asturias falls (October). The requeté fight through mountains and rain. The losses mount. The moral crisis does not resolve — it deepens. Martín's letters to his father grow shorter. Don Eusebio's letters grow bitter. "Your grandfather fought for the fueros. You are fighting for the men who will abolish them." Martín does not reply. The chapter ends with the entire northern coast under Nationalist control. The Republic has lost its coal, iron, and heavy industry. The Carlists have won the northern campaign. The cost: they have killed Catholic men fighting for the same fueros, and they have helped deliver the Basque Country to a regime that will ban the language, close the schools, and abolish the autonomy the Basques fought for. The chapter's final image: Martín stands in a village square. An old oak tree — a Basque oak, like the Gernikako Arbola — has been cut down by the retreating Republicans. The stump is raw. The roots are exposed. Martín looks at it and does not speak.

*Emotional palette: [Exhaustion], [Moral collapse]. The reader should feel the weight of what has been done — and the silence of a man who cannot articulate what it means.*

**END OF ACT THREE.**

### ACT FOUR — ATTRITION  (Chapters 10–12)

*December 1937 – November 1938. Teruel, Aragon, the Ebro. The machine grinds men down.*

**10. The Cold.** December 1937–February 1938. Teruel. The Republic's first major offensive catches the Nationalists by surprise. The requeté are sent to hold the line in the worst cold in living memory — twenty degrees below zero. Men freeze in trenches. Rifles jam. The ground is iron. Martín survives because Tomás keeps him alive — sharing blankets, digging deeper, refusing to let him sleep in the open. The fighting is house-to-house in a frozen town. The requeté take casualties. The chapter is deliberately claustrophobic — the world reduced to a trench, a blanket, a rifle, and the cold. There is no theology here. There is no crusade. There is survival. Padre Joaquín, aging and thin, moves through the lines blessing the frozen dead. His blessings are rote. His breath is visible. He does not sit down because he is afraid he will not get up.

*Emotional palette: [Desperation], [Numbness]. The reader should feel the cold — and the erasure of everything that is not survival.*

**11. The Homecoming.** March 1938. Martín receives leave — ten days. He returns to Pamplona. The city is unchanged — the same stone streets, the same church towers, the same Plaza del Castillo. But Martín is changed. He walks through the streets and sees the red berets and the yoke-and-arrows and the flags of the unified party. He visits his father. Don Eusebio is older. Stooped. Quieter. They sit at the dining table. The crucifix is above them. Don Eusebio does not ask about the war. He says: "The fueros are being discussed. In Burgos." A pause. "They are not being discussed well." Martín sleeps in his childhood room. The red beret is on the hook in the hallway — the same hook where his father's beret hung. He cannot sleep. He goes to the church. He kneels. He does not pray. He kneels. The leave ends. He returns to the front. Don Eusebio stands at the window and watches him go. He does not adjust the beret this time.

*Emotional palette: [Alienation], [Tenderness]. The reader should feel the distance that has opened between Martín and the world he left — and the love between father and son that persists in the absence of language.*

**12. The River.** July–November 1938. The Battle of the Ebro. The war's longest and bloodiest battle. The requeté are in the line for weeks — the blasted rock of the Ebro valley, the hills that look like the surface of the moon after bombardment. The fighting is attrition — point and counterpoint, hill and valley, attack and counterattack. Martín and Tomás share a position on a ridge. The air war is constant — German and Italian aircraft, Republican fighters, the sound of engines and bombs that never stops. Tomás is killed by a mortar round. Not heroic. Not meaningful. A round that lands in the wrong place at the wrong time. Martín holds him. Tomás says: "Tell my mother." He does not finish the sentence. Martín wraps him in a blanket and marks the position. The requeté hold the ridge. They are pulled from the line in October. Of the hundred and twenty men who entered the line in July, sixty-three walk out.

*Emotional palette: [Grief], [Desolation]. The reader should feel the death — not as drama but as waste. Tomás is not a symbol. He is a man who wanted to go home.*

**END OF ACT FOUR.**

### ACT FIVE — VICTORY  (Chapters 13–15)

*1939. The war ends. The crusade succeeds. The silence begins.*

**13. The Chaplain's Confession.** January 1939. Barcelona falls. The requeté are in a rear area, waiting. Martín visits Padre Joaquín in the field hospital — the chaplain has collapsed, not from a wound but from exhaustion and something else. They sit together in the quiet. Padre Joaquín speaks — not as a pastor but as a man. "I blessed the bullets. I blessed the men who fired them. I blessed the men who died from them. I am not sure these are the same blessing." He does not weep. He is past weeping. He says: "I told them God was with them. I was not sure God was with anyone." Martín listens. He does not have an answer. He holds the crucifix — the one from his grandfather. He does not know if it means what his father told him it meant. Padre Joaquín says: "Your faith is not gone. It is in a different place. I do not know where." The chapter ends with the news: the Republic is collapsing. The war will be over soon.

*Emotional palette: [Intimacy], [Spiritual desolation]. The reader should feel the quiet between two men who have lost the thing they carried — and the tenderness of the loss.*

**14. The End.** March–April 1939. The war ends. Franco enters Madrid on March 28. The requeté are not in Madrid — they are in a garrison in Aragon, waiting for orders. The news comes by radio. The men cheer. Some weep. Martín stands outside and listens. He does not cheer. He does not weep. He holds the crucifix. The war is over. The crusade has succeeded. There is no Carlist king. There is no theocratic monarchy. There is Franco. Salazar finds Martín in the evening. He says: "We built a new Spain." Martín says nothing. Salazar walks away. The chapter's final image: the requeté pack their equipment. The red berets go into the packs. The yoke-and-arrows remain on the caps. The beret is no longer the symbol. The party is the symbol.

*Emotional palette: [Emptiness], [Finality]. The reader should feel the victory arrive — and the absence of what it was supposed to deliver.*

**15. The Altar.** April–May 1939. Martín returns to Pamplona. The city celebrates. The requeté march through the streets. The red berets are visible — but they are outnumbered by the Falangist flags. Don Eusebio is in the crowd. He does not cheer. He watches his son march past. Martín visits Tomás's family in Aoiz. He gives Tomás's mother the scapular she sewed. He says: "He was brave. He was my friend." He does not say: "He died for nothing." He goes home. He stands in the dining room. The crucifix is above the table. The red beret is in his hand. Don Eusebio comes in. They stand together. Don Eusebio takes the crucifix from the wall, holds it, and hangs it back. He says nothing. Martín puts the red beret on the hook in the hallway. He goes to his room. He sits on the bed. A letter waits on the pillow — forwarded from the battalion. From Amaia, in exile in France. "The school is closed. The language is banned. You won. I hope the king you fought for was worth it." Martín reads the letter. He folds it. He places it under the crucifix on his bedside table. The final image: the crucifix on the wall. The red beret on the hook. The letter on the table. The silence.

*Emotional palette: [Silence], [Weight]. The reader should feel the absence — the space where the crusade used to be. The silence is not peace. It is the space where a century of Carlist faith was.*

**END.**

## Notes on the Outline

- **Callback seeds:**
  - The wooden crucifix (Ch1 → Ch6 → Ch9 → Ch13 → Ch15): Given by Don Eusebio, held in battle, questioned, shared with Padre Joaquín, hung on the wall.
  - The red beret (Ch1 → Ch5 → Ch9 → Ch14 → Ch15): Put on, forced to bear the yoke-and-arrows, worn through the campaigns, hung on the hook.
  - Tomás's scapular (Ch1 → Ch4 → Ch12 → Ch15): His mother's sewing, shared in the trench, recovered from his body, returned to his mother.
  - Amaia's school (Ch8 → Ch15): The closed school in the occupied town, the letter from exile.
  - Don Eusebio's silence (Ch1 → Ch11 → Ch15): Adjusts the beret, watches from the window, says nothing.
  - The oak tree (Ch9 → Ch15): The cut stump in the Basque village, the implied roots beneath.
  - "Tell my mother" (Ch12 → Ch15): Tomás's unfinished sentence, completed by Martín's visit to Aoiz.
  - Padre Joaquín's blessings (Ch1 → Ch6 → Ch9 → Ch13): Pastoral certainty → doubt → rote → confession.

- **Emotional palette annotations:** Each chapter aims for two distinct emotions in tension — conviction and farewell, recognition and horror, grief and numbness. The tension prevents sentimentality.

- **Reader state:**
  - After Ch1: The reader believes this is a crusade. The Carlist cause is specific and beautiful.
  - After Ch5: The reader realizes the crusade is being absorbed. The unification decree is the first betrayal.
  - After Ch8: The reader understands the moral catastrophe. The Carlists are fighting Catholics for the same fueros.
  - After Ch12: The reader feels the waste. Tomás's death is not meaningful. It is waste.
  - After Ch15: The reader sits with the silence. The crucifix is on the wall. The beret is on the hook. The king did not come.

- **Fixed dialogue:** 
  - "Tell my mother." — Tomás, Ch12 (unfinished).
  - "We are Catholic. We are Basque. We had the fueros. You took them." — Amaia, Ch8.
  - "The unification is not the death of your tradition. It is the birth of a greater one." — Salazar, Ch5.
  - "I blessed the bullets. I blessed the men who fired them. I blessed the men who died from them. I am not sure these are the same blessing." — Padre Joaquín, Ch13.
  - "You won. I hope the king you fought for was worth it." — Amaia's letter, Ch15.

- **Structural misdirection markers:**
  - Act I (misdirection active): The reader believes this is a war story about a young Carlist's crusade.
  - Act II (misdirection cracking): The unification decree reveals that the crusade is a vehicle for Franco's consolidation.
  - Act III (misdirection collapsed): The northern campaign reveals that the crusade has a hole in it — the enemy is not godless.
  - Act IV (the truth): The war is a machine. The crusade was the fuel.
  - Act V (the aftermath): Victory is the defeat. The silence is the answer.

- **Word count target:** 80,000–100,000 words. Average ~5,500–6,500 words per chapter. Chapters 4, 8, 12, and 15 may be longer (the key dramatic moments). Chapters 3, 10, and 11 may be shorter (the compression chapters).
