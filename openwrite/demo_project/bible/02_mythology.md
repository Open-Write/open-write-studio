# THE RED BERET — Section II: The World and Its Mythology

*This is the world-building document. It defines the historical setting, the political landscape, and the cultural terrain of the novel. See also: [`01_concept.md`](01_concept.md) for thematic frame.*

## II. THE WORLD AND ITS MYTHORY

**The world.** Historical fiction. Navarre, the Basque Country, Castile, Aragon, and Madrid — Spain, 1936–1939. The novel takes place entirely within the geography of the Spanish Civil War and its immediate aftermath. The world is real, the historical events are documented, and the setting is not speculative. The reader should recognize the landscape, the names, the dates. The novel's power comes from the fact that this happened.

The physical terrain matters: the red earth and church towers of Navarre, the Castilian meseta (flat, brown, endless), the mountains of the Basque Country (green, steep, ancient), the frozen trenches of Teruel, the blasted rock of the Ebro valley, the streets of Madrid at war's end. Each landscape carries a moral weight. Navarre is home and faith. Castile is the march south and the first cracks. The Basque Country is the moral catastrophe — Carlists fighting Basque Catholics for the same fueros from opposite sides. Teruel is the machine grinding men down. The Ebro is the abyss. Madrid at the end is the hollow throne.

**The central element: Carlism.** Carlism is the political and cultural movement at the novel's center. It is not a fiction. It is a century-old tradition with its own theology, its own military culture, its own martyrs, and its own vision of Spain.

Carlism's roots: the First Carlist War (1833–1840), when Don Carlos, brother of Ferdinand VII, claimed the throne against the liberal succession. The Carlists fought for tradition: the fueros (regional liberties, especially Basque and Navarrese), the Catholic faith as the foundation of the state, and a monarchy bound by law and tradition, not by liberal constitution. They fought again in the Second Carlist War (1846–1849) and the Third (1872–1876). Each defeat deepened the tradition. Each war produced martyrs, songs, and a red beret passed from father to son.

By 1936, Carlism is concentrated in Navarre. The Carlist claimant is Alfonso Carlos de Borbón (great-grandson of the original Don Carlos). The political organization is the Comunión Tradicionalista (Carlist Communion). The military arm is the Requeté — volunteer infantry, organized in tercios (battalions), wearing the red beret that is the movement's most visible symbol. The Carlist vision: a theocratic monarchy under Carlist law, with the fueros preserved, the Church restored to its rightful place, and liberal democracy abolished. It is specific. It is beautiful to those who hold it. And in 1936, it is about to be used and discarded by a man who wants power, not a kingdom.

**Faction dynamics: The Nationalist coalition.** The Nationalist side is not monolithic. It is an uneasy coalition of factions that Franco holds together by giving each something while concentrating all power in himself:

- **The Military (Franco, Mola, the Army of Africa).** The backbone. Professional officers, colonial troops (Regulares — Moroccan soldiers, the Spanish Foreign Legion). They want order, discipline, and a strong Spain. Franco wants to be the man on top. He is cautious, methodical, politically cunning. He plays the factions against each other. He is not a character in this novel — he is a force, like gravity.

- **The Falange.** Founded by José Antonio Primo de Rivera (1933). Fascist ideology — national syndicalism, anti-communist, anti-liberal, revolutionary. José Antonio was executed by Republicans in November 1936, becoming the Nationalists' most potent martyr. Franco absorbs the Falange into a unified party (February 1937), neutering its radicalism while appropriating its symbolism. The Falange wants a new Spain — neither monarchist nor liberal, but Falangist. Franco gives them a party structure and takes their soul.

- **The Carlists (Requeté).** Ultra-Catholic, monarchist (for a rival Bourbon branch), regionalist (for the fueros). They provide some of the Nationalists' best volunteer infantry. Their blood is on every front. Their political aim: the Carlist king on the throne, the fueros restored, the Church supreme. Franco gives them Catholic rhetoric and takes everything else. The fueros are abolished. The king is not crowned. The red beret becomes a museum piece.

- **The Monarchists (Alfonsine).** Supporters of Alfonso XIII (exiled). Conservative, constitutional monarchists. Many in the officer corps. They want a monarchy, but an Alfonsine one — not a Carlist one. Franco plays them against the Carlists.

- **The Church.** The Spanish Catholic hierarchy overwhelmingly supports the Nationalists. Cardinal Gomá issues a pastoral letter (July 1937, "Collective Letter of the Spanish Bishops") justifying the rebellion as a crusade against godless communism. The Church gets restored privileges, returned properties, and moral authority. The cost: the Church becomes the chaplain of a dictator. Notable exception: Bishop Múgica of Vitoria, who supports the Basque cause and refuses to sign the Collective Letter. He is forced into exile.

Franco's genius is managing these factions. He gives the Falangists a unified party (which dilutes them). He gives the Carlists Catholic rhetoric (which costs him nothing). He gives the monarchists authoritarian order (which is not a monarchy). He gives the Church its privileges (in exchange for its silence). And he keeps all of them dependent on him. No rival can consolidate. The Caudillo is the only constant.

**The Republican side.** The Republic is the enemy across the line. It is not a villain. It is a legitimate government defending itself against a military uprising. The Republic has its own moral architecture: the defense of democracy, the protection of workers' rights, the resistance to fascism. It also has its own internal contradictions: Soviet influence, the suppression of the POUM, the May Days in Barcelona, the anarchist-communist tensions. The Republic's atrocities — the burning of churches, the execution of clergy, the Paracuellos massacres — are real and must be acknowledged without being sensationalized. The Carlist soldiers across the line see the Republic as godless. The Republic sees the Carlists as reactionaries. Both are telling the truth about the other. Neither is telling the whole truth about itself.

**The Basque Country as moral crucible.** The Basque Country is the novel's moral center. Navarre — Basque in culture, Catholic, traditional — sides with the Nationalists. Biscay and Gipuzkoa — more industrial, more urban, also Catholic — side with the Republic, fighting for Basque autonomy under the PNV (Partido Nacionalista Vasco). When the Nationalists launch the northern campaign in 1937, Carlist requeté fight Basque Nationalists. Catholic men killing Catholic men for the same fueros from opposite sides. The Basque Nationalists are not godless — they are Catholic, conservative, pro-autonomy. Their bishop (Múgica) blesses their flag. Their soldiers pray before battle. And the Carlists kill them. This is the moral catastrophe at the heart of the novel.

**Institutional context.**

- **The Carlist Communion (Comunión Tradicionalista).** The political organization. Run by a junta (council) of elders. Don Eusebio belongs to this world. The Communion negotiates with the military conspirators (Mola) before the uprising, extracting promises about the fueros and the Carlist king. The promises are not kept.

- **The Requeté.** The military arm. Volunteer infantry, organized in tercios (battalions). Each tercio has a chaplain (capellán). The Requeté is not a professional army — it is a militia of believers. Their training is minimal. Their faith is not.

- **The Church.** Parish priests, bishops, the Vatican. The Church hierarchy supports Franco. Individual priests are divided — some are Carlist, some are Basque Nationalist, some are simply trying to survive. Padre Joaquín is a Carlist priest who blesses bullets and hears confessions before battle. The Church's endorsement of Franco is the novel's central theological problem.

- **The Spanish Army.** Professional officers, conscripts, the Army of Africa. The Army is the Nationalist coalition's ultimate guarantor. Franco is its commander. Teniente Vidal represents this world — professional, non-ideological, focused on winning.

**The world outside the main setting.** The broader war: Germany's Condor Legion bombs Guernica. Italy's CTV fights at Guadalajara and the Ebro. The International Brigades defend Madrid. The Soviet Union supplies the Republic. Britain and France maintain Non-Intervention. The war is a proxy conflict and a rehearsal for World War II. Martín does not see most of this. He sees the front line, the battalion, the letters from home. The larger war arrives in fragments — a rumor, a newspaper, a German officer in a staff car. The novel is deliberately narrow: one man's war, not the war's全景.

**Calendar and time.** The novel follows the war's chronology exactly. No flashbacks. No time jumps beyond the natural gaps between campaigns. Each act opens with a date and, where appropriate, a reference to a Carlist feast day or historical anniversary — connecting the present war to the three Carlist wars of the 19th century. The structure enforces inevitability: the reader moves forward with Martín toward a victory that is also a defeat.

---

*This is historical fiction. The world is real. The facts are documented. The characters are invented, but the events they move through are not. The novel's power comes from the reader's knowledge that this happened — that the Carlists fought, that Franco absorbed them, that the fueros were abolished, that the Church blessed the dictator. The fiction is the interior life. The history is the architecture.*
