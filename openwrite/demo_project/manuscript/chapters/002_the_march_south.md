## The March South

The train carried us south from Pamplona on the nineteenth of July. I know the date because Tomás wrote it on the inside of his pack with a pencil stub, the date and his mother's name and the words "Aoiz, Navarra," like a headstone he was getting the inscription ready for. He did it as another man might check his ammunition. I watched him form the letters: careful, slow, the pencil gripped tight in fingers more used to a crook than a pen.

I am at the table now. The inkwell is full. Outside, on the Calle Mayor, a truck is passing. I can hear the engine, the diesel knock that has replaced the horse-carts of my father's time. The vibration puts me on the train. The floorboards of the hardware store carry the truck's rumble the same way the railcar's planks carried the clatter of the wheels on the track. The body does not distinguish between one vibration and another. It only says: movement. It only says: going.

The railcar was third class. Wooden benches, bare frames with canvas flaps for windows that the wind pressed inward and then released, inward and released, like breathing. Sixty men sat and stood and crouched in a car meant for forty. The rifles were between the knees or across the shoulders or leaning against the walls. The air was sweat and wool and the oil from the rifle stocks and the dust that came through the windows in sheets when the wind turned. The train moved slowly. It stopped at towns I had never heard of: Tafalla, Olite, Tudela. At each stop more men climbed in and the car compressed and the air thickened.

Tomás slept. He slept sitting upright with his chin on his chest and his pack in his lap and his mouth open and his breathing steady. He ate the same way, without ceremony or transition, sleep and eating both tasks he had been assigned and intended to complete. I stayed awake beside him and watched the window and the window showed me the world I had never seen.

The green went first. Navarre is green, the valleys and the hillsides and the fields of wheat that turn gold in July but stay green at the edges where the irrigation runs. I had known this without knowing I knew it. Green was the color of home the way stone was the texture and the bells were the sound. A thing I had stood inside, looked past.

South of Tudela the green ended. The hills flattened. The fields turned brown. The trees thinned and then stopped and the horizon widened until it was a line, a single line, brown on one side and pale blue on the other, only air between. The sky was enormous. In the valleys of Navarre the sky is a ceiling: the mountains hold it, the trees break it, the church towers puncture it. On the meseta the sky is a floor. It presses down. It fills the space that the hills and the trees and the rivers have vacated. The eye reaches for something to hold and finds nothing. Only the brown. Only the flat. Only the line where the land ends and the sky begins, and the line never gets closer.

My father had spoken of the meseta: Castile, the central plateau, the tableland. He spoke of it as a place the Army went, a place where people passed through. But people lived here. I saw them at the stations: men and women in clothes the color of the dust, standing on platforms in towns that seemed to be made of the same material as the ground, adobe and clay, the brown baked hard by the sun. They watched the train pass. Some waved. Most stared. Their faces were dark and still. The sun had done to their faces what it had done to the land: flattened it, burned the green out, left the brown.

I am writing this and I can feel the heat stored somewhere in the body's accounting, filed under the skin. The railcar was an oven. The men around me were wet through. Their shirts clung to their backs. Their faces shone. Tomás slept through it all, his breathing unchanged, his pack dark where his arms wrapped around it. The land held. Brown. Flat. Brown. The train moved through it like a needle through cloth, stitching a line from the green to the brown, and the cloth went on forever.

Padre Joaquín was three cars ahead. I missed him on the train. I heard about it later. He had said Mass at dawn in the Pamplona station before we boarded, using a wooden crate as an altar, his blanket over his shoulders despite the heat, his voice carrying across the platform to the men who were already forming lines. He blessed the train. He blessed the tracks. He blessed the direction south, as though south itself could receive a blessing. I was in the car when he said it. I missed the words. I felt the movement of the train beginning and the pull of the south and the wool of the beret on my head and I looked out the window at the green turning brown and I thought: this is what the crusade looks like. This is the land we are saving.

I want to be precise. He thought the brown land was something to be saved. He thought the flatness was an absence that needed filling. He was wrong. The meseta was full of its own heat, its own distance, its own people who had been living on this tableland for centuries, people who had managed fine without boys from the green valleys who had spent their lives under mountains.

---

We reached the village on the evening of July twenty-third. The day after the Magdalene. I remember standing in the square and seeing a banner strung between two houses: VIVA CRISTO REY. The banner was painted on a bedsheet. The letters were red. The paint had run in places where the sheet had been rained on and the red bled into the white and the whole thing hung slack in the still air.

The village had a name. I have forgotten it. I have tried to remember, sitting at this table with my eyes closed, trying to bring back the name on the sign at the station, the name the sergeant called out when he read the billeting orders. It is gone. The name is gone but the square is there: the church on the north side, the fountain in the center, the houses with their shutters closed against the heat. The fountain was dry. The stone was warm under my fingers when I touched it. The church door was open and the inside was dark and cool and I could smell the wax of candles and the old stone and the faint sweetness of incense that had not been fresh for days.

Tomás dropped his pack in the square. He sat on it. He took off his beret and wiped his forehead with his sleeve and put the beret back on and looked around.

"It's flat," he said.

I nodded.

"Where are the hills?"

"There are no hills."

He considered this. He looked at the horizon, the line, the brown, the pale blue, and his face moved through something I could not read. His eyes narrowed. His mouth pressed flat. The face of a man who has arrived at a place and is measuring it against the place he left.

"I don't like it," he said. He said it as a fact, not a complaint.

We were billeted in a farmhouse on the edge of the village. The house was stone and plaster, the roof tile, the courtyard swept. The owner was a woman in her sixties, wide, her face a map of lines, her hands rough and quick. She gave us bread and olives and a jug of water and showed us the room where we would sleep, a long room with a dirt floor and straw mattresses and a window that looked out on the courtyard and the fields beyond. The fields were brown. The sky was enormous.

"You are from Navarre," the woman said.

"Yes."

"My cousin went to Pamplona once. He said it rains."

"It rains."

"We could use some rain." She looked at the fields. "Two years now."

She brought us a stew: chickpeas, some meat, peppers. It was good. Different from the cooking at home, heavier, spicier, the oil richer. Tomás ate three bowls, his spoon moving from bowl to mouth in a steady rhythm. I ate one bowl and drank the water and the water was warm and tasted of clay.

That evening we sat in the courtyard. The heat came off the stones in waves. The sky turned orange and then purple and then dark. The stars came out, more stars than I had seen in Pamplona, where the buildings and the church towers blocked the edges of the sky. Here the sky went all the way down. The stars were everywhere. The Milky Way was a band of light across the center, thick and pale, and I looked at it and the sky kept going and my chest opened and my eyes had nothing to hold. The buildings and the church towers and the river were five hundred kilometers behind me and above me there was nothing. Only the stars pressing through.

Tomás sat beside me. He was writing a letter, or rather, he was holding a pencil and a scrap of paper and frowning at both.

"Write to my mother," he said. "Tell her I'm well."

I took the paper. The pencil was blunt. I sharpened it with my knife and Tomás watched me do this with the patient attention of a man who has never sharpened a pencil with a knife and finds the skill interesting but not transferable.

"Tell her the food is not so bad," he said. "Tell her the woman here cooks well. Tell her I am with good men."

I wrote what he said. I wrote in my own hand, the handwriting my father taught me, small, precise, slanted to the right. The words looked strange in that courtyard, under that sky, on that scrap of paper. They looked like words from another world.

"Tell her I will come home."

I wrote it. The pencil moved. The words were on the paper. *I will come home.* Tomás said it as a fact. He said it as he said I'll be beside you, as he said It's heavy at first, you get used to it. A statement about the world. A truth that did not require evidence because it was self-evident: he was here, he would fight, he would go home. The logic of the shepherd's boy from Aoiz who had never been the center of his own story and did not expect to be and who measured the war in terms of departure and return, not crusade or cause or king.

I folded the letter. I gave it to him. He put it in his pack, next to the inscription on the inside flap.

"You write to yours?" he asked.

"My father doesn't need me to write. He knows."

Tomás nodded. He accepted this as he accepted most things about me, with a shrug that said: you know your business. He lay back on the straw and put his beret over his face and within minutes his breathing was steady and I was alone in the courtyard with the stars and the heat and the knowledge that I was five hundred kilometers from Pamplona and the green was gone and the hills were gone and the river was gone and the only thing that remained was the beret on my head and the crucifix in my pocket and the stocky man sleeping on the straw who had said I will come home and meant it the way a man means the sun will rise.

---

The feast of Santiago fell on the twenty-fifth. July twenty-five. The patron saint of Spain. In Pamplona the day is a thing, the cathedral fills, the streets are hung with banners, the children carry small flags, the old women wear their best mantillas. The requeté mark it as a military feast: Mass in the morning, the blessing of the colors, a sermon about the sword of the apostle. I had heard the sermon every year of my life. Santiago Matamoros. Saint James the Moor-slayer. The patron who rides into battle with his sword raised and his white horse leaping and the infidel beneath him. The story was carved into the altarpiece of San Lorenzo. I had looked at it since I was a boy, the horse's eyes wild, the saint's arm straight, the bodies under the hooves.

In the village there was no cathedral. There was the church, small, stone, cool inside, the altar simple, the saints in niches along the walls. Padre Joaquín said Mass. He stood at the altar in his cassock with the blanket still over his shoulders. He wore it everywhere, like part of his ordination. He spoke the words of the Mass and his voice was the same voice that had blessed us in the Plaza del Castillo: warm, measured, the cadences of a man who has said these words ten thousand times and has not yet tired of them.

"After the Mass," he said, "we will bless the road."

He blessed the road. We stood in the square and he stood at the head of the column and he raised his hand and made the sign of the cross over the road that led south and he said, "Go with God. The road is His. The march is His. The cause is His." And the men bowed their heads and I bowed mine and the beret pressed against my forehead and the sun was on my neck and the road was white with dust and it went south into the brown and the flat and the heat.

After Mass I sat in the church. The others had gone. Tomás was outside, talking to the woman about her chickens. He had opinions about chickens, it turned out, and the woman was listening with the skeptical attention of a woman who has raised chickens for forty years and does not require instruction from a soldier. I sat in the pew and looked at the altar. The saints in their niches. The candle wax on the stone floor. The crucifix above the tabernacle, smaller than my grandfather's, the corpus less strained, the arms even. I reached into my pocket and touched the crucifix my father had given me. The wood was warm from my body. My thumb found the groove where my father's thumb had been. The groove was smooth. The wood had been held by three generations of men who believed the same thing, and I was the fourth, and the belief was in my hand and in my pocket and on my head and in the church and on the road and in the blessing and I held it.

But I am ahead of myself. That morning, in the church, on the feast of Santiago, the wall was intact.

---

Vidal arrived the next day.

His name was unknown to me then. I saw a man walking across the square with a map case over his shoulder and binoculars around his neck and the officer's cap on his head, the peaked thing with the insignia, the thing that said Army, not requeté. He was tall. Lean. Angular. His boots were polished. His uniform was clean, maintained, the creases sharp, the buttons bright. He moved as a man moves who has been walking in uniform for a decade: the stride even, the arms close to the body, the eyes always moving, not nervously but professionally, scanning a room the way a soldier does when he enters it and catalogs the exits and the windows and the faces.

He stopped in the center of the square. He looked at the church, the houses, the fountain, the men scattered in groups, some cleaning rifles, some sleeping in the shade, some playing cards on an ammunition crate. He looked at all of this and his face revealed nothing, as if it had been trained not to.

He found the battalion commander, Comandante Etxeberria, a Carlist elder, white-haired, stiff-backed, the kind of man who wore the beret as a crown. They spoke. I was too far to hear. I saw Etxeberria's face, the stiffness, the resistance, the slow nod. I saw Vidal's face, the patience, the professionalism, the bare minimum of expression. They shook hands. Vidal saluted. He turned and walked to the farmhouse that had been designated as the command post and he went inside and stayed for three hours.

Later I learned what had happened. Vidal had been sent by the Army, the regular Army, the professional force, to organize the requeté battalion into something that could function as a military unit. The requeté had courage. They had faith. They had the red beret and the crucifix and the blessing. What they lacked was fire discipline, logistics, communications, and any understanding of how to hold ground against a force that shot back. His job was to provide these things. Not to inspire. Not to bless. To make the battalion work.

I first spoke to him the following morning. We were on the road south, the battalion marching, the column strung out along the white road, the dust rising. I was near the middle, beside Tomás, my rifle on my shoulder, the sun on my neck. Vidal came down the column from the front. He walked with his map case and his binoculars and his polished boots and he stopped at each section and spoke to the men. When he reached us he stopped and looked at me and then at Tomás and then at the men behind us.

"Name," he said.

"Martín. Echeverría."

"You've fired a rifle."

It was not a question. I said, "Yes."

"Show me your sight picture."

I raised the rifle. I looked through the rear sight, found the front post, settled it on a rock beside the road. My cheek was against the stock. The stock smelled of oil.

"Low," Vidal said. "You're shooting at the base. Aim at the top third. The bullet drops."

He said this without inflection. Not criticism. Information. A man saying the road turns left, the water is warm. He looked at Tomás.

"You," he said.

"Tomás. Eguía."

"You've fired a rifle at something that moved."

Tomás grinned. "Rabbits."

Vidal's face held steady. But something in his eyes shifted. He looked at Tomás the way my father looked at a man who knew iron, a nod, brief, the kind that says: you know your work.

"We'll use you on the forward posts," he said. He moved on. His boots were loud on the road. The dust shed off them.

Tomás watched him go. "That one's an officer," he said. A classification. Like saying that one's a ewe or that one's a ram.

I watched Vidal move down the column. He stopped at each section. He asked names. He checked rifles. He looked at boots and canteens. He was building something, not in the way Padre Joaquín built, with words and blessings and the architecture of faith, but in the way my father built, with measurements and materials and the architecture of use. Counting. Sorting. Making the column into a thing that could function. He did it without raising his voice, without a speech, without a blessing. He did it by looking and measuring and speaking in short sentences that contained only what was necessary.

I had not seen this before. In the requeté the authority was moral, the elder's voice, the priest's blessing, the weight of tradition. In the Army the authority was professional. Vidal had no tradition. He had no blessing. He had a map case and binoculars and ten years of service and the knowledge of how to keep men alive in terrain that was trying to kill them. And he applied this knowledge with the flat efficiency of a man who has done it before and will do it again. He requires your obedience. The obedience is enough.

My father had a craft. The store, the iron, the wood, the counting. The precision of the hand, the economy of the movement, the satisfaction of a bolt set true. Vidal had the same thing. His material was men, not iron. His tools were orders, not wrenches. The precision was the same. The economy was the same. The refusal to waste.

The boy on the road that morning saw it. The man at this table knows what the craft served. The boy did not.

---

We reached another village. Or the same village. The meseta villages blurred. They were the same stone and the same dust and the same dry fountain and the same church and the same faces dark from the sun. I remember this one because of the conversation.

We were in the square. Evening. The heat was dropping. Tomás was talking to a group of men, local men, farmers, their hands thick with work, their Spanish flat and fast, the vowels clipped in a way I was still learning to hear. I was sitting on a stone bench near the fountain, writing to my father. The letter was short. It had to be short. My father did not read long letters. A long letter was a sign that the writer had not thought enough before picking up the pen.

*We are well. The men are ready. The road is long and flat. The country is different from home. I have met a professional officer, an Army man. He is competent. Tomás is beside me. The crucifix is in my pocket. The beret is on my head.*

I stopped. I read it. The words were the words my father would want to hear. They were his words, written in his style: short, factual, the Carlist content between the lines. *The crucifix is in my pocket.* That meant: I am still what you made me. *The beret is on my head.* That meant: the tradition continues. My father would read these lines and understand. He would fold the letter and put it in the drawer of the counter in the store and he would not write back, not yet, not until there was something to say, and the silence would be his answer: I know. I trust. Continue.

One of the farmers came over. He was fifty, maybe sixty. His face was the color of the earth. He sat on the bench beside me and looked at the letter.

"You write home," he said.

"Yes."

"Navarre?"

"Pamplona."

He nodded. He looked at my beret. "The red," he said. "We've seen the red. You're the Carlists."

"We're the requeté."

"Same thing." He smiled. It was a friendly smile. The smile of a man who is not hostile, only curious. "You fight for the king."

"For the king. For the fueros. For Christ the King."

The smile stayed. The eyes moved to my face, to the beret, to the crucifix I had pulled from my pocket without thinking and was holding. "The fueros," he said. He said the word carefully, without certainty. "What are the fueros?"

The question was simple. The man asked it without malice, as he might ask what kind of seed I was planting or what river my valley followed. A question of geography. A question about a place he had never been and a thing he had never heard of.

I looked at the man and his face was open and waiting and I could offer him only a word that belonged to a world he had never entered. The fueros, the ancient Basque-Navarrese laws that predated Spain, were the foundation of the Carlist cause, the reason we marched, the reason we fought, the reason the red beret existed. And this man, this farmer on the meseta, Nationalist and Catholic and conservative and on our side, had never heard the word.

"They are the rights of Navarre," I said. "The old laws. The laws the Republic took."

"Ah," he said. He nodded. "Like the land."

"No. Not like the land."

"But something taken."

"Yes. Something taken."

He nodded again. He had what he needed: a parallel, a hook, something to hang the word on. The fueros were like the land. Something taken. Something to be restored. The history, the theology, the centuries of Carlist wars, the blood my grandfather had spilled in the hills above Estella, all of that was excess. He needed a category. I gave him one. It was enough for him.

It was not enough for me.

The man went back to his friends and Tomás was laughing with the other farmers and the square was filling with the blue of evening and I held the crucifix in my pocket and the letter to my father was on my lap and I thought: the man has never heard the word fueros. He is on our side and the word is foreign to him. He fights for Franco and for Spain and for the Church and for the land and the word that my grandfather died for and my father lives for stays in my pocket next to the crucifix, known only to me.

Perhaps the cause is broad enough to hold men who do not know its name. Perhaps the crusade is larger than the fueros, larger than Navarre, larger than the tradition my father handed me with the crucifix and the beret. Perhaps the man on the bench is right and the fueros are like the land, something taken, to be restored, and the specifics do not matter because the fight is the same and the enemy is the same and the blessing is the same.

The boy on the bench thought: the coalition is broad. The cause is wide. The man beside me does not need to know the word to fight for the thing the word protects.

The breadth was the danger. A coalition without a shared name has no shared purpose, and a purpose unnamed is a purpose that can be redefined by whoever holds the power to name it. The boy on the bench did not know.

---

That night I found Tomás in the room where we slept. The straw mattresses were laid out in a row. The men were settling, the sound of boots being pulled off, the creak of straw, the mutter of prayers. The window was open and the air was cooling and the stars were coming through.

I sat on my mattress. Tomás was already lying down, his beret over his face. I said his name. He lifted the beret with one eye showing.

"The man in the square," I said. "He didn't know what the fueros were."

Tomás looked at me. The one eye was calm. "So?"

"So, he's on our side. He's fighting with us. And he doesn't know."

Tomás put the beret back. He adjusted it. He was quiet for a moment and I thought he had gone to sleep and then he said, from under the wool, "We fight. We win. We go home."

The words were muffled. The wool made them flat. They were a man's summary of a war he had already settled: there was a fight, the fight would be won, and after the win there was home. The fueros were my question. The going home was his. The man on the bench could stay ignorant of the word. Tomás could live without the word. He needed the road, the rifle, the bread, and the certainty that the road led back to Aoiz.

I lay down. The straw was dry and smelled of sun. The crucifix was under my pillow. The beret was on my head. I closed my eyes and the dark came and it was the dark of the meseta, the dark that goes all the way down, the dark of open country with only the flat and the brown and the stars pressing through the window like holes in a ceiling that someone had kicked from above.

I slept. I dreamed of nothing. The body was tired and the body slept and the mind followed. The road was under my feet even in sleep. I could feel it, the white dust, the heat, the line where the brown met the blue.

In the morning we marched. The column formed in the square. Padre Joaquín blessed the road again. The women of the village stood at their doors and watched. The man from the bench was there. He raised his hand. I raised mine. We were brothers in a cause he could not name and I could not explain and the blessing was on us both.

We marched south. The road was white. The dust rose. Under the white dust, the beret pressed against my forehead. Tomás was beside me, his stride steady. Vidal was ahead, his map case over his shoulder. Padre Joaquín was behind us, his blanket over his shoulders, his lips moving in prayer or in habit or in something that had not yet become doubt.

The meseta opened. The brown went on. The sky pressed down. He knew the road. He knew the beret. He knew the crucifix and the blessing and the stocky man beside him who had said we fight, we win, we go home and who believed it the way the boy believed the bells.

I am at the table. The ink is drying at the edges of the well. Outside the window the Calle Mayor is quiet, the shops are closed, the streetlights are on. The hardware store is dark around me. The shelves hold their bolts and hinges and screws. The smell is iron and oil and dust.

He does not know. The man at the table knows where the road leads and the boy does not, and the man cannot warn him because the warning is the writing and the distance between them is thirty years and a war and a table in a hardware store in Pamplona where the inkwell is full and the pen is steady and the boy is marching south.
