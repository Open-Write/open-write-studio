# Chapter 10 — The Cold

---

I am writing this in the back room of the hardware store. The inkwell is full. I filled it this morning before I sat down, the way my father taught me. The chair is hard. The wood of the table is cold under my forearms, and for a moment I am somewhere else. I am in a trench outside Teruel, and the cold is in my hands, and my hands are shaking, and I cannot make them stop.

The body remembers what the mind would rather file away. The cold of that table is a memory of the trench. I know this, here, at this table, with the funnel, the rag, the inkwell full. But the skin does not distinguish. The skin says: you are cold. And then I am there again.

---

December 1937. We marched north from the Ebro into the mountains around Teruel. The snow was already on the peaks. The roads were hard — frozen mud, ruts like iron, the wheels of the supply carts bouncing and the mules struggling for footing. The wind came down from the Sierra de Albarracín and cut through the greatcoats as if they were paper. We had been issued wool gloves. They were thin. They were not enough.

The temperature dropped each day we marched. First the breath became visible — white plumes that rose and vanished. Then the water in the canteens froze. Then the mud in the roads hardened and the ruts became ridges and the men stumbled on them in the dark, cursing, their boots finding no purchase. The stars were very clear. The sky was black, the stars hard and bright, giving no warmth. We marched at night because the Republic had air cover during the day. The planes were a nuisance. The cold was the real enemy — the only one that cared nothing for the color of your uniform.

We reached the line on the twenty-first of December. The Republic had launched its offensive three days before — a surprise push to take the city before Franco could respond. The Republicans held Teruel. We were sent to hold the approaches, to dig in on the ridges above the city and wait for reinforcements. Vidal walked the line the first night. He skipped God, king, fueros. He said: "Dig deep. The deeper the warmer. If you do not dig, you will die."

He was right. Men were dying. From the cold.

I can feel it now — the way the cold entered the body. An occupation. First the extremities: fingers, toes, nose, ears. Then the hands and feet, the numbness spreading inward like water soaking into cloth. Then the arms and legs, the muscles stiffening, the joints locking. Then the chest, the breath shortening, the lungs catching on air that was so cold it felt solid going down. Then the mind — the thoughts slowing, the will to move fading, the body's argument that lying still was easier than standing up, that sleeping was easier than staying awake. That argument was the killer. The men who slept in the open did not wake.

Tomás dug. He dug when the rest of us were too cold to hold a shovel. He dug the trench deeper, wider, cutting into the frozen earth with a pickaxe, the blade ringing on the ground as if he were striking stone. His hands were bare. He had taken off the gloves because the wet wool made the shovel handle slick. The skin of his palms was cracked and bleeding and he did not stop. He dug until the trench was deep enough to stand in, deep enough to lie down in, deep enough that when the wind came over the lip it passed above us instead of through us.

"Get in," he said.

I was standing at the lip of the trench, watching the snow fall. The snow was fine. Hard. Dry. Driven sideways by the wind. It stung the face. It collected in the folds of the greatcoat, in the creases of the collar, in the space between the glove and the sleeve. My fingers were inside my gloves and every sensation was gone.

"Get in," Tomás said again. He pulled me down by the sleeve. I fell into the trench. The bottom was frozen mud. Tomás pulled one blanket from his own shoulders and put it over me. He pulled a second — I never learned where he got it — and wrapped it around my legs. He kept a third around himself. He sat beside me. His body was warm. Less cold, at least. He pressed his shoulder against mine.

"Stay awake," he said. "If you sleep, you don't get up."

I stayed awake. The night was long. The wind moved over the lip of the trench and the snow collected on the edge and sometimes a drift fell in and dusted our legs. Tomás talked. He talked about Aoiz. He talked about his mother's kitchen, the fire in the winter, the way the bread came out of the oven with the crust dark and the inside soft and the steam rising. He talked about the sheep on the hillside above the town, the sound of the bells in the morning, the dog that followed his father — a border collie with one blue eye and one brown, named Txuri. He talked about swimming in the river when the snowmelt came down from the mountains and the water was so cold it took the breath and the boys would dare each other to stay in until the lips turned blue.

He talked until his voice was hoarse. He talked because talking kept us both awake. He talked because the sound of a voice was a wall against the cold, and the wall was thin, but it was there.

---

The first man froze on the third night.

His name was Ibarra. From Pamplona, a year older than me, a carpenter's son. His mates said he went to sleep without a word. Sat down, pulled his blanket around his shoulders, closed his eyes. In the morning they touched him and he was stiff. His face was white. His lips were blue. His eyes were open. Frost on his eyelashes, on his eyebrows, on the stubble of his jaw. He looked like a statue. Someone tried to close his eyes. The lids were frozen. They covered his face with the blanket.

Padre Joaquín came. He was moving slowly. His limp was worse — the cold had stiffened the old injury, the childhood leg, and each step was deliberate, placed, the body calculating the distance between one foot and the next. His cassock was under the military blanket. His breath was visible — white, rising, vanishing. His face was thin. The skin tight on the bones. The cheeks hollow. His eyes red-rimmed from the wind.

He knelt beside Ibarra. He put his hand on the blanket that covered the face. He said the words. The same words from the barns, the fields, the plazas, the ditches. The same from the first day at the cuartel in Pamplona, at the oak stump, in the rain. His breath rose with each sentence. White plumes. The words were in the air and then they were gone.

He stood. He moved to the next trench, and the next, his breath rising in white plumes, his cassock visible under the military blanket. Another man had died in the night — a conscript from Vidal's regulars, a man from Burgos whose name escaped me. Padre Joaquín knelt again. Said the same words. Stood. Moved on.

He did not sit down. I watched him all morning. He moved from trench to trench, from body to body, his breath rising, his limp heavy but his pace steady. The rote of it. The machinery of blessing. The same words for every body, the same breath, the same hand on the blanket.

I think he was afraid that if he sat down, he would not get up. I think the standing was the only thing keeping him vertical. The cold had gone into his bones and his bones were holding him up through habit, through the muscle memory of fifty years of standing — at the altar, in the confessional, at the bedside of the dying, in the trenches. If he sat, the habit would break. The cold would win. He would freeze in the position of rest, the way Ibarra had frozen, the others too, their bodies finding the position of least resistance and staying there.

He did not sit.

---

Vidal called a meeting on the fourth day. The officers, the NCOs, the requeté section leaders gathered in a communications trench where corrugated iron kept the worst of the wind off. Vidal's face was lean and his eyes were moving. His map was spread on a board.

"The Republicans hold the city," he said. "We hold the ridges. Franco is sending reinforcements. They will arrive in ten days."

"Ten days," someone said.

"Ten days," Vidal said. "We hold."

A section leader named Etxeberria — a big man from the valleys, his red beret crusted with frost, the Carlist pin at the front a dull bump under the ice — said: "With what? The rifles jam. The bolts freeze. I have men who cannot grip a trigger."

"I know," Vidal said. "Keep the bolts inside the greatcoats. Body heat. Lubricate with any fat you have — tallow, lard, cooking oil. If you have nothing, use your own grease. Clean the weapons every hour. If a bolt seizes, warm it with your hands. Do not use fire — the metal contracts and the bolt cracks."

Etxeberria nodded. His jaw was tight.

Vidal looked at the group. His voice dropped. Lower. The register I had heard once before, on the road to Madrid, when the column was strung out and the planes came.

"Men will die," he said. "Not from the enemy. From the cold. Keep them moving. Keep them digging. Keep them awake. If a man stops moving, get him moving. If he will not move, carry him. If you cannot carry him, put him in the deepest part of the trench and cover him and send for me."

He folded the map. The meeting was over.

---

The fighting came on Christmas Eve.

The Republicans attacked at dawn — a push from the city toward our ridge. They came through the snow, dark shapes in white, their breath visible, their rifles at their hips. They were as cold as we were. Feet crunching on the frozen crust. Faces wrapped in cloth — scarves, rags, torn blankets. Only the eyes were visible. Red. Wide.

We fired. The rifles cracked. The sound was sharp in the cold — sharper than in warm air, the report cutting through the silence like a blade. The bolts were stiff. I worked mine with both hands, the metal biting my bare fingers, the mechanism grinding. Each round was an effort. Each chambering a negotiation between the hand and the frozen steel. Tomás had wrapped his bolt in a strip of cloth from his blanket. It helped. The cloth absorbed the moisture, kept the frost off the metal. He fired steadily, methodically, the stock against his shoulder, his cheek on the comb, his eyes steady.

The Republicans reached the wire. The wire was frozen. It snapped when they hit it — brittle, the strands breaking like thread. They came through. Close now. Close enough to see the faces under the wrappings. A boy — seventeen, maybe. His mouth was open. His teeth were chattering. He had a rifle and he was trying to work the bolt and the bolt was frozen and his fingers would not close.

I shot him. He fell. He lay in the snow and the snow was white around him and then it was red and then it was white again as the falling snow covered the red.

Tomás pulled me down. "Stay low." A burst of fire over the trench lip. Bullets throwing up sprays of frozen earth. The sound of metal on metal — a bullet hitting the corrugated iron behind us, the ring of it, the echo bouncing off the trench walls.

We held. The attack lasted an hour. The Republicans pulled back, leaving their dead in the snow. Dark shapes on the white ground. The snow covered them slowly — first the boots, then the legs, then the torsos, then the arms, then the faces. By noon the field was white again and the dead were under it, the only sign they had been there the dark patches where the blood had melted the snow and frozen again into red ice.

---

Padre Joaquín came to the line that afternoon.

He moved along the trench, stepping over the frozen dead, his breath rising. He had his leather pouch — the communion wafers, the wine, the prayer book. He stopped at each body. He knelt. He said the words. He blessed requeté and conscript alike. The Republican dead were out in the field under the snow. No one blessed them.

He reached our section. He knelt beside a man from Etxeberria's group — a young man, twenty perhaps, his face white, his hands still gripping his rifle. Padre Joaquín put his hand on the man's forehead. The skin was rigid. The priest's fingers were shaking — from the cold, from age, from something else.

"God receives you," he said. His voice was flat. A voice stripped of warmth, of pastoral care. A voice of a man who had said the same words so many times they had worn smooth, like a stone in a river, the edges gone. He made the sign of the cross. He stood. His knee cracked. He moved to the next body.

Tomás watched him. Tomás, who skipped Mass, who carried his mother's scapular but rarely prayed, who fought because his brothers fought and his father expected it — Tomás watched the priest move from body to body and his face was still. Not hard. Not soft. Still.

"Is it the same prayer?" Tomás asked. His voice was quiet. "For all of them?"

Padre Joaquín kept moving. Maybe he heard. Maybe he chose silence. He was at the next body. Kneeling. The words. The breath. The rise and the fall.

---

January came. The cold deepened. The temperature dropped to twenty below — the number that Vidal wrote on his map, the number the men passed from trench to trench like a curse. Twenty below. The breath froze on the scarves and the scarves became masks of ice. The water in the canteens was solid by noon. We melted snow for drinking water, holding the canteen inside the greatcoat against the chest, the metal cold, the skin burning where it touched.

Men froze at their posts. A man would be standing, watching, his rifle in his hands, and the next man would look over and the man would be still. Still in a way that was different from the stillness of watching. The stillness of a body that had stopped. They would touch him. Cold. Rigid. The eyes open, the frost on the lashes. They would carry him to the rear and lay him beside the others and cover his face and Padre Joaquín would come.

Tomás kept me alive. I will say it plainly because there is no other way to say it. He kept me alive. He shared his blanket. He dug the trench deeper when my hands were too frozen to grip a shovel. He woke me when I fell asleep — a hand on my shoulder, firm, his voice in my ear: "Stay awake, cura." He pressed bread into my hands when I struggled to eat, holding my fingers around it, waiting until I bit into it before he would eat his own portion. He wrapped my feet in strips of cloth torn from his blanket when my boots cracked and the cold came in through the splits.

I never understood why he did this. I never asked. The cold permits only the next action — the next breath, the next step, the next movement of the hand that keeps the blood flowing and the body upright. Tomás performed those actions for both of us. His body was beside mine in the trench, his shoulder against mine, his blanket over both of us, his warmth the only warmth I knew.

Once, in the deepest part of the night, when the wind was screaming over the trench and the snow was driven so hard it was horizontal, I felt Tomás press his face against the back of my neck. His nose was cold. His lips were cracked. His breath was warm for a moment — a small, desperate warmth, the body's last argument against the cold — and then it cooled and became the cold again.

He was keeping us both alive with the heat of his body. Two men, pressed together under two blankets, in a trench in the frozen earth, in the worst cold in living memory. If either of us moved apart, we would die.

---

The house-to-house fighting began in late January.

Franco's reinforcements had arrived. The order came to retake the city. We moved down from the ridges into Teruel itself. Into the streets. The houses. The city was frozen. The walls were ice. The windows were shattered — from the bombardment, from the cold, from two months of fighting that had passed through the streets like a disease. Glass crunched underfoot. Each step a small sharp breaking, the crystals grinding under the boot soles.

We fought room to room. A door. A hallway. A staircase. The grenades went in first — the blast echoing in the confined spaces, the plaster dust rising, the smoke filling the rooms. Then the men went in. Short bursts. The rifles enormous indoors — the report bouncing off the walls, the ceiling, the floor, the muzzle flash bright in the dark rooms. The Republicans fought from the upper floors, from the windows, from the rooftops. They fired down on us and we fired up and the bullets punched through the frozen walls and the plaster fell in chunks and the dust was white in the air, like snow.

I was in a room on the second floor of a building that had been a school. The desks were overturned. The blackboard was on the wall — chalk marks still visible, the numbers 1 through 10 in a child's hand. The window was gone. The wind came in. The floor was covered with glass and plaster and the dark stains of blood that had frozen into the wood. Tomás was beside me. His rifle was at the window. He fired. He worked the bolt. He fired again.

A Republican appeared in the doorway across the street — a man in a dark coat, his face wrapped, a rifle in his hands. Tomás fired. The man fell. Another appeared. Tomás fired. The man fell. The doorway was dark. The bodies were in it. No one else came.

We held the school for two days. We slept in the classrooms, on the floors, under the desks. The cold was marginally less inside — the walls blocked the wind, and the bodies of eight men in a small room raised the temperature enough that the water in the canteens stayed liquid until morning. Tomás slept with his back against the wall and his rifle across his knees and his blanket over both of us. His arm was around my shoulders. I stayed. The cold had burned away whatever pride or discomfort might have existed between two men sharing warmth. Only the body's need remained, and the body accepts no argument.

Padre Joaquín came to the school on the second day. He climbed the stairs slowly — one hand on the wall, the other on the railing, his bad leg stiff, his breath coming in short white bursts. He stood in the doorway of the classroom. His face was gray. His lips were cracked. His eyes were red. He looked at the eight men on the floor, the overturned desks, the blackboard with its arithmetic, the bloodstains on the frozen wood.

He said Mass off a desk. He put the prayer book on the wood and the communion wafers beside it and the small bottle of wine — the wine was frozen. He held the bottle in his hands for a long time, warming it. The wax of the candle — we had one candle — dripped on the desk. The flame was small. It threw shadows on the walls. The men knelt. Tomás knelt. I knelt. Padre Joaquín's hands shook as he raised the wafer. His voice was hoarse. The Latin was rough. He gave each man communion. His fingers were cold when they placed the wafer on my tongue. The wafer was cold. The wine, when he poured it, was cold.

He folded the prayer book. He put the wine and the wafers back in the leather pouch. He looked at us — eight men in a frozen classroom, sitting on the floor, wrapped in blankets, their rifles beside them, their faces gaunt, their eyes hollow. He skipped the sermon. He said nothing of God's plan or the crusade or the justice of the cause.

He left. I heard his steps on the stairs. Slow. Deliberate. One at a time.

---

February came. The city was retaken. The Republicans pulled back. The flag went up over Teruel — the red and yellow, the yoke and the arrows — and the men cheered, or tried to, the sound thin in the frozen air, the voices hoarse, the breath rising. I stood in the street and looked at the flag. My hands hung at my sides. My fingers would not close. The skin on the knuckles had split and healed and split again and the scars were white against the red. I looked at the flag and the flag moved in the wind and the wind was cold and my body did not respond.

Tomás stood beside me. His red beret was white with frost, the Carlist pin at the front catching a dull glint of sun through the ice. His face was thinner than it had been in December — the cheeks hollow, the jaw sharp, the skin stretched tight. His hands were in his pockets. He looked at the flag. He looked at me.

"Let's go home," he said.

We did not go home. We marched south. The cold followed us out of the mountains and into the plains and then, slowly, it released its grip. The air warmed. The ice melted. The canteens held liquid water. The bolts moved freely. The body unclenched — the muscles softening, the joints loosening, the skin losing the tight, burning feeling that had been constant for two months. I took off my gloves and looked at my hands. The skin was rough, cracked, the knuckles swollen, the nails split. The fingers were thinner. The veins were visible. They were the hands of an old man. I was twenty-three.

Tomás's hands were worse. He had torn his blanket into strips for my feet and for wrapping the rifle bolts and for covering the frozen dead. His blanket was half its original size. He wrapped it around his shoulders like a shawl and it barely reached his waist and the cold went into his legs and his back and he said nothing. His fingers were cracked to the bone in places — the skin split, the flesh visible, the blood dried to dark crusts. He wrapped them in strips of cloth and kept going.

He was thinner. We were all thinner. But Tomás had given away more than the rest of us — more food, more warmth, more of the small resources that kept a body alive in the cold. He had given them to me. He had kept me alive with his body, his blankets, his bread, his voice in the dark, talking about Aoiz, about the sheep, about the dog with two different eyes, about the river in spring.

I looked at him as we marched. His face was set. His eyes were on the road. His steps were steady. He looked straight ahead. The thing between us — whatever it was, whatever name it had — was a thing that required no looking at. It was in the blanket, the bread, the shoulder pressed against shoulder in the dark. It was in the body's knowledge that this man had kept me alive, and that I had let him, and that the letting was its own kind of debt.

I did not know what I would do with that debt. That in four months, on the banks of the Ebro, I would hold Tomás in my arms and his blood would be on my hands and his last word would be an unfinished sentence to a mother in Aoiz. That the debt would come due and I would fail to pay it and it would follow me for thirty years to this table, to this inkwell, to this pen moving across the page.

The road south was long. The mountains behind us. The plains ahead. The sun on our faces, weak but present, the first real warmth since December. Tomás walked beside me. His blanket on his shoulders. His beret white with old frost, the pin catching a dull glint of sun. His hands in his pockets.

The skin of his neck was visible above the collar. The skin was rough, red, cracked from the cold. The tendons stood out. The pulse was visible — a small movement, steady, the body's proof that it was still running, still moving, still alive.

I looked at that pulse. I looked at the skin of his neck. I looked at the road ahead. The mountains behind. The sun on the frozen ground. The steam rising from the earth as the ice melted and the world softened and the cold released its grip.

Tomás watched the road. His breath was steady. His steps were steady. The pulse in his neck was steady.

I walked beside him. The red beret on my head. The crucifix in my pocket. The cold in my bones. The road ahead.
