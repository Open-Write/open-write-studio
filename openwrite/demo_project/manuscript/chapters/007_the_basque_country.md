## The Basque Country

I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The pen is in my hand. Outside, the hardware store is quiet. A cart passes on the Calle Mayor. Wheels loud on the stone. Then quiet again.

I want to write about green. The body gives me the eyes first, the eyes full of green after months of brown, the eyes full of mountains after months of flat, the eyes full of trees after months of scrub and dust and clay. Green was the first thing. Before the town, before the church, before the silence — green. Green on the slopes and green in the valleys and green along the rivers where the water ran clear over stones. A green I knew. A green I had grown up in.

---

Bilbao fell on June 19. Word came down the line in the evening: a runner with a paper, Vidal reading it aloud, his voice clipped, his face flat. Bilbao. The city the Republic held in the north. Iron, coal, the factories. Gone. Men cheered. Tomás slapped the shoulder of the man beside him. Padre Joaquín made the sign of the cross.

I cheered because the men cheered. I had seen the maps. Bilbao was the anchor. With it gone, the northern campaign would move east through Gipuzkoa and into the remaining Basque territory. Strategy was clear. The catechism approved.

Two days later, we loaded onto trucks. North and east, through roads I did not know, roads that climbed out of the brown flatlands and into hills. The hills grew taller, and the road wound between them, and the scrub gave way to grass and the grass gave way to trees and the trees were green. Oak and beech and chestnut. Dense along the hillsides, thinning at the ridgelines, thick again in the gullies where streams ran.

Tomás sat beside me in the truck bed. His beret was pushed back. His eyes were on the road, or rather on the land beyond it — green slopes stretching to the tree line, rivers flashing in the valleys below. His face had a look I had seen before — the eyes soft, the mouth loose, the way a man looks when he hears a song from childhood.

"This looks like home," he said.

It did. The mountains were higher, steeper, the valleys narrower. But the green was the same green. Rivers ran as Navarrese rivers ran: fast, cold, over gray stones. I could name the trees from the truck bed: oak, beech, the tall chestnuts with their spring leaves. I knew the shape of those hills. I knew how the light fell on the western slopes in the afternoon, gold against green, and how the shadows gathered in the valleys early.

My eyes moved over the land as a man's eyes move over a room he grew up in. I could name the grass. I could read the ridgeline. I knew where the sheep would graze and where the wolves would come from and where the shepherds would build their shelters. This was Navarre land. Basque land. The same stone under the same sky.

Below us, in a valley, a river caught the sun. It ran silver between green banks, the water low from the dry weeks, the stones showing white and flat. A bridge crossed it. Stone, arched, old, moss on the piers where the water touched. A bridge like the bridges in the Valdizarbe, like the bridge at Puente la Reina where the Camino converges. I looked at it and my chest pulled tight, the breath shallow, the way it pulls before a cry that does not come. A stone bridge over a cold river in a green valley. Home.

We passed through a village. Stone houses, a church tower, a plaza with a fountain. Women at the fountain. Children stopped playing and watched the trucks. A man in a doorway, arms crossed.

"They look like ours," Tomás said.

He meant the houses. Stone, two stories, balconies with iron railings, wooden shutters. Identical construction to Pamplona, to Aoiz — the kind a man sees in every village from San Sebastián to Bayonne.

I nodded. My eyes held the village as it passed — three seconds, four — and then the road turned and the trees covered it and we were climbing again.

---

We entered the town in the late afternoon. The sun was low, westering behind the mountains, and the light came gold and horizontal through the streets. A Basque town. Its name was on the map but I will not write it here. It was a town. It had a church and a plaza and a school and streets of stone and a river running below.

The requeté dismounted in the plaza. Vidal gave orders. Billets were assigned. Men spread through the streets in groups of four and five, rifles slung, berets on, packs heavy.

I walked with Tomás toward the church. Padre Joaquín was behind us, his cassock muddy at the hem, his leather pouch over his shoulder. Our boots were loud on the stone. The streets were narrow, the buildings close, balconies above us where laundry hung, white sheets catching the late light. Flower boxes on some windows. Geraniums, red, the only color against the gray stone.

Nobody spoke.

I mean: the townspeople did not speak. They were there. I saw them. Faces at windows. A woman in a doorway, holding a child against her hip, her hand on the child's head, pressing it into her shoulder. An old man sitting on a bench in the plaza, his hands on his knees, his eyes on the red berets as they passed. A boy, maybe twelve, standing in a narrow lane, his back against the wall, his eyes wide, following us with his head as we walked. Two women at a well, their buckets full, standing still, watching us come and watching us go. A curtain moved in an upper window. Then stillness.

Nobody cheered. They stayed in their doorways and at their windows. They did not offer bread or wine. They did not shake our hands. They watched.

I had seen this before. In Castilian villages, when we arrived, the women came out. Bread. Wine. Sometimes flowers. The older women kissed the crucifix Padre Joaquín held. The men shook hands. Children ran alongside. That was how it went. That was what an army of liberation received.

Here, silence. The old man on the bench. His face was brown and lined, the face of a man who had worked outside all his life. Deep creases from the eyes to the jaw. His eyes were on me. Not on the unit. On me. As if he were reading something in my face, or in my beret, or in the yoke-and-arrows pin on my collar.

I looked away. I looked at the church.

---

The church was open. Heavy wooden doors, arched, iron handles. Inside, cool and dark, the smell of stone and candle wax and incense that had settled into the walls over centuries. My eyes adjusted. Light came from high windows, narrow, the glass plain, no color. Rows of wooden pews. A stone floor, worn smooth by generations of kneelers. An altar with cloth, white, clean. A tabernacle. A crucifix above the altar. The silence of a church that had not been empty for long — candle stubs on the altar, a prayer book open on the lectern, flowers wilted in a vase.

My eyes found it and stayed.

It was a corpus. Carved wood. Polychrome. The arms extended. The head tilted to the right, the crown of thorns painted gold and red. The face was composed in suffering, the brow furrowed, the eyes half-closed, the mouth slightly open. A wound in the side, painted red. Blood on the hands, on the feet, thin lines of red descending from the nails.

I knew this corpus. I had grown up under this corpus. It hung above the dining table in my father's house — a smaller version, carved by the same tradition, painted in the same style. It hung in every church in Navarre. In Pamplona, in Estella, in Puente la Reina, in the smallest village chapel in the Valdizarbe. The same face. The same crown. The same Christ.

My feet stopped. I stood in the nave and looked up at the crucifix and the crucifix looked back at me with the same eyes I had seen above my father's table for twenty-two years.

Padre Joaquín came to stand beside me. His eyes were on the crucifix. His hand rose, made the sign of the cross. His lips moved in a prayer I could not hear.

Tomás stood behind us. I heard him shift his weight. His pack creaked. He said nothing.

I walked to the altar rail and looked up. Close now. I could see the grain of the wood through the paint. I could see where the polychrome had cracked along the arm, where someone had repaired it, carefully, skillfully, the brush following the original line. I could see a maker's mark on the base, small, half-hidden by the cloth. A workshop in the Basque Country, maybe. Or Navarre.

I reached up and touched the altar rail. Wood, smooth, polished by hands. My fingers closed around it. I stood there and looked up and the corpus looked down and between us, a silence. A silence full of the prayers that had been said in this church, in this town, in this language, for generations. Prayers in Euskara. Prayers to the same God. Prayers for rain, for harvest, for the health of children, for the repose of the dead. Prayers that were those prayers said in Navarrese churches, in those words, to that corpus, with that faith.

---

Padre Joaquín said nothing in the church. We walked out. Sunlight was sharp after the dark. I squinted. The plaza opened before us — stone, a fountain, the old man still on the bench.

Vidal had billeted our section in a house near the school. Two stories, stone, a balcony facing the street. The owners were gone, fled, maybe, or moved to a relative's house deeper in the town. The house was clean. Beds made. Kitchen intact. A crucifix in each room. A calendar on the wall, saints' days marked in red ink. Basque calendar. Feast days I knew — San Fermín, Santiago, the Virgen del Pilar — alongside names I had never seen. Local saints. Basque saints.

Tomás set his pack down. He looked around the kitchen. He opened a cupboard.

"Beans," he said. "Dried. And salt cod."

He held up a tin of sardines.

"Better than Madrid."

He smiled. I did not.

---

I went to the school the next morning. Alone. Early. The streets were empty. Mist hung in the valley, thin, gray, the mountains rising above it, green and solid. My boots were loud on the stone. A dog watched me from a doorway. A rooster crowed from somewhere behind the houses.

A low building next to the church. One room. Stone walls, a wooden floor, desks arranged in rows. A blackboard on the far wall. A crucifix above the blackboard — smaller than the one in the church, painted in that style. The tilted head. The half-closed eyes.

I stood in the doorway. My eyes moved across the room.

The desks were overturned. Some were broken — legs snapped, tops splintered. Books were on the floor, pages torn, covers bent. A shelf had been pulled from the wall, leaving raw plaster and four holes where the screws had been. Chalk dust smeared by boots.

On the wall beside the blackboard, a chart. Torn at one corner, hanging by a single nail. An alphabet chart. But not in Castilian. In Euskara.

My eyes went to it and my feet carried me forward without asking. I stood before it.

A, B, D, E, G, H, I, J, K, L, M, N, Ñ, O, P, R, S, T, TX, TZ, U, X, Z. Letters I knew. Letters I had seen in my grandmother's kitchen in Pamplona, where she kept a similar chart pinned to the wall beside the stove. She had pointed to it when I was five. She had made me sound out the letters. She had spoken Euskara to me — the same guttural consonants, the same clipped vowels, the same rhythm of a language that moved in the mouth like stone and water.

I reached out and touched the chart. Paper. Thin, printed, the ink faded slightly where the sun had hit it through the window. A child's hand had drawn a flower in the margin, small, in pencil, five petals, a stem. A child's hand had practiced the letters at the bottom — A, B, D, E — in careful strokes, uneven, the pencil pressing harder on the downstroke than the upstroke.

My grandmother spoke this language. My father understood it, though he answered in Castilian. The women in the market in Pamplona spoke it to each other, fast, the words overlapping, the rhythm different from the Castilian that surrounded it. The language of the hills. The language the shepherds used. The language of the fueros, written in it, argued in it, the legal and customary rights of the Basque people carried in those letters for centuries.

I pulled the chart from the wall. The nail held for a moment, then the paper tore and the chart came free in my hands. I folded it and put it in my breast pocket.

Tomás found me there. He stood in the doorway. He looked at the overturned desks, the scattered books, the torn chart's empty nail.

"Found anything?" he said.

I shook my head.

He nodded. He did not come in. He waited in the doorway, his beret pushed back, his face calm, and we walked to the plaza together.

---

I saw the flag on the second day. In the street behind the church, in a narrow lane that ran between the school and a row of houses. Someone had torn it from a window or a balcony. It lay in the gutter, half in the mud, half on the stone.

I stopped.

An ikurriña. Red ground, green saltire, white cross. The Basque flag. I had seen it before — in Pamplona, in the old quarter, hanging from a balcony during Aberri Eguna, the Basque national day. My father had pointed at it once. He had said: "Separatists." His voice was flat. He did not say more. He did not need to. The flag meant Basque autonomy without the king, without the Church's primacy, without the fueros as the Carlists understood them. The flag was the enemy's flag.

And here it was. In the mud. Torn down by men wearing red berets.

I crouched. I picked it up. The fabric was damp. The red was dark with mud. The green was bright where the fold had protected it. The white cross was stained. A corner had been torn, the threads pulling free. It was a flag: fabric, dye, stitching. It was light in my hands.

I looked at it. My eyes held it as they had held the crucifix, looking for something in the object that the object itself could not explain.

A woman watched me from a window above. I felt her eyes. I looked up. She was there, middle-aged, her hair pulled back, her face still. She saw me crouching in the gutter with her flag in my hands. She stayed at the window. She watched.

I set the flag down on the stone. I smoothed it with my palm, an old instinct, the kind that makes a man straighten a tablecloth or fold a blanket. I stood. The woman's eyes followed me as I walked away.

I turned the corner and stopped. My hand was on the wall. My breath was short. A pulse beat in my throat. I stood in the narrow lane and looked at my hand on the stone, the calloused palm, the short fingers, a hand that had held a rifle for nine months, and I did not understand why I had picked up the flag or why I had smoothed it or why my throat was tight.

Tomás came around the corner. He saw me standing there, my hand on the wall.

"You all right?" he said.

"Yes."

He looked at my face. He did not ask more. We walked back to the billet together. His boots were steady on the stone. Mine were not.

---

Days passed. The battalion settled in. Billets were established, patrols sent out, the perimeter secured. Vidal organized a supply line — trucks from the south, food, ammunition, mail. Tomás fixed the broken table in our kitchen with a piece of wood and some nails from his pack. Padre Joaquín said Mass in the church each morning. A few soldiers attended. A few townspeople attended — old women, mostly, kneeling in the back pews, their rosaries moving through their fingers.

I walked the town. I could not stop walking it. My eyes moved over the stone walls, the wooden balconies, the church tower, the river below, the mountains above. Everything was familiar. Everything was home.

Each morning I walked a different route. Down to the river, where the water ran clear over stones and a woman washed clothes, her hands red from the cold, the sound of the fabric against the rock carrying up the bank. Up to the ridge above the town, where I could see the valley spread below, green and quiet, smoke rising from chimneys, a hawk circling above the tree line. Through the market, where a few vendors sold bread and cheese and salt cod, their voices low, their eyes on the red berets moving between the stalls. A woman sold peppers from a basket. A man sharpened knives on a stone wheel, the sound carrying through the narrow street. A cheese seller wrapped a piece in paper and handed it to me. I paid. He did not speak. His hand was rough. His eyes were on my pin.

In the mornings, I stood on the balcony of our billet and looked at the mountains. Green. Deep green, the green of old forests, of trees that had stood for a hundred years. Mist in the valley. Sun on the ridgelines. Birds I knew — blackbirds, thrushes, the sharp call of a jay. The air was cool and it smelled of woodsmoke and wet grass and the river.

In Pamplona, I had stood on the roof of the hardware store and looked at the mountains. The Pyrenees to the north, the Sierra de Aralar to the west. Green on green on green.

My eyes could find no difference. They kept moving from the mountains to the valley to the river to the town, searching for the mark that would say: this is enemy territory. I could find no mark. No line. The green did not change.

---

On the fourth day, I sat on the bench in the plaza with the old man. He was there every morning, his hands on his knees, his eyes on the square. I sat beside him. He did not look at me. He looked at the fountain, at the church, at the red berets moving through the street.

We sat for ten minutes without speaking. His hands were brown, the knuckles swollen, the nails cracked. A farmer's hands. A man who had worked the land. His boots were old, resoled, the leather dark with oil. His jacket was wool, brown, patched at the elbow. He smelled of tobacco and earth and something else, something I knew from my grandfather, the smell of a man who has spent his life outside in all seasons.

I said: "Buenos dias."

He looked at me. His eyes were dark, deep-set, the skin around them creased from years of sun. He studied my face. He studied my beret. He studied the pin on my collar. His mouth was a line. His jaw was set. His eyes held mine for five seconds, maybe ten, and in them I saw something I recognized from the mirror, from the mornings in the trench before an attack, from the face of a man who has seen men in uniform come and go, who sits on the bench and does not move.

He said nothing. He looked back at the fountain.

I sat with him for another five minutes. The fountain ran. A child crossed the plaza, carrying bread. A dog slept in the sun. A pigeon landed on the fountain's rim and drank. The church bell rang noon. The old man did not move.

Then I stood and walked away. When I reached the corner, I looked back. He was still there. His hands on his knees. His eyes on the square.

---

Padre Joaquín said Mass on Sunday. The church was half-full, our men in the pews on the left, the townspeople on the right. A line down the nave, invisible but clear. I sat in the third row, Tomás beside me. His beret was on his knee. His hands were folded. He did not sing. He moved his lips during the responses but no sound came.

The soldiers stood and knelt and responded in loud voices, the Navarrese accent filling the vaulted ceiling, bouncing off the stone. Townspeople knelt and responded in low ones, barely audible, the words the same but softer, as if spoken in a room where someone was sleeping. Two Masses in one church. Two congregations under one roof.

An old woman in the row ahead of me held her rosary through the entire Mass. Her fingers moved bead by bead, steady, her lips moving in a rhythm separate from Padre Joaquín's words.

During the consecration, Padre Joaquín held the host above the altar. His hands were steady. His voice was clear. "Hoc est enim corpus meum." This is my body. I looked up at the crucifix above him. The corpus tilted its head. Its eyes were half-closed. It watched the consecration with the same expression I had known all my life — the tilted head, the mouth open as if about to speak.

The same Mass. The same words. The same body and blood.

After Mass, I walked with Padre Joaquín through the town. His limp was more pronounced, the road had been hard, the marching long. His cassock was worn thin at the hem. His hands clasped his prayer book against his chest. He moved slowly, pausing at corners, his eyes on the ground, a man who had walked through many towns and had begun to see that each one was the same.

We passed the school. I stopped. He stopped beside me.

"The school has been closed," I said.

He looked at the building. His eyes moved over the doorway, the shuttered windows, the sign above the door in Euskara and Castilian. A padlock on the door. The paint peeling from the window frames.

"Yes," he said.

"Children learned here."

"Yes."

We stood in silence. His hands moved on the prayer book, fingers tracing the leather cover, a habit of his, a man rubbing a coin in his pocket.

"The chart on the wall," I said. "Euskara. My grandmother spoke it."

He looked at me. His eyes were tired. The lines around them had deepened since the march south.

"Many grandmothers," he said. His voice was quiet. "It is the language of this place. It is the language of the confessional. It is the language of the women who taught their children the Hail Mary."

He looked at the church. He looked at the mountains. He looked at the street where the flag had lain.

"I said Mass to them this morning," he said. "Our men. And their women. The same Mass. The same words. The same body and blood."

He paused. His fingers moved on the prayer book.

"I blessed the requete who took this town," he said. "And I said Mass for the people of this town. The same blessing. The same God. I do not know how to hold both in one hand."

He did not finish. He walked on. I stood in front of the school and watched him go, the slight figure in the black cassock, the limp, the prayer book against his chest, moving through the streets of a town that was ours now, that we had taken, that looked like home.

I stood there for a long time. A child ran past, chasing a hoop. A woman called from a window. The church bell rang three. Life in the town went on, quietly, around the soldiers, through them, past them, as if the soldiers were stones in a river and the water moved around them and continued downstream.

---

I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The pen is in my hand. The hardware store is dark.

I have the chart in a drawer. I took it with me when we left the town. I have never shown it to anyone. It is folded, once, the paper soft from handling, the ink faded where the sun hit it. The child's flower is still there, in pencil, in the margin. Five petals. A stem. A, B, D, E. My grandmother's letters. I look at them and my fingers remember the chart on her wall, the paper smooth under a child's hand, her voice behind me, shaping the sounds, patient, repeating them until my mouth could hold them.
