## First Blood

I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The hardware store is dark, the shelves holding their hinges and their bolts, the smell iron, oil, old wood. Outside, the Calle Mayor is quiet. A cat crosses the street. I can hear its paws on the stone.

I want to write about the village. I want to write about what happened there. But the body does not give me the village first. It gives me the hands. The trigger, the recoil, the smoke. The hands afterward, shaking on the stock of the rifle while the dirt pressed against my knees and the sun was on the back of my neck and somewhere a man was on the ground and did not get up.

The pen moves. The boy moves with it.

---

The feast of the Virgen del Pilar was the twelfth of October. I know this because Padre Joaquín marked it on his breviary with a pencil line, like a man marking a debt he means to pay. The feast of the patroness of Spain. In Pamplona the day means processions, flowers, the cathedral full, women in mantillas, children carrying small flags with the red and yellow. In the village on the meseta it meant a Mass said on an ammunition crate, in a church with no glass in the windows and the wind coming through the apse where a shell had taken the wall.

Padre Joaquín said the Mass. He stood at the altar in his cassock with the blanket over his shoulders. The blanket was dirty now, the wool dark with sweat and road dust, the fringe frayed. He had worn it every day since Pamplona. He wore it as a soldier wears a thing that has become part of the body, so that without it the shoulders feel the wind differently.

The requeté knelt. The red berets were on the ground beside each man, the wool folded, the shape of the head still pressed into the felt. I knelt between Tomás and a boy from Estella whose name I have forgotten. The stone floor was cold through the trousers. My knees found a groove where other knees had worn the stone, generations of knees, the faithful and the dead.

Padre Joaquín's voice carried. It always carried: warm, measured, the cadences of a man who has said these words ten thousand times. "God sees your sacrifice," he said. "God knows your heart. The crusade is His work, and you are His instruments."

I believed him. The words were not decoration. They were not comfort. They were the truth, like the beret, the crucifix, the fueros. The framework held. The Church blessed the cause, the cause blessed the Church, the blessing went from the altar to the rifle to the hand and back again. A circle, complete.

After the Mass, Padre Joaquín blessed the rifles. He walked down the line. He stopped at each man. He made the sign of the cross over the barrel, said the words, and the man bowed his head and picked up the rifle. When he reached me he looked at my face, then at the rifle, then at my hands. My hands were steady. He made the cross. He said the words. He moved to Tomás.

Tomás held the rifle out like a thing he was offering. "Bless it well, Father," he said. "I need it to work."

Padre Joaquín's mouth moved. Not a smile. Something smaller. The acknowledgment of a man who has heard the same joke from soldiers for twenty years and still finds it human. He blessed the rifle. Tomás nodded, pulled it back, checked the sight with the quick hands of a man who trusts his fingers more than his eyes.

---

We moved at dawn. Not the whole battalion — two companies, ours and the Navarrese company from Estella. Vidal had the map open on the hood of a truck. His finger traced a line. "The village is here. The Republicans hold the east end. We take the church and the square and we push them into the riverbed." He looked at the men. His face was flat. "Dig in after. No one advances past the riverbed without my order."

He folded the map. He did not bless us. He did not pray. He put the map in the case, slung the case over his shoulder, walked to the head of the column. The column followed.

The terrain was broken. After weeks of flat meseta, the land buckled. Hills, shallow valleys, olive groves on the slopes, the trees silver-green and twisted. The road wound between the hills, then left the road and we moved through the olive groves in a line, crouching, the rifles across the chests, the berets dark with sweat.

Tomás was in front of me. His back was broad. His pack shifted with each step, the straps creaking. He moved through the olive trees as he moved through everything: steady, without wasted motion. His feet found the ground like a shepherd's — reading the slope, the roots, the loose stone. He did not look back. He did not need to. He knew I was there.

The sun was high. The olives threw thin shadows. The air smelled of dust and the green of the leaves and, under it, something chemical, something that did not belong to the grove. Cordite. Faint. A trace on the wind. The smell of something that had happened here before we arrived.

We reached the edge of the grove. Below us, the ground dropped to a shallow valley and rose again to the village. The church was on the far side, its tower broken halfway up, the stone raw where the masonry had been knocked loose. Houses. White walls. A road through the center. A square with a fountain. I could see all of this — four hundred meters, maybe five — the air clear, the light flat, the village lying in it like a thing on a table.

Vidal was crouched at the edge of the grove. He had the binoculars up. He looked at the village for a long time. He lowered them. He said something to the sergeant. The sergeant moved down the line. The word was passed in whispers that were not whispers, as men pass a word they do not want the wind to carry: we go in ten minutes.

I knelt. The dirt was dry under my knees. I checked the rifle. The bolt moved. The magazine was full. The sight was clean. My hands did these things with the certainty of practice — the same hands that had counted bolts in the store, held the crucifix, folded Tomás's letter to his mother. The hands knew their work. The mind was already behind them.

Tomás crouched beside me. He looked at the village. He looked at me. His face was calm. He reached under his collar and pulled out the scapular his mother had sewed and touched it and tucked it back.

"Ready," he said. It was not a question.

---

We went down the slope. The order was silence. No talking. No coughing. The rifles were off the shoulders, in the hands, fingers beside the triggers, safety catches forward. The ground was scrub and loose stone. The feet slid and caught and slid again. My boots found the same ground Tomás's boots had found. I watched his back. The pack. The straps. The rifle in his hands.

The first shot came from the village. A crack, flat, the sound bouncing off the hill behind us. Then another. Then a burst, automatic, the sound like cloth tearing. The bullets went over us. The sound was high and thin and wrong, the sound of something that should not be in the air, a sound that belonged to a world I had read about but not entered.

We dropped. The order came down the line: get down, get down. I was on my stomach in the scrub. The dirt was in my mouth. The rifle was under me, the barrel in the dirt. I pulled it free and wiped the sight with my sleeve. The shooting continued. The bullets came over us in waves, high, then lower, then high again, as if the shooter was adjusting, learning our position.

Vidal's voice. Not loud. Carried. "Return fire. The church tower."

I rose to my knees. I brought the rifle up. My cheek found the stock. The sight picture was there: the front post, the rear notch, the church tower, the broken stone. I moved the post to the top third of the tower, as Vidal had taught me on the road — the bullet drops. My finger found the trigger. The trigger was cold. The metal was cold and my finger was warm and the space between was the space between the boy who had not fired and the boy who was about to.

I fired.

The recoil pushed into my shoulder. The smoke came back, white, acrid, the smell of cordite sharp in the nose. The sound was inside my head, a crack that was also a pressure, a thing that filled the space behind the eyes. I worked the bolt. I chambered another round. I found the sight picture again. The tower. The stone. I fired again.

Down the line the others were firing. The sound was everywhere — the crack of the rifles, the tearing of the automatics, the shouts, the dust rising. I was part of it now. I was inside it. The rifle was in my hands, the trigger under my finger, the smoke in my nose, the sound in my head. The world had narrowed to the width of the sight picture: the post, the notch, the target.

A man came out of a house. He was running. He ran from the door to the wall of the next house, crouched, his arms pumping. He wore a shirt. Blue, or gray. His head was bare. He ran the way a man runs who knows the distance between one wall and the next is the distance between living and dying. His feet hit the dirt. The dust rose around them.

I fired.

The man's body changed direction. It was a small thing. The running had a line, a trajectory, the body moving forward and down, and the bullet changed the line. The body went sideways. The feet left the ground. The body landed on its side and the dust came up around it and the dust settled and the body did not move.

I worked the bolt. The spent casing jumped. It landed in the dirt beside my knee. The brass was hot. I could feel its heat near my skin. I chambered another round. I found the sight picture. I looked at the place where the man had been. The dust was settling. The body was there. Blue shirt. One arm extended. The face was in the dirt.

I fired two more times at the wall where other men were shooting. The rifle moved in my hands. The recoil was the same each time: a push, a return, a push. The mechanics of it. The shoulder absorbed. The cheek found the stock again without being told. The trigger finger knew the weight of the pull, the point where the resistance broke and the firing pin fell.

It was over in forty minutes. The firing from the village stopped. A white cloth appeared on a pole above the church door. Vidal stood up. He walked down the line. "Cease fire," he said. "Cease fire."

I lowered the rifle. My hands were on the stock. My fingers were curved around the grip. The trigger finger was straight, pressed against the guard. I looked at my hands. They were steady.

Then they were not.

---

The shaking started in the fingers. Both hands. The fingers curled. Uncurled. Curled again. I could not stop them. I pressed them flat on the stock. The wood was warm from the firing. The shaking went through the stock into the wood and the wood did not stop it. I pressed them on the dirt. Dirt was warm under my palms. The shaking went into it. I put the rifle down. I put my hands on my knees. I pressed. The shaking was in the wrists now, the forearms, a tremor that moved through the muscles like water finding a crack, running where the crack led.

I was kneeling. Dirt under my knees. Scrub around me. The sun on my neck. The rifle on the ground. Spent casings in the dirt. Smoke still thin in the air, hanging, the wind not strong enough to move it. The smell of cordite in my nose, on my clothes, in my hair.

Tomás sat down beside me. He did not say my name. He sat. He took his canteen off his belt, uncapped it, held it out. I looked at the canteen. My hand reached for it. The hand shook. The fingers closed around the canteen, the metal warm, the water inside warm. I lifted it and drank. The water tasted of metal. I drank again. Some of it ran down my chin. I did not wipe it.

I gave the canteen back. Tomás capped it. He put it on his belt. He took out a cigarette, lit it with a match. The match head flared. The smoke went up. He took a drag and held the cigarette out to me.

I took it. The paper was warm. The smoke was hot in my throat. I coughed. The coughing shook my whole body and for a moment the hands were shaking with it, the machine coming apart at the joints. I held the cigarette between my fingers, the ash fell, I smoked again.

"The first one is the worst," Tomás said.

I looked at him. His face was close. His eyes were brown and steady. He was not shaking. His hands were still. He sat as he always sat — easy, weight settled, body arranged around its own center. The scapular was outside his shirt now. He had pulled it out at some point and it hung on his chest, the cloth dark with sweat.

"That was the first one," I said.

"I know."

He offered the cigarette again. I took it. The shaking was slower now. The fingers still trembled but the tremor had a rhythm, a pulse, the heart's rhythm finding the hands. I smoked. The smoke went down. The lungs took it. Processing. Absorbing. The cigarette burned. The ash grew. I flicked it. The ash fell on the dirt and the dirt took it.

We sat. The firing had stopped. The silence was the silence after, the silence that is the absence of the thing that was there before. Men were moving along the line. Someone was calling names. Someone was carrying a stretcher. The white cloth still hung on the church door.

Tomás looked at the village. "We take it now?"

"We take it now."

He stood. He held out his hand. I took it. His grip was strong and dry, his palm rough from the crook, the rifle, the years of shepherding. He pulled me up. I stood. The ground was under my feet. The rifle in my hand. The shaking in my fingers. I walked.

---

The village was ours by noon. The Republicans had pulled back to the riverbed. A few bodies in the streets. A man against a wall, sitting, his head forward, his hands in his lap, a hole in his chest the size of a fist. A woman in a doorway, her arm out, her face in the shadow. I did not look at them. I walked past. The rifle on my shoulder. The beret on my head. Dirt on my boots. I walked through the square, past the fountain. The fountain was dry. The stone was warm. My hand touched it as I passed — warm and smooth and indifferent, like every fountain in every village.

We dug in on the far side of the village. The trenches were shallow. The dirt was hard. The shovel rang against stone. Tomás dug beside me. He did not talk. The shovel moved. The dirt piled. The trench deepened. My hands held the shovel — wood and metal, the wood smooth, the metal heavy. The shaking was there but slower, the tremor now a pulse, like the heartbeat: without attention, without effort, the rhythm set.

Vidal came by. He looked at the trench. He looked at the fields beyond. "Good," he said. "Extend it to that rock. Fields of fire."

He moved on. His boots were clean. His uniform was pressed. He had not fired a shot. He had directed the attack from the edge of the olive grove, the binoculars up, the map open, the orders passed in short sentences. He had done his work. The requeté had done theirs.

---

The evening came. The heat dropped. The sky turned orange, then purple. The trenches were done. The men settled. Cigarettes. Water. Bread torn from loaves the village women had brought. The bread was hard, the crust thick, the inside dense. It tasted of flour and salt and the hands of the woman who made it.

I sat in the trench. My back against the dirt wall. The rifle beside me. The beret on my head. My hands in my lap. They were still. The shaking had slowed through the afternoon, the tremor receding like water draining from a pool, and now the hands were quiet, the fingers resting on the thighs, the palms up, the skin dirty, the lines of the palms filled with dust.

Tomás sat beside me. He was eating bread. He tore a piece and held it out. I took it. I ate. The bread was hard. My jaw worked. I ate because I needed to eat. The mind was in the olive grove, on the slope, with the rifle in the hands and the man in the blue shirt running and the body changing direction. The forty minutes between the first shot and the white cloth. The smoke, the sound, the trigger, the recoil. The spent casing jumping from the bolt. Landing in the dirt.

I finished the bread. I drank from the canteen. The water was warm, tasting of metal. I capped the canteen and put it beside me. My hands returned to my lap.

"You did well," Tomás said. He said it as a fact, a statement about the world that did not require evidence. You did well. As he had said I will come home. As he had said We fight, we win, we go home. A truth that was true because Tomás said it and Tomás did not lie.

I said nothing. The man in the blue shirt was dead. I had fired the rifle. The bullet had found him. The body had changed direction. The dust had come up. The arm had been extended. The face had been in the dirt. These were facts. They were not heroic. They were not terrible. They were the mechanics of a thing that had happened, like a bolt setting true in a hinge. The trigger. The recoil. The smoke. The man. The ground.

I held the crucifix. It was in my pocket. My hand found it without being told. The wood was warm from my body. The groove where my father's thumb had been was smooth under my thumb. The corpus was small, the arms even, the head bowed. The weight was the weight of three generations who had held this wood before me, believed what I believed, fought these wars, carried this name.

---

The dark came. The stars came. The Milky Way was across the center of the sky, a band of light thick and pale, and the sky went all the way down and the stars pressed through. The trench was a line of dirt in the dark. The men were shapes. The cigarettes were points of light. The breathing was slow.

I lay on my back. The rifle beside me. The beret under my head. The dirt cool through the blanket. My hands on my chest. The crucifix in my right hand. My fingers closed around it. The wood pressed into the palm. The corpus under the thumb. The groove was there.

I prayed. I did not choose to pray. The hands closed around the crucifix and the breath slowed and the eyes closed and I did what I had been taught to do since the first morning Mass with my father, since the first Hail Mary on my mother's knee, since the first blessing at the font.

The words were not there. I reached for the words. They were not there. The Our Father. The Hail Mary. The Glory Be. The prayers I had said every morning of my life. They were in the mouth somewhere, in the memory of the mouth, in the muscle of the tongue, but they would not come. The hands were praying and the mind was not forming the words and the gap between them was the gap between the crucifix in the hand and the man in the blue shirt on the ground and the dust settling and the arm extended and the face in the dirt.

Under me, the dirt. Above, the stars. The hands held the crucifix. The breath went in and out. The man was dead. I had killed him. These were facts. They sat in the gut like the bread from supper: present, heavy, requiring digestion. I would process them. I would carry them.

I prayed without words. I prayed with the hands and the breath and the dark. The crucifix was in the hand and the hand was shaking, not much, the faintest tremor, the pulse of what the hands were learning, and the prayer was the tremor, the shaking, the thing the hands did when the mind could not do anything at all.

Tomás was sleeping. I could hear his breathing. Steady. The breathing of a man who has decided that sleep is owed. His scapular was outside his shirt. I could see it in the starlight, the cloth dark, the stitching uneven where his mother's hands had sewn it. He slept as he dug, as he marched, as he gave water: completely, without reservation, committed to the task.

The crucifix was in my hand. I prayed. The dark held me. The stars turned. The hands carried the day as the hands carry all days: in the bones, in the grip, in the place where the shaking lives and where the shaking will stop.

---

In the morning, Vidal called the officers and the sergeants. I was near enough to hear. He had the map open. His finger was on a point south of where we were. His voice was flat.

"We are being moved," he said. "The battalion goes to the Madrid front."

The word went down the line. Madrid. The men talked. The talk was low, the way men talk about a thing that is both a destination and a sentence.

Tomás heard it from the man beside him. He looked at me. His face was calm. The brown eyes, the round cheeks, the mouth that was quick to smile and quicker to close.

"Madrid," he said.

"Madrid."

He nodded. He checked his rifle, his pack, his canteen. The hands moved through the familiar inventory: the bolt, the magazine, the bread, the water, the scapular, the letter to his mother. He was ready. He was always ready. The tasks were the same whether the road led to a village on the meseta or to the capital of Spain.

I picked up my rifle. The stock was smooth under my hands. The barrel was clean. The sight was straight. The rifle had done its work yesterday and it would do its work tomorrow. The hands held it. The hands were steady. The shaking was gone. Not gone — quiet. The tremor had receded in the night.

I did not know that the stillness of the hands was the beginning of the numbness. I did not know that the numbness would grow and thicken and carry me through the next village and the next, through Madrid and Teruel and the Ebro.

I knew the rifle was in my hands. I knew the beret was on my head. I knew the crucifix was in my pocket. I knew Madrid was ahead. I knew Tomás was beside me.

I am at the table. The ink is drying at the edge of the well. The pen is in my hand. The hardware store is dark. The cat has moved to the windowsill. The Calle Mayor is quiet.

The boy picked up the rifle. He walked north with the battalion. His hands were steady. He did not know the hands would stay steady. He did not know the steady was the wound.

I am writing this and the pen moves and the boy moves with it — and the distance collapses. I can feel the stock in my hands, the beret on my head, the crucifix in my pocket. The road under my feet. The sun on my neck. Madrid ahead, always ahead, the door that opens and never closes.

The king did not come. But the boy did not know that yet. The boy knew the road. The road was enough.
