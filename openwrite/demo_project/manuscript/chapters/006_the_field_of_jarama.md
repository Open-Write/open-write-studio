## The Field of Jarama

I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The pen is in my hand. The hardware store is dark. The Calle Mayor is quiet.

I want to write about a field. The body gives me the ears first — the ears full of the artillery, the ears full of the sniper cracks and the mortar thumps and the constant, grinding noise of a war that ran around the clock. Before the names, before the count, before the stalemate — the ears. The ears were the body's first memory of Jarama.

---

February 1937. We moved south from Madrid by train and then on foot. The train took us through olive groves and bare trees, a landscape I did not recognize: flat, dry, scarred with gullies and scrub. Not the green valleys of Navarre. Not the meseta of Castile. A landscape that had been fought over and abandoned and fought over again.

Tomás walked beside me. His face was calm. His beret was pushed back. The yoke-and-arrows pin caught the light — a small flash, there and gone, the tin dull from wear.

"Jarama," he said. "South of Madrid. They're trying to cut the road to Valencia."

He said it as a man says the name of a town he has been assigned to.

We arrived at the position in the afternoon. A hole in the ground, a trench dug into the clay, with sandbags stacked along the lip and corrugated tin propped against the forward wall. Vidal moved down the line, assigning positions. His voice was clipped. His face was flat. He pointed.

"Here. You're here."

Tomás and I settled in.

Our first meal was bread and canned sardines. Tomás opened the tin with his bayonet. Oil ran over his fingers. He licked them. He tore the bread in half and gave me the larger piece. We ate in the trench, sitting on the firing step, our backs against the clay wall, our knees drawn up. Sardines and bread and water from the tin that tasted of metal and earth. A smell rose from the trench floor — wet clay, old cordite, the sweat of men who had been in this hole before us and had left their smell in the walls.

"You hear that?" Tomás said.

I listened. A sniper's crack. A whistle. A ping on the tin.

"That's our neighbor," he said. "Friendly fellow. Says hello every twenty minutes."

He folded the sardine tin into the wall, pulled his beret down, closed his eyes. In thirty seconds, he was asleep. I sat beside him and listened to the field and the field spoke in bullets.

---

Our section was three steps long and shoulder-width. Barely deep enough to stand in without exposing the head above the parapet. The tin roof was low. A man had to crouch or sit. Sandbags lined the forward wall, some split, leaking their contents into the mud. A firing step, cut into the clay, slippery with use. An ammunition box served as a table. A water tin. A space for two men to sleep if they slept in shifts and did not stretch.

Dirt was the first thing. It was everywhere: under the fingernails, in the creases of the skin, in the folds of the blanket, in the bread. Clay, red-brown, the color of something bled out. It stuck to everything and it did not dry. I could feel it through the boots, through the socks, through the skin, a cold that settled in and did not leave. My spine curved against the wall. My shoulders pressed into the sandbag. My knees drew up. A body arranges itself around a hole the way water arranges itself around a stone — finding the shape, filling it, becoming the shape.

Tomás dug a shelf into the forward wall with his bayonet. He set the water tin on it. He set the bread on it. He arranged his blanket against the back wall and sat down with his knees up and his rifle across his lap.

"Not bad," he said.

I looked at him.

"Compared to Madrid. At least here we can see the sky."

Above the parapet, a strip of gray. Below the parapet, the field, brown, flat, pocked with shell craters, scarred with the marks of men and machines. And across the field, a line of scrub and low trees, and beyond that, another trench.

Their trench.

---

Sound defined Jarama.

Artillery was loudest — a distant press against the chest, a rumble that shook the sandbags and made the tin rattle. That sound was background. The body absorbed it like a heartbeat, present, constant, beneath notice.

It was the other sounds. The sniper's crack, a single rifle, sharp, close, followed by the whistle of the bullet passing overhead and the ping of it hitting the tin. That sound was the body's teacher. Crack meant the bullet had passed. Whistle meant it was still coming. Silence after meant it missed. I learned to read the sounds as I had once read the creak of the hardware store door. By instinct, by repetition, by the body's insistence that survival required a catalog.

Mortars. Thump of launch, whistle of arc, crump of impact. Body learned the sequence. Thump-whistle-crump. Distant crump: someone else's trench. Close crump: yours.

And beneath it all, the voices. Our voices — the requeté, calling to each other down the line, the Navarrese dialect thick in the mud. And other voices. From across the field. From the other trench.

---

I heard them on the second night.

Tomás was asleep. His face was toward the wall. His hands were under his chin. Scapular on his chest. I was on watch, sitting on the firing step, back against the parapet, ears open to the field.

A voice came. Then another. Then a cluster of them, men talking, the cadence of conversation, the rise and fall of voices in the dark.

At first I thought they were ours. Requeté, further down the line. But the words were wrong. A language I did not recognize: guttural, clipped, consonants hard as stone.

I listened. Artillery came and went. Voices continued.

Then another language. Softer, longer, the vowels stretched. French, maybe. Or something close to it. I had heard French once — a merchant in Pamplona who sold cloth, his accent thick, his words slippery. This was like that but different. Rougher. A man who was cold and tired and did not care about his accent.

I sat on the firing step and listened to unseen men speaking languages foreign to me, and the sound was the sound of men in a trench — the same sounds we made, the same cadence, the same rise and fall. Not the sounds of the godless Reds the propaganda had described. Not the sounds of men who burned churches. Just men. Talking. In the dark. In a hole in the ground. In Spain.

Once, near dawn, a voice carried across the field. Clear. Close. A man speaking in English, slow and deliberate, as if reading aloud or reciting. I could not understand the words. But the rhythm was familiar — the rhythm of a man praying. The cadence of a man speaking to God in the dark, in a language God was supposed to hear. It went on for a minute. Then it stopped. Then silence. Then the sniper's crack resumed.

I sat on the firing step and stayed still.

---

Over the next days, I learned to distinguish them.

German. Short sentences. Clipped. Consonants like rifle fire, staccato, precise. A group of them, maybe twenty meters from our position, their voices carrying across the field when the wind was right.

English. Longer sentences. A rise and fall that sounded almost like singing, though it was something else entirely. A language that moved differently in the mouth.

French. I heard it in the mornings, when the field was quiet and the voices carried. A man singing, softly, barely above a whisper, a melody I did not recognize. It went on for a few minutes and then stopped.

Once, through a gap in the scrub, I saw a figure. A man, standing in their trench, his head and shoulders above the parapet. He was looking at the sky. He wore a flat cap, English, I thought, from pictures I had seen. He stood there for five seconds, maybe ten. Then he dropped down. A sniper's crack followed. Too late. He was already gone. I had seen his face. Young. Thin. A mustache. He looked like a man I might have passed on the street in Pamplona and not noticed.

Tomás heard them too. He stayed quiet at first. On the third day, lying on his back in the trench, his beret over his eyes, he said:

"English."

"Maybe."

"Definitely English. Had a neighbor who worked the sheep in England. Came back with words like that." He paused. "Not Reds, then. Just English."

He said it without concern. The trench across the field held men from England, from Germany, from France, from countries I could not name. They had come to Spain to fight for the Republic. We had come to fight for the Nationalists. We were in holes on opposite sides of a brown field, and the field was worth nothing, and we were killing each other over it.

Propaganda had said godless Reds. Atheists. Anarchists. Men who burned churches and executed priests and desecrated the sacraments. I had believed it, not with the conviction of the catechism but with the acceptance of a man who has been told a thing and has no reason to doubt it.

These men across the field did not sound godless. They sounded tired and cold, men who had traveled a long way to fight in a country that was not theirs, for a cause they believed in, and now they were in a hole and the hole was wet and the war was not what they expected.

I kept this to myself. The thought stayed in my ears, the sound of foreign voices in the dark, where thoughts go when the mouth does not know what to do with them.

---

Snipers were constant.

Crack. Bullet overhead. Then silence, and then another crack. All day. All night. Rhythm of a war fought one bullet at a time.

Body learned. It crouched. It pressed against the wall. It moved below the parapet without being told. Skin felt the air move when a bullet passed close — a shift, a pressure, gone before the mind registered it. Ears catalogued the angles. Crack from the left: sniper to our right. Ping on the tin above: he had the elevation. Thud in the sandbag: he was firing low.

Tomás showed me how to read the dirt. He pointed to a gash in the sandbag. "He's in those trees." He pointed to a mark on the tin. "He's further right. Maybe the ruined house."

He ran his finger along the parapet. "Don't stand. Don't reach. Don't silhouette. He's watching. Always watching."

On the fourth day, a man three positions down, a volunteer from Estella, young, maybe nineteen, stood to adjust a sandbag. A crack. He fell backward into the trench. Bullet took him in the forehead. He was dead before his shoulders hit the floor.

Vidal came. He looked at the body. His face was flat.

"Bury him tonight. Do not stand above the parapet. Do not reach above the parapet. If you need something, crawl."

He looked at the field. Brown. Flat. Cratered.

"This is not a siege. This is a field. A field does not care which side you are on."

He left. Tomás looked at me. His face was round and dirty and calm.

"Good advice," he said.

---

Confessions began on the third night.

Padre Joaquín sat at the end of the trench, in a section where the wall widened slightly — enough for one man to sit and one man to kneel. His back was against the sandbag wall. His cassock was muddy at the hem. A military blanket was around his shoulders. A candle stub sat on an ammunition box, the flame bending in the draft that moved through the trench.

Men came. One by one. They knelt in the dirt. They spoke in low voices. I could hear them from where I sat: not the words but the rhythm. Rise and fall of a man's voice in confession. The pauses. The silences. The priest's voice in response, low, measured, the cadence of thirty years in the confessional.

In Pamplona, confession was a matter of minutes. Bless me, Father, for I have sinned. I missed Mass. I spoke unkindly to my mother. I entertained an impure thought. Five minutes. The absolution was quick. A man walked out lighter, the weight of the sin transferred to the formula of the words.

Here, the confessions were longer. Men knelt and stayed for ten minutes, fifteen, twenty. Voices went on. Pauses grew. Padre Joaquín's voice came through, still measured, still pastoral, but something in it had changed. A slowness. A hesitation between the question and the answer, as if the answer required more thought than it once had.

I went on the fifth night. Knelt in the dirt, beret in my hands. The yoke-and-arrows pin pressed against my thumb. Candle was low. Padre Joaquín's face was half in shadow, the lines deepened by the flickering light. His eyes were on the ground.

"Bless me, Father, for I have sinned. It has been three weeks since my last confession."

"What is your sin, my son?"

I had killed men. I had fired the rifle and men had fallen. This was war. This was the crusade. Catechism was clear: killing in a just war, under lawful authority, was not a sin. Padre Joaquín had said so. He had blessed the rifles. He had blessed the requeté.

"I have killed men," I said.

"How many?"

"I don't know. I fire. They fall. I don't count."

A pause. Candle flickered. Artillery came and went — distant rumble, shift in the sandbags, tin rattling.

"Do you feel remorse?"

I looked up. His face was in shadow. His hands were in his lap — small hands, the priest's hands, hands that bless and baptize and give the sacrament. They were still.

"I was told it was just," I said. "I was told God was with us. I believed it."

A longer pause. Candle burned lower. Wax pooled on the box. I could hear the man in the next position breathing. Wind on the parapet. Artillery, distant, constant.

"God is with you," Padre Joaquín said. His voice was warm, measured, certain. But the certainty was slower. Words came as if from further away. "The cause is just. The Church has said so."

He paused. His hands moved in his lap, a small motion, fingers folding and unfolding.

"Pray for clarity," he said. "The killing is not a sin if the cause is just. But you must know it is just. Not believe. Know."

He gave the absolution. Words were the same. Formula was the same. Sign of the cross was the same. But something in the delivery was slower, as if the words had further to travel from the place where they were kept to the place where they were spoken.

I made the act of contrition. I stood. Another man was already kneeling.

I went back to my position. Tomás was awake. He looked at me.

"Confession?"

"Yes."

"Better?"

I stayed quiet. Answer was in my ears — Padre Joaquín's voice, the hesitation, the pause between the question and the response, as if the response were no longer stored in the place where the priest expected to find it.

---

Days passed. Trench was the world. Parapet was the horizon. Sky was a strip of gray. And across the field, in another trench, men spoke in languages I could not parse and fired rifles at the sound of our voices.

Food was bread, canned meat, sometimes rice, cooked over a fire Tomás built in a hole in the wall. Coffee was bitter. Water tasted of the tin. Sleep came in shifts: two hours on, two hours off, the body learning to rest in increments, never deep, never complete. Skin stayed damp. Clothes stayed dirty. A man's smell became his identity: the requeté smelled of clay and cordite and sweat, and after a week, a man could not smell himself anymore. Only others.

Snipers took men. Mortars took men. A man stood at the wrong moment. A man reached for a water tin. A man leaned too far above the sandbag. Dead accumulated slowly — one here, one there — the way a field accumulates stones after a rain.

Vidal read the names each evening. He stood at the end of the trench, paper in his hand, face flat, voice clipped. Name, hometown, date. He folded the paper. Names accumulated. Trench grew quieter.

Padre Joaquín blessed each one. He came with his leather pouch: communion wafers, wine, prayer book. He knelt beside each body, hands shaking. Blessing was the same: same words, same God, same sacrament. But his hands shook, and the shaking was new.

Tomás talked around the dead. He ate and slept and cleaned his rifle. He watched the field. He talked about food, about sleep, about his mother's hen.

"She writes that the hen is laying again," he said, folding his letter and putting it in his breast pocket. "Three eggs last week. She says it's the spring."

It was February. In Navarre, spring was still a month away. But his mother had written it, and he believed it, and the belief was its own warmth.

---

On the eighth day, the International Brigades attacked.

We heard them before we saw them. Voices in the dark — German, shouting orders. Clink of equipment. Sound of men moving through scrub. Then machine guns opened up and rifles cracked and noise was a wall — solid, physical, pressing against the ears and the chest and the skin.

I fired. Rifle kicked. Brass spun into the dirt. Smoke rose from the barrel. I worked the bolt. I fired again. Beside me, Tomás fired — steady, flat, jaw set, eyes on the field. Men came across the cratered ground. They fell. More came. They fell. I could hear them — not just the gunfire but the voices. Shouting. Calling out. A man screaming. A voice giving orders in German, sharp, urgent. And then a voice in English: "Keep low! Keep bloody low!" And then the machine gun drowned everything.

Attack lasted twenty minutes. Body did not measure time. It measured the space between trigger pull and bullet finding its mark. It measured the distance between parapet and men running toward it. It measured the weight of rifle in hands and kick against shoulder and heat of barrel through cloth. My ears rang. My skin was slick with sweat and dirt. My shoulder ached from the recoil. I fired until the bolt clicked on an empty chamber. I reloaded. I fired again.

Then it stopped. Men withdrew. Field was quiet. Smoke drifted across the craters. A smell came — cordite, blood, the sharp smell of fear in the sweat. My hands were shaking. I set the rifle down. I looked at my fingers. Dirt and oil and a cut on the thumb where the bolt had caught it. I had not felt it.

Vidal counted. He walked the line, stopped at each position, made marks on a paper. When he reached the end, he folded the paper and read the names.

Seventeen.

Name, hometown, date. Name, hometown, date. Seventeen names. Seventeen hometowns. Seventeen dates. Some I recognized. Most I did not. They were names. They were facts. They went into the air and then they went into the ground and the ground held them without comment.

Padre Joaquín came. He blessed the dead. He knelt in the dirt beside each body. His hands shook. Blessing was the same: same words, same God, same sacrament. But his hands shook, and the shaking was new.

We buried them at dawn. Graves were shallow — three feet, maybe less. Clay was hard. Shovels struck stone. Men dug in shifts. I dug. Tomás dug. We dug without speaking. Clay came up in chunks. Red-brown. Wet. A smell of earth and iron. We laid them in and covered them. We marked each grave with a stick. We carved names with a knife. Seventeen sticks. Seventeen names. Seventeen mounds of clay that would wash flat in the first rain.

Padre Joaquín stood at the end of the row. He held his prayer book. He read the words for the dead. His voice was steady. His hands were not.

---

That night, Tomás and I sat in the trench. Candle was a stub on an ammunition box. Dark was thick. Artillery came and went.

"Seventeen," Tomás said.

I nodded.

He looked at the parapet. Above it, the strip of sky, black, starless, clouds low.

"We held," he said.

I nodded again.

He was quiet for a long time. His hands were on his knees. His face was in shadow. Beret on his head. Pin caught the candle — a small flash, quick, gone.

"Men across the field are holding too," he said.

His voice was flat.

"We hold. They hold. Nobody moves." He paused. "That's the field."

He leaned his head against the wall. He closed his eyes. His breathing slowed. He slept.

I sat. Candle burned low. Wax pooled. Artillery came and went. Voices across the field were quiet. Field was quiet.

I held the crucifix. Wood was warm. Groove under the thumb. Corpus under the fingers. Weight.

I thought of the voices. German. English. French. They were in their trench and I was in mine and the field was between us and the field was worth nothing and we were dying for it.

I thought of Padre Joaquín's hands. The shaking. Fingers folding and unfolding. Blessing that was the same but slower. Pause between question and answer. A priest who had blessed the rifles and blessed the requeté and blessed the dead and now his hands shook when he knelt in the dirt and spoke the words that were supposed to carry a man from this life to the next.

I thought of my father's letter. *The king was not consulted. I do not know what we are fighting for now.*

Wood was warm. Corpus was small. Arms extended. Feet crossed. My grandfather had held this crucifix. My father had held this crucifix. In three wars. Same wood. Same corpus. Same weight.

---

I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The pen is in my hand. The hardware store is dark.

I did not know.

Field held. Neither side advanced. Graves were shallow mounds of red-brown clay behind the trench. Sticks marked them — pale wood, names carved with a knife. Seventeen sticks. Seventeen names. Flies moved over the graves. Wind moved the grass on the parapet. Artillery was distant. Field was quiet.
