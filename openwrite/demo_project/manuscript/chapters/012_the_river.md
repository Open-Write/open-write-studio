# Chapter 12 — The River

---

I am writing this in the back room of the hardware store. The inkwell is full. I filled it this morning, the funnel, the slow pour, the rag. My father's rag. The crack in the wood runs from the left edge to just past center. I have been following it with my thumb as I write. It is deeper near the center. The table is older. The hand holding the pen is older.

I am going to write about the Ebro. I have put it off. Three chapters I have written since I sat down — the frozen streets of Teruel, the train to Pamplona, my father at the window — and each time I reached this chapter I found something else to do. A letter to answer. A hinge to count. The body knows what the hand resists. The body knows what comes next.

Tomás comes next.

---

July 1938. We crossed the Ebro at night. The pontoons were laid between vineyards, flat-bottomed boats roped together, planks across them, and the water below was black and slow and wide. The far bank dissolved into dark. The current pulled at the pontoons. The planks shifted under our boots. The men ahead were shapes, shoulders, rifles, the glint of a canteen. Silence held. The engineers had worked in silence. We crossed the same way. Water against the boats, boots on wood, the occasional clink of a rifle barrel touching a buckle. Those were the only sounds.

Tomás was behind me. I knew because I could hear him breathing, a particular rhythm, slightly faster than mine. He had taken the extra ammunition belt from Ruiz, who had a bad knee, and the blanket roll from Lekuona, who was seventeen and could barely carry his own rifle. Tomás did this without being asked. It was the same at Teruel, at Aragon, since the first day when he appeared beside me in the Plaza del Castillo and said his name and offered to carry the spare canteen. A man who filled space with his body and his silence.

We climbed the far bank. Vineyard, then rock. The terrain changed in a hundred meters, from the soft alluvial soil of the riverbank to limestone. Pale in the starlight. Eroded, pocked with holes where water had eaten into it over centuries. The hillside rose ahead of us, bare of trees, bare of grass. Rock, dust, the stumps of things that had been growing before the shelling began.

We climbed for an hour. The ridge was ahead, a dark line against the sky, ragged, like the edge of a broken plate. It had been fought over for three weeks before we arrived. Three weeks of attack and counterattack, hill and valley, advance and retreat. The men we were relieving came down as we went up. I kept my eyes on the path.

Vidal was at the top. He stood in a shallow trench dug into the limestone, his map case against his chest, his officer's cap dark with dust. He looked at us — a quick count, eyes moving from face to face — and turned back to his map. He said: "Dig deeper. The rock is soft on the north side. Mortars walk across this ridge every morning at six." His voice was flat. Professional. The voice he used for orders.

We dug. The rock was not soft. It was limestone, fractured. The picks rang against it, sparks flew, the holes we made were shallow. A foot deep. Enough to lie in. Not enough to save you when the mortar came.

---

The ridge had no name. Or it had a name I never learned. The maps called it by a number. The men called it by the number. I called it nothing. It ran east-west above the river. The far side dropped to a valley where the Republicans held trenches and machine-gun positions. The near side dropped to us. Between the two ridges was a slope of blasted rock, stripped to stone. The surface of the moon.

The Ebro valley in the summer of 1938 had been stripped of everything livable. The trees were gone, cut by shellfire, burned to stumps that jutted from the rock like broken teeth. The grass was gone. The soil was gone. What remained was stone and dust — white, gray, pale yellow — and a red iron dust that covered everything. It got into the rifles, the food, the water, the eyes. It never settled. The wind from the river carried it in waves, and the waves broke against the ridge and the dust hung in the air like a curtain that never lifted.

The sound was constant. The memory of the Ebro is first a memory of sound. Artillery from both sides — the Nationalist batteries behind us, the Republican batteries across the valley — firing day and night. The guns were far enough away that the individual rounds leaving the barrels were inaudible. You heard only the arrival. A whistle that rose and fell. Then the impact. Then the echo rolling back across the valley. And under the artillery, the machine guns, a sound like tearing cloth, long bursts that stuttered and stopped and stuttered again. And under that, the rifles, single shots, scattered, men firing at shapes they could barely see. Above all of it, the aircraft.

German, Italian, Republican. The sky was never empty. The Condor Legion flew in formation, Junkers 52 trimotors, fat and slow, dropping their loads on the Republican positions across the valley. The Italian Savoias came lower, their engines a higher pitch, a whine that cut through the heavier drone of the Germans. Sometimes a Republican fighter came up from the south — a Polikarpov, green, with red stars on the wings — and the anti-aircraft guns would open up and the tracers would arc across the sky. The fighter would dive and pull away. The guns would follow it until it disappeared. The sound of engines was the sound of breathing. You stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing your own pulse. It was always there. The silence, when it came — a lull between barrages, a gap in the air traffic — was worse. Silence meant something was about to happen.

Tomás and I shared a position on the north face of the ridge. A shallow hole in the limestone. Two men deep, if you lay flat. The hole was too shallow to stand or sit. We lay on our sides or on our backs and watched the sky and waited for the mortar.

The mortar came every morning at six. I will write about this with precision. The rounds walked from east to west. You could hear them leave the tube — a cough, a thump — and then the whistle, rising, and then the impact. The first round would land fifty meters east of our position. The second, thirty meters. The third, ten. After the third, the pattern shifted, sometimes it walked past us, sometimes it came back. You learned to count. One. Two. Three. Move. Tomás and I moved after the third round every morning for four months. We moved to the south side of the ridge, where the rock was broken into steps and shelves, and we pressed ourselves into the steps and waited. Then we moved back.

This was our life. I will not make it into something it was not. It was not heroic. It was not meaningful. It was the daily labor of staying alive in a place that was trying to kill us. I still carried the crucifix. I still prayed, haltingly, the words coming slower each week. Tomás did not pray. He carried his mother's scapular on a cord around his neck and he touched it before the mortar came and he said nothing. The touching was enough. The cord was enough. The body remembered what words could not hold.

---

August. The heat was a wall. The limestone held it and gave it back. The air in the holes shimmered. The dust was a constant taste in the mouth. Water was the currency. The river was below us, the Ebro, wide and slow, brown with silt, but getting to it meant crossing two hundred meters of open rock under observation. The engineers laid pipes. They were hit, repaired, hit again. Water came in cans, carried by men who ran the two hundred meters and hoped the snipers were eating or sleeping or looking the other way.

Tomás organized the water. He did this without being asked, the way he organized everything. He knew which men were weakest. He knew which canteens leaked. He knew the hours when the snipers were most active. Midmorning was safest, the sun was behind our positions and the glare made the Republican scopes useless. He made a schedule. He shared his own water when the schedule failed. I saw him give his canteen to Lekuona, the boy, and drink nothing for an entire afternoon. I kept quiet. Tomás would have shrugged. The kind of small act that kept men alive.

He wrote letters. He still dictated them to me, as he had since the beginning. His writing had improved — he could form the words now, slowly, his large hand gripping the pen like a tool — but he preferred to speak them. "Tell my mother the food is not so bad. Tell her I am well. Tell her the heat is worse than the cold." I wrote what he said. His weight loss, his loose shirt, the new lines around his eyes. She would see them soon enough, when he came home.

If he came home.

The thought was there, like the dust, in the air, in the lungs, in the eyes. Impossible to avoid.

---

September. The Republicans counterattacked. They crossed the river at night, the same pontoons, the same flat boats, the same silence, and hit the line to our east. We could hear the fighting. Small arms. Machine guns. The heavier crump of grenades. Flares went up and the sky turned green-white and the shadows of the rocks leaped and shifted. The flares died. The dark came back. Vidal moved men to the right flank. He left us in place. He said: "Hold here. If they come over the ridge, you are the second line." His voice was flat. The same voice he used for every order.

The ridge held. The counterattack was stopped. The Republicans pulled back across the river. The dead were left on the slope, shapes in the dust, motionless. They lay there for three days before the details could get to them. The smell came on the second day. Tomás tied his scarf over his nose and mouth and went back to organizing the water.

We were rotated out for two days. A village behind the lines, stone houses, a church with no roof. We slept on the floor of the church. The altar was intact. The tabernacle was open, empty. Someone had taken the vessels. Or the vessels were simply absent. I lay on the stone floor and stared at the sky through the gaps where the roof had been. My body resisted the silence. The silence was so complete it was a sound of its own. A ringing. A pressure in the ears. I lay there and listened and sleep eluded me.

Tomás slept. He slept as he did everything, completely, without reservation. On his back, his hands on his chest, his mouth open. The scapular cord was visible at his collar. He snored. A sound that had annoyed me at Teruel, when the cold made sleep precious and the snoring stole it. A sound I would have given anything to hear later, after, when the silence was permanent.

We went back to the ridge. The mortar was waiting. Six o'clock. The walk began again.

---

October. The rain came. The limestone turned to mud, a thin gray paste that coated the rock and made every surface treacherous. The holes flooded. We bailed them with helmets. The water was gray with limestone dust. Tomás dug a channel, a small trench leading from our hole to a crack in the rock where the water could drain. It took him four hours with a pick and a broken piece of shrapnel used as a trowel. The channel worked. The water drained. Our hole was still wet but passable. Tomás lay in it that night and said: "At least it's not Teruel." He said it with a half-smile, a shrug.

The rain stopped. The cold came. A damp October cold that settled into the rock and into the bones. Different from Teruel. No minus-twenty, no men freezing in their holes. The blankets were thin. We shared. Tomás and I lay back to back in the hole, his shoulder against mine, the blankets over both of us. His body was warm. He was always warm. A stocky man, broad-shouldered, built for the cold of the Navarrese mountains. The Ebro heat had worn him down. The cold revived him. He slept better. He ate more. He organized the water and the ammunition and the rotation with the same quiet efficiency.

I want to hold him here. In this moment. Alive, warm, his shoulder against mine, the blankets over us, the mortar finished for the night, the stars above the ridge. The scapular cord at his collar. His breath, slightly faster than mine. The half-smile when he said "At least it's not Teruel." I want to hold him here because what comes next is what always comes next, and the holding changes nothing, and the writing changes nothing, and the ink on this page is no substitute for the man.

But I am looking. I am calling it.

---

November 3rd. A Wednesday. I know it was a Wednesday because Vidal had received the weekly supply report on Tuesday evening and he read it aloud to the officers and the report listed the ammunition, the food, the medical supplies, the number of men still fit for duty. Seventy-one. Out of a hundred and twenty. Forty-nine dead or wounded. The rest were still on the ridge.

The morning was clear. The rain had stopped two days before. The sky was pale blue. The air was cold. The dust was low. The mortar came at six. One. Two. Three. We moved to the south side of the ridge. The rounds walked past. We moved back.

Tomás was sitting in the hole. He had his rifle across his knees. He was cleaning it, with a rag and oil, the movements his father had taught him for a shepherd's tools. His hands were large and scarred and they moved with the certainty of a thousand mornings.

He looked up.

"I have been thinking," he said.

This was unusual. Tomás kept his thoughts to himself. He spoke when he had something to say and fell silent otherwise. The sentence hung in the air.

"About Aoiz," he said. "The wheat field above the house. In summer. When the wheat is high."

I looked at him. His face was thinner than it had been in July. The lines around his eyes were deeper. His hair was too long — it always was — and it fell across his forehead in dark strands. His hands were on the rifle. The scapular cord was at his collar.

"I want to go home," he said.

He said it simply. Like a man saying he is hungry or thirsty or tired. A need stated plainly. His hands had stopped moving on the rifle. They rested on the stock, the fingers curled, the knuckles pale. He looked past me, past the ridge, past the dust, to a wheat field above a house in a town I had never seen.

"You will," I said.

He looked at me. His eyes were dark. Brown. Steady. He was silent. We both knew what the war had done to promises. We both knew what the ridge had done to the future. Between us was everything we had shared, the trenches, the cold, the heat, the water, the letters to his mother, the shoulder against mine in the dark. None of it needed speaking. It existed in the body, and the body does not require language.

He went back to cleaning the rifle.

---

The mortar came again at four in the afternoon. This was unusual. The morning barrage was routine. An afternoon barrage meant the Republicans were adjusting, shifting fire plans, testing responses, preparing something. Vidal sent a runner to check the eastern flank. The runner came back. Nothing.

The first round landed to the east. The second, closer. The third —

The third round landed in our hole.

I was three meters away. I had stepped out to check the drainage channel, Tomás's channel, the one he had dug with a pick and a piece of shrapnel. I was on my knees, clearing debris from the crack, when the whistle came. The whistle was different. Closer. Higher. The sound a round makes when it is falling, not flying. I dropped flat. The rock hit my back. The dust came. The ringing came.

I stood. The hole was gone. The rock had collapsed. Dust and limestone and the splintered wood of the rifle stock. A shape in the dust.

Tomás.

I reached him in three steps. I pulled the rock away. His body was underneath, on his side, his hands still on the rifle, or where the rifle had been. The stock was shattered. His hands were around nothing. His eyes were open. He was looking at me.

He was alive. The round had not killed him outright. Shrapnel had torn through his left side, the ribs, the lung. I could see it. I will stop there. He was bleeding. The blood was dark. It pooled in the dust and the dust turned red.

I knelt. I pulled him toward me. His head was against my chest. My hands were on his side, pressing, as I had pressed at Teruel, as I had pressed at Jarama, as the body knows to press when blood is leaving and the man is dying. My hands were red. His blood was warm. It was the warmest thing on the ridge.

He looked at me. His eyes were clear. His breathing was steady. His hands were still on the rifle, or where the rifle had been.

"Tell my mother," he said.

His voice was quiet. Quiet, hesitant, almost inaudible. The register he used in his letters. The register he used when the letters were for his mother and no one else.

"Tell my mother —"

He did not finish the sentence.

His eyes stayed open. His body stayed still. The breath went out and stayed gone and the weight of him settled against my chest and the warmth of his blood cooled against my hands. The ridge was quiet for a moment. A gap in the artillery, a lull in the machine guns. A silence that pressed against the eardrums and held.

I held him.

I held him, his head against my chest, my arms around his shoulders, his body heavy the way a sleeping body is heavy. But this was sleep's opposite. The weight was different. Sleep is a weight that holds itself. Death is a weight that does not.

I held him and the dust settled and the artillery resumed and the machine guns stuttered and the sound of engines came back, the constant, the always, the sound of a sky indifferent to what happens on the ground beneath it.

---

I wrapped him in a blanket. The blanket we had shared, thin, gray, army-issue, worn through at the edges. I wrapped him carefully, because care is what remains when everything else has been taken. I tucked the edges. I folded the flap over his face. I had seen it. The clear eyes. The unfinished sentence. That was enough for the rest of my life.

His mother's scapular was on the cord at his neck. I reached under the blanket and worked the cord free, gently, the way you work a knot from a thread you do not want to break. The scapular was small. Brown wool, frayed at the edges, the image of the Virgin faded by sweat and rain and the oil of his skin. His mother had sewed it. He had touched it before every battle. He had touched it that morning.

I put it in my pocket. The pocket with the crucifix. Wood and wool. The two things that had kept us alive on the ridge. One of us, anyway.

I marked the position. A cairn, three stones stacked on the rock. The way you mark a place you intend to return to. A grave when there is no time for a grave because the mortar is still walking and the ridge is still contested and the dead must wait for the living to finish what they started.

I went back to the hole. What was left of it. I cleared the rock. I dug with my hands. I found the pieces of Tomás's rifle, the stock, the barrel, the bolt. I stacked them beside the cairn. I lay in the hole. The dust was red. His blood. I lay in it. I did not move. The mortar came again at six. I did not move. The rounds walked across the ridge. East to west. The whistle. The impact. The dust. I did not move.

---

Vidal came at dusk. He stood at the edge of the hole and looked at the blanket and the cairn and the pieces of the rifle. His face held. The face he wore at every position, at every cairn, at every blanketed shape on every ridge he had ever held.

"Eguía," he said.

"Yes."

A pause. He looked at the ridge. The Republican positions across the valley. The blasted rock. The dust.

"He was a good soldier," Vidal said.

He said it flat, factual, a report filed, a line in a ledger. He turned and walked back to his position. His boots crunched on the limestone. His officer's cap was dark with dust. He walked away without turning.

---

The next days were machinery. The continued fighting, the rotation, the relief — the machinery of war doing what the machinery of war does. It ground on. I ground with it.

In late November, the requeté were pulled from the line. We walked down the ridge — the same ridge we had climbed in July, the rock, the dust, the pale limestone — and the men walked in silence. The silence of men who have lost things beyond naming. I counted. As I had counted the mortar rounds. One. Two. Three.

Sixty-three.

Of the hundred and twenty who entered the line in July, sixty-three walked out. I counted them as they passed me on the path down to the river. Faces I knew. Faces I did not know, replacements, men who had come in August or September to fill the holes, to stand in the places where other men had stood. Some were boys who had arrived in August and aged twenty years in three months. Some were veterans of Jarama who had been pulled from reserve units to fill gaps that could not be filled. They walked slow, deliberate, each step placed with care, the walk of men who had learned the ground.

I crossed the river. The pontoons were different — newer, wider, the engineers had improved them — but the water was the same. Black. Slow. Wide. I crossed at night, as I had crossed in July. The stars were above me. The current was below me. The scapular was in my pocket, next to the crucifix. The weight of both was the weight of a man who was not crossing because a mortar round had taken him from a ridge in the blasted rock above a river that did not care.

---

I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The crack runs through the wood from the left edge to just past center. The pen is in my hand.

I went to Aoiz. Not then — later. After the war. 1939. I took the bus from Pamplona. Thirty kilometers south. The road winds through the valleys, through the passes, through the green that Tomás had spoken about. The wheat field above the house. In summer. When the wheat is high.

His mother opened the door. A small woman. Dark hair going gray. Hands that were large, like his, scarred from the smallholding. She looked at me for a long moment. Her hand went to the doorframe. Her eyes dropped to my pocket, the shape of the scapular through the cloth. She stepped back and held the door open.

I gave her the scapular. Brown wool. Frayed at the edges. The image of the Virgin faded.

"Tomás asked me to give you this," I said.

She held it. Her fingers closed around it. Her jaw was set. She held it carefully, with purpose, the hands of a woman holding something she had made once and was holding again. A long moment passed between us. The hallway behind her was dark, the walls bare, a crucifix above the coat rack.

"Thank you," she said.

She closed the door.

I walked back to the bus stop. The wheat field was above the house. The wheat was not high. It was November. The field was stubble. Brown stalks. The earth was dark. A field that would grow again in spring. A field that did not know it had lost the man who looked at it from the ridge and wanted to come home.

The crucifix is still in my pocket. Wood and wool. The crucifix from my father. The scapular I gave to Tomás's mother in Aoiz, in a hallway with bare walls and a crucifix above the coat rack. One I kept. One I returned.

The inkwell is full. The pen is in my hand. Tomás is on the ridge. He will always be on the ridge. The wheat field above Aoiz will grow and be cut and grow again and the man who wanted to see it will not see it and the sentence he began will not be finished and the scapular will remain in my pocket until I give it away or until the pocket wears through and the wool falls to the floor of whatever room I am in when the carrying ends.

The body does not forget. The body dumps its memories on the floor and says: sort it out. I have sorted. The ridge. The dust. The mortar. The blanket. The cairn. The unfinished sentence. The weight of him against my chest.

Sixty-three walked out.
