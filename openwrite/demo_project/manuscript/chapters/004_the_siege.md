## The Siege

I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The funnel is beside it, the rag folded, the crack in the wood running from the corner to the second joint. Outside the hardware store the Calle Mayor is dark. A dog barks once and stops. The shelves hold their hinges and their bolts and the silence holds everything else.

Madrid. The word is in my mouth like a stone I have been carrying since the meseta. I want to set it down. I want to write what the city sounded like. What the eyes remembered, the ears already knew. The ears got there first.

The body gives me the noise before the picture. It always does. The rumble in the chest before the rumble in the sky. The vibration in the teeth before the artillery in the hills. The body says: this is bigger than you. Listen.

I am listening. The pen moves. The boy moves with it.

---

We came to Madrid from the north. The road was dust, then stone, then dust again. The land changed. The meseta flattened behind us, the hills rose, the olive groves thinned. The road widened. The traffic thickened: trucks, carts, men on foot, mules pulling ammunition wagons. The column slowed. The requeté walked in files on either side of the road, letting the vehicles pass. The dust was constant. It rose from the wheels and the hooves and the boots, hung in the air like a second sky, brown and close, pressing on the face and the hands and the rifle.

I heard Madrid before I saw it. The war. The artillery came first, a low, steady pulse from the south and west, arriving through the ground more than the air. The village had been rifle fire, sharp and personal, the crack of a single shot and the tearing of a single automatic. This was a rhythm without a source, a vibration in the ribs, a thing you felt in the fillings of your teeth and the arches of your feet. The ground transmitted it. The boots felt it. The body knew it before the mind gave it a name. We stopped on the road and listened. The men around me were quiet. The column had been talking — the usual talk, the small complaints, the jokes about the dust and the food and the distance — and the talk stopped. The silence of men listening. The silence of men who have arrived at a place they have heard about and are hearing for the first time.

Tomás walked beside me. His pack was high on his shoulders. His face was dirty. He had shaved once since the village and the beard came in patches, dark on the jaw, thin on the cheeks, the face of a man who would never grow a proper beard. He cocked his head.

"Artillery," he said.

"I know."

"Big."

I nodded. It was bigger than anything I had heard. The village had been a church, a square, a riverbed, forty minutes of rifle fire and a white cloth. This was a city. The war here ran around the clock. The white cloth belonged to smaller wars.

We crested a low ridge. Madrid opened below us.

I had expected beauty from the photographs in the Pamplona newspapers — the Gran Vía, the Retiro Park, the Palace. Smoke rose from a dozen points. The buildings were gray. The smoke was gray. The sky was gray. Between the smoke the sun found gaps, lit the walls white. The glass flashed, sharp and quick, like sparks from a flint. The city was enormous. It filled the valley, climbed the hills, spread to the edges of sight, kept going. Pamplona was a walled town. The meseta was empty. Madrid was a body — dense, sprawling, breathing smoke.

The artillery pulsed. From here I could see the flashes on the hills to the west — small lights, quick, followed by the vibration that had been in my teeth since the ridge. The guns were distant. The pulse was constant. It came through the ground and the air and the chest. It became the texture of the day, as the dust had been the texture of the march: present, without beginning or end.

We descended into the outskirts. The buildings closed around us. Walls, windows, doors. The windows were dark or broken or covered with sandbags. The doors were barricaded — furniture, earth, corrugated tin. A woman stood in a doorway holding a pot of water. She looked at the column and went inside. A child sat on a pile of rubble, pulling a piece of wire through his fingers. He watched us pass with eyes that were old. The streets were narrow and the column compressed. The men walked closer together. The rifles were in the hands now. The echo changed. The artillery was still there but the buildings caught it and threw it back, closer, more personal, bouncing between walls and arriving from directions that had no guns. The air smelled of plaster dust, cooking fires, something sweet and rotting underneath, the smell of a city where the garbage was not collected and the dead were sometimes in the walls.

A burst of machine-gun fire. Close. The tearing was the same as the village but louder, sharper, the cloth ripped by a larger hand. The men ducked. Some pressed against the walls. Others kept walking — men who had done this before, men who knew a wall would stop a bullet and a street would not.

Vidal was at the front. He walked straight through it. The map case over his shoulder, his eyes on the buildings, his mouth closed. He had been here before, or somewhere like here. Morocco. The Rif. The colonial wars where gunfire in a city was the sound of a Tuesday. He turned and looked at the column. His face was flat.

"Keep moving. Stay close to the walls."

---

The position was a row of buildings on the eastern edge of the University City district. The buildings had been apartments once. Now they were fortifications. Sandbags in the windows, machine guns on the upper floors, communication trenches cut through the walls into the buildings behind. The requeté were to hold the reserve line, two blocks back from the front. Vidal's orders. The Army of Africa was ahead of us.

I saw them that evening.

The Army of Africa. The Regulares. The Legion. The names were in every mouth in Pamplona, spoken over wine in lowered voices, as men speak of things they have glimpsed only in photographs.

They came down the street in a column, stride long and easy, pack riding high, the step of men who had made a profession of it. The Regulares were first. Moroccan soldiers. Dark faces, dark eyes, short men with compact bodies and rifles held at the port with a familiarity worn into the hands. Their uniforms were different — the brown of the colonial service, the boots higher, the equipment cleaner. They moved in silence. Their eyes were on the buildings, the rooftops, the windows. They looked at everything. They saw everything. Their faces were still.

Behind them, the Legion. Spanish soldiers, but harder than us. Harder than the requeté. They wore the uniform differently — open collars, sleeves rolled, the bearing of men who had decided that regulations were suggestions and survival was the only discipline that mattered. Their faces were drawn tight over the bones, the eyes set deep. They carried their rifles with one hand, the barrel forward, the stock against the hip. They walked with a rolling gait, the stride of men who had marched across the Rif and the Sahara and the Strait.

A Legionnaire looked at me. His eyes were dark. The skin around them was creased from sun, dust, the squinting a man does when he is looking at distances for a living. He looked at my beret. At my face. At the rifle in my hands. His mouth moved. A grimace, maybe. The recognition of a man who sees a younger version of himself. There was a scar on his chin, a pale line from the lip to the jaw. The scar had healed badly. The skin puckered around it, the puckering giving his face a quality of permanent skepticism, as if the world had asked him a question he had already answered.

He looked away. The column moved on. Their boots were loud on the stone. The rhythm was different from ours — heavier, more certain, the stride longer, set by men who had walked farther and fought longer.

Tomás stood beside me. He watched the column pass. His face was quiet. The round cheeks, the dirty jaw, the eyes that were quick to notice and slow to judge. His gaze moved over the Regulares, over the Legion, measuring, counting.

"At least they're on our side," he said.

The Regulares moved like water — fluid, silent, the eyes always moving. The Legion moved like fire — direct, fast, the bodies angled forward. We fought for God and king and the fueros and the red beret and the crucifix in the pocket. They fought because fighting was their craft.

---

The assault began at dawn.

I heard it. The noise was a wall, a wall of noise that rose from the south and west, hit the buildings. The buildings shook. The windows rattled. Dust fell from the ceiling. The machine guns opened — dozens, the bursts overlapping into a continuous roar, grinding now, metal feeding on metal. The artillery doubled. The ground shook. The cups on the floor of our position jumped and the water in the canteens rippled and the vibration was in the spine, in the skull, in the place behind the eyes where the noise becomes a pressure.

The requeté were in the reserve line. Two blocks back. We sat in the rooms of the ruined apartments and listened. The roar came through the walls, the floors, the ceiling. There was no direction to it. It was the city being torn.

Tomás sat against a wall. His rifle was across his knees. His hands were on the stock, the fingers loose, the grip ready. He listened. His head was tilted. The ear toward the noise. His lips were dry. He licked them once and the tongue left a wet line that dried in seconds. The dust was on his eyelashes. He did not blink it away.

"Big," he said again.

I nodded. The word was insufficient. It was total. The roar filled the space between the walls and the ceiling and the floor and the lungs. I breathed it. The air was thick with it — dust and cordite and the smell of something burning, something chemical, the smell of a city's insides opened to the air.

The assault went on. The morning passed. The roar rose and fell but held. The machine guns were the constant. A grinding, industrial rhythm, men feeding belts of ammunition into guns that spat them out at a rate the ear could not count. The artillery was the punctuation — the deep thump that arrived a second after the flash, the vibration in the ground, the dust falling. Between them, the rifles. Distant, individual, the crack of a single shot carrying through the roar, sharper than the crowd, more human.

I sat and listened. My hands were on my rifle. The stock was smooth. The barrel was cold. The hands were steady. The steadiness I had found in the trench after the village was still there — the numbness that I did not yet know was numbness, the body's quiet acceptance that this was the world now, that a city being torn was a sound a man could sit inside and eat his bread, drink his water, wait.

At midday the assault changed shape. The machine guns thinned. The artillery shifted, the shells falling further from us, the vibration receding. The rifle fire became sporadic. The grinding stopped. The absence of the wall returned individual sounds — a man coughing, a cup being set down, boots on the stairs.

Vidal came in. His face was the same. Flat, professional, the face of a man who has news and will deliver it without decoration.

"The assault failed," he said. "The Republic held. The International Brigades are in the city."

He said more. The sentence was in my ears and the sentence was a door. The assault failed. The words were simple. A fact, like the man in the blue shirt in the village, like the body changing direction, like the dust. Madrid held. The war continued.

I looked at Tomás. He was listening to Vidal. His face was calm. The same face from the olive grove, from the trench, from the road. He nodded, the face of a man who had heard a fact and placed it.

I did not know it was a door. The crusade, the quick victory, the king returning, the fueros restored, the Church triumphant — the narrative that had made the marching and the killing into a story with an end. Madrid held. The war was a siege now, and a siege has only duration.

The boy knew the assault had failed. The boy knew the war would continue. The boy knew the rifle was in his hands and the beret was on his head and the crucifix was in his pocket and Tomás was beside him. The sounds of the city filled the silence. He thought the silence was a pause.

---

The dead came in the afternoon.

They were carried on stretchers, on doors torn from hinges, on blankets held at the corners by four men. They came from the front lines through the communication trenches and into the streets behind the reserve line and they were laid on the ground in a row beside a building that had been a school. The building's wall was pockmarked with bullet holes. The windows were gone. The chalkboard was visible through one of the openings, the green surface covered with dust, the chalk tray empty.

I counted. The counting stopped itself. The row was long. The bodies were covered with blankets or coats or nothing. Some were in uniform — the Army uniform, the colonial brown, the Legion's open collar. Some were in civilian clothes — shirts, trousers, the clothes of men who had picked up a rifle and walked to the front and come back dead. A few were in the blue-gray of the International Brigades. The Republic's volunteers. The enemy. They were dead in the same row as our dead, the bodies touching, the blood mixing in the dust. A boot stuck out from under a blanket. The sole was worn through. The foot inside it was bare and dirty and still. Beside it, a hand reached from the edge of a coat, the fingers curled, the nails black. The hand had been reaching for something when it stopped.

Padre Joaquín came.

He walked down the row. His cassock was dirty. The blanket was over his shoulders. His small hands were folded over the prayer book. He walked with the measured step, the slight limp, the body arranged around its center. He stopped at the first body. He knelt. He made the sign of the cross. He said the words. The same blessing he had said over the rifles in the village, over the men before the assault, over the living and the willing and the armed. His voice carried. Warm. Measured. The cadences of thirty years.

"In nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus Sancti."

He stood. He moved to the next body. He knelt. The same cross. The same words. The same voice. The body under the blanket was different — the shape was different, the size, the blanket fell differently over the face and the feet. The blessing was the same. The body was different.

He moved down the row. Body after body. The hands making the cross. The voice saying the words. The knees bending and straightening. The cassock pooling in the dust. He blessed the Nationalist dead and the enemy dead and the Moroccan dead and the Legion dead. He blessed the man in uniform and the man in civilian clothes and the man in the blue-gray of the International Brigades. He blessed them all. His knees were leaving prints in the dust, two small craters that deepened with each body, and the cassock was wearing through at the knees and the fabric was thin and the skin beneath it was raw. He did not stop. His hands were steady. His voice carried over the rifle fire in the distance and the trucks in the street and the men talking in low voices by the wall. He blessed a man whose face was gone. He blessed a man whose hands were still raised as if reaching for something. He blessed them all the same.

I watched. Tomás stood beside me. His face was quiet. The eyes were on Padre Joaquín. The mouth was closed. The hands were in his pockets. He watched the priest bless the dead and he said nothing. The dead were dead. The priest was a priest. The blessing was a blessing. These were facts.

Padre Joaquín reached the end of the row. He stood. He was small. The cassock hung on him. The blanket was dark with sweat. His face was lined and tanned and the lines were deeper than they had been in Pamplona. He looked at the row of bodies. He looked at his hands. The hands were still. The prayer book was in the left hand. The right hand was open, the fingers spread, the palm toward the ground. The hand that had blessed. The hand that had touched the dead.

He put the hand down. He walked to the wall of the school building. He sat on the step. His hands stayed in his lap. His eyes were open. He sat and looked at the street and the dust and the men moving, the sky above the buildings, the smoke rising from the south.

I went to him. The feet took me. I stood in front of him. He looked up. His eyes were brown and the skin around them was tight, the tightness deeper than anything the sun could do. The bloodshot whites. The heaviness of the lids. His gaze passed through my face rather than settling on it.

"Father," I said.

"Hijo," he said.

I sat beside him. The step was warm from the sun. The dust was on my boots. The rifle was on my shoulder. The beret was on my head. We sat and the city breathed around us — the distant artillery, the sporadic rifle fire, the trucks in the streets, the men carrying stretchers, the smoke rising. Madrid. A capital. A body under bombardment. The scale of it was a thing the ear understood before the mind could hold it. The village had been a village. This was a country.

Tomás came after a while. He stood in front of us. He looked at Padre Joaquín. He looked at me. He reached into his pack and took out bread and held it out.

"Eat," he said.

I took the bread. Tomás gave bread to Padre Joaquín. The priest took it. He held it in his hands. He looked at the bread as a man looks at a thing he has seen ten thousand times and has never seen before. His fingers pressed into the crust and the crust crackled and the sound was small against the artillery. He broke a piece. He held it. He did not lift it to his mouth.

Tomás sat down. He ate his bread. He chewed steadily, the jaw working, the throat swallowing. He ate completely, committed, the body attending to the task. When he finished he wiped his hands on his trousers and took out a cigarette and lit it.

"The big priest," he said. "Blessing the big dead."

Padre Joaquín looked at him. The words hung. Tomás was noting it without judgment, without theology, the pragmatism of a man who has to walk in the weather he is given.

"The blessing does not change," Padre Joaquín said. The words barely reached past the step. His hands were still on the bread. "The blessing does not change."

Tomás nodded. He smoked. The smoke went up. The city breathed around him — the artillery pulsing, the dead in their row, the priest on the step. A truck passed in the street behind us, its headlights sweeping across the wall and gone. The engine faded. The artillery filled the space it left.

---

That night the artillery held. The noise was in the walls and the floor and the ceiling and the body. The vibration was in the spine. The machine guns fired in bursts — three, four, a pause, three, four, a pause — the rhythm of men at work, the cadence of a shift in a factory, the factory that turned living men into dead ones.

I lay on my back. The rifle beside me. The beret under my head. The crucifix in my hand. The wood was warm from the body. The groove under the thumb. The corpus under the fingers. The weight. The ceiling above me was cracked and a piece of plaster had fallen and lay on the floor beside my boot and the plaster was white against the dark floor and the white was the only clean thing in the room. Through the window frame — the glass long gone — the sky was orange at the edges from the fires in the south and the orange pulsed with the artillery and the pulse was a heartbeat in the sky, a rhythm that had replaced the stars.

Madrid came through the walls. The artillery was the heartbeat — low, steady, the pulse of a body larger than any I had known. The machine guns were the breath — quick, sharp, the rhythm of lungs working hard. The rifles were the voice — individual, human, the single shot that carried through the roar like a name through a crowd. Under them all, the city itself: the creak of buildings settling, the glass in the streets, the rubble shifting, a city being unmade and remade and unmade again.

My ears were learning. The body learns through the ears before the mind learns through the eyes. The ears said: this is a capital. The ears said: this will last. The ears said: the war you are hearing is a war that has a center and the center is here and the center holds.

I held the crucifix. The wood pressed into the palm. The corpus under the thumb. The prayer came. The Our Father. The words were there this time — they had been absent in the trench after the village, the mouth reaching for them and finding nothing. Here, in the dark of the ruined apartment, with the artillery in the walls, the dead in the row beside the school, Padre Joaquín on the step with the bread in his hands, the words came. Our Father, who art in heaven. Hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. The mouth moved. The breath carried each word into the dark. The dark received them. The artillery was above them and below them and around them and still the words came, one after another, the old order, the old sequence, the body remembering what the mind had lost and found and would lose again.

I said the words. The mouth formed them. The breath carried them. The ears heard them under the artillery, the machine guns, the noise of the city. The words were small. The noise was large. The gap between them was the gap between the crucifix and the rifle, between the beret and the helmet, between the boy who had marched from Pamplona and the man he was becoming.

Tomás was sleeping. His breathing was steady. The scapular was on his chest. His hands were folded over the rifle. He slept as he always slept — completely, the body committed to rest as it was committed to everything else. He had heard the assault fail and he had eaten bread and smoked a cigarette and blessed the dead with the word "big" and he had slept. The pragmatism of a man who walks in the weather he is given.

I held the crucifix. The artillery found its rhythm. Three rounds. A pause. Three rounds. The vibration in the floor. The dust settling. The dark holding everything — the noise, the body, the prayer.

I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The pen is in my hand. The hardware store is dark. The Calle Mayor is quiet.

I can hear Madrid now. At this table. The ears remember what the mind has stored in the body — dumped on the floor, all of it. The artillery in the teeth. The machine guns in the ribs. The rifles in the skull. The silence between, where the city breathed.
