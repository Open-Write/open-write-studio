# Chapter 8 — The Schoolteacher

---

The town had a school. A low building next to the church. One room. Stone walls, a wooden door, a sign above it in two languages: Ikastola / Escuela. The windows were shuttered. A padlock hung from the hasp, rusted, the chain looped twice through the metal loop bolted to the doorframe. The paint on the window frames was peeling, curling back in strips the color of old bone. The roof tiles were intact. Someone had swept the steps recently — the stone was clean, the broom marks still visible in the dust.

I had walked past it three times. Each morning, on my route through the town, I passed the door and looked at the padlock and the shuttered windows and the sign in two languages, and each morning I kept walking. The first morning, I noted it — the padlock, the shutters, the sign. The second morning, I slowed. The third morning, I stopped for a moment and read the sign again. Ikastola. Escuela. The Euskara word first. The Castilian second. On the fourth morning, I tried the door. I do not know why I stopped on the fourth morning and not the third. I do not know why I tried the door instead of walking past. I think it was the steps — the broom marks in the dust. Someone had swept them. Someone had come to a locked school and swept the steps.

The door was not locked. The padlock was decorative — the chain held it to the hasp but the hasp was bolted to soft stone and someone had worked the bolt loose. I pushed. The door opened on iron hinges that groaned and then held.

Tomás was behind me. He said nothing. He followed me inside. The air changed when the door closed behind us — cooler, still, the smell of chalk dust and old ink and the faint sweetness of children's sweat, the residue of small bodies that had sat at these desks through hot afternoons, their elbows on the wood, their pencils moving, their mouths shaping sounds in a language the world was about to forbid.

The room was large. Two windows faced the street, shutters closed, a thin line of light at each edge cutting across the floor in long pale rectangles. The blackboard was on the far wall, green slate, the chalk tray below it still holding a piece of white chalk and a piece of yellow. Above the blackboard, a crucifix. Wood. Dark varnish. The corpus tilted its head, eyes half-open, mouth slightly parted, the expression of a man about to speak who had not yet decided what to say. I knew that expression. I had seen it in every classroom and every church and every room where someone had hung a cross and expected it to watch.

The desks were overturned. Not broken — overturned. Someone had walked through the room and pushed them over, one by one, methodically, as a man clears a field. They lay on their sides, legs in the air, the writing surfaces facing the floor. Ink bottles had spilled. The ink had dried in black pools on the stone floor, cracked at the edges where it had dried fastest, the cracks spreading like veins. A child's slate lay under one desk, the writing surface scratched with arithmetic — numbers, a sum, the answer circled in a child's careful hand. 7 + 5 = 12. A pencil stub was beside it. The wood was chewed at the end, the teeth marks visible.

On the wall beside the blackboard, four tacks in the plaster where the Euskara alphabet chart had hung. The paper was gone — I had taken it days ago, folded it into my breast pocket, where it had stayed. Beside the empty space, a reading primer lay open, the pages splayed, the cover torn. A map of the Basque provinces was pinned to the wall — Bizkaia, Gipuzkoa, Araba, Nafarroa — the borders drawn in ink, the mountains sketched in brown, the rivers in blue. Someone had circled Tolosa in red.

Tomás stood by the door. His arms were crossed. His eyes moved over the overturned desks, the spilled ink, the empty space on the wall. He said nothing. His jaw was set. His hands were under his arms, pressed against his chest, as if he were cold, though the room was warm and the air smelled of chalk dust and old ink and something sweet, the residue of children who had sat at these desks and breathed this air and written on these surfaces with pencils chewed at the ends.

---

She was in the doorway.

I did not hear her come in. One moment the doorway was empty and the next she was standing in it, her hand on the frame, her eyes on the room. She was tall — taller than me by a finger's width. Dark hair pulled back from her face, the hairline straight, the parting clean. Brown eyes. She wore a dark skirt and a white blouse and her hands were at her sides, the fingers slightly spread, as a teacher stands when she is waiting for the room to settle. A Basque beret — a txapela — was tucked under her arm.

She looked at the overturned desks. She looked at the spilled ink. She looked at the chalk tray with its two pieces of chalk. She looked at the crucifix above the blackboard. She looked at my jacket — the chart's edge visible above the button, the paper softened from days in my pocket, the black letters and the red vowels and the corner of the child's flower.

She did not look at me.

"You found the door," she said.

Her voice was clear. Castilian, the accent of a Basque speaker — the vowels slightly flattened, the consonants precise, each word separated from the next as if she were choosing them one at a time. A teacher's voice. A voice that had spent years explaining things to children.

"I am Amaia Etxeberria," she said. "I taught here."

I had nothing to say. I said: "Martín. Requeté."

She nodded. She stepped into the room. Her shoes were flat, the soles thin, and they made a soft sound on the stone floor. She paused at the threshold, her eyes moving over the room like someone entering a house they once lived in — knowing where everything was, knowing everything had changed. She walked to the nearest desk and set it upright. The legs scraped on the stone. She set the next one upright. She moved through the room, desk by desk, her hands on the wood, her movements unhurried, as she must have arranged them at the start of each school day. Her fingers found the edges of the writing surfaces and tested them for damage. She righted a chair that had lost a leg and leaned it against the wall. She picked up a broken pencil from the floor and set it on a desk. She picked up the reading primer and closed it and laid it on the desk beside the pencil. She picked up the child's slate with the arithmetic and held it for a moment, her thumb on the circled answer, and then she set it upright on the chalk tray beside the white and yellow chalk.

When she reached the blackboard, she picked up the white chalk from the tray. She held it in her right hand, between thumb and forefinger, the other fingers curled. She looked at the green slate. She did not write anything.

"The children sat here," she said. "Twenty-three of them. Ages six to twelve. I taught them to read in Euskara and in Castilian. I taught them arithmetic. I taught them the catechism. I taught them to write their names. I taught them the names of the mountains."

She set the chalk down. She turned to face me. Her face was angular, serious. The skin was clear, the cheekbones high, the mouth a line. Her eyes were on mine.

"They closed the school in June," she said. "The order came from the military governor. Basque is not to be taught. The alphabet chart was to be removed. The crucifix stays. The crucifix is acceptable."

She paused. Her hands were at her sides again, the fingers slightly spread.

"Do you know what the fueros are?" she said.

I knew. I had known since I was old enough to carry the red beret to the Plaza del Castillo on the feast of San Fermín. The fueros. The ancient rights of the Basque-Navarrese people. Self-government. Tax exemption. The right to bear arms. The right to refuse conscription outside our own territory. My father had spoken of them at the dinner table, his voice rising, his hand on the wood, the way he spoke when the subject was sacred. My grandfather had fought for them. The requeté marched south to defend them.

"I know," I said.

"Do you know what you have taken?"

Her voice was steady. Clear, measured, the voice of a woman who had taught twenty-three children to read. She was stating. As a teacher states a fact to a student who has not yet understood it.

I reached inside my jacket and took out the chart. I unfolded it. The paper was creased, soft from days in my pocket. The letters were black, the vowels red. The child's flower in the margin, five petals and a stem. The same letters my grandmother had on her wall, in her kitchen, above the table where she kneaded bread. Her voice in my ear, shaping the sounds, patient, repeating them until my mouth could hold them. Aita. Ama. Etxe. The words for father, mother, house.

She watched me. Her face did not change.

She turned from me and walked to the wall where the chart had hung. The four tacks were still in the plaster. Only a torn strip of paper still clung to one tack, curling down like a page half-turned. She touched the tack with her forefinger. The nail was short, the finger calloused at the tip from years of holding chalk.

"This chart," she said. "I made it myself. I drew each letter. I painted the vowels in red so the children would see them. My father made one when I was a child. In Tolosa. Before the Republic, when teaching in Euskara was illegal. He made it in secret and hung it in the kitchen. My mother took it down when the Guardia Civil came through the town. She hid it under the mattress. When they left, she hung it again."

She turned back to me.

"We are Catholic," she said. "We are Basque. We had the fueros. You took them."

She said it clearly, each word placed beside the last. She was teaching me. She was telling me something I had not heard, or something I knew and had not yet been forced to hear from a mouth that was not my father's, in a room where twenty-three children had learned to read in a language that was now forbidden.

I looked at the crucifix above the blackboard. The corpus tilted its head. The same God who watched over the classroom in Pamplona where I had learned my letters, where my grandmother had pointed to the chart on the wall and shaped the sounds for me, patient, repeating them until my mouth could hold them. The wood darkened with age. The same half-open eyes.

Tomás was still at the door. His arms crossed, his eyes on Amaia. He did not speak. His jaw worked slightly, the muscles moving under the skin, as it did before a man said something he had been holding back. He did not say it. He looked at the floor.

Amaia spoke again. This time in Euskara.

The sounds came from her mouth and entered the room and I recognized them. The sounds, if not the words. I had last spoken Euskara as a child, before my grandmother died, before the language retreated to the margins of my memory and stayed there, folded, like the chart inside my jacket. I knew the sounds. The soft consonants. The vowels that opened and closed like hands. The rhythm of a sentence in Euskara — it moved forward and paused and moved forward again, like a man walking through hills, like water over stones. The tx and the tz and the ts, the sounds Castilian lacks, the sounds that live only in the valleys between the Pyrenees and the sea.

She was speaking to me. She had switched from Castilian to Euskara as if switching registers, as if she had decided that the language of instruction was no longer adequate and the language of the kitchen, of the grandmother, of the chart on the wall, was the one that belonged in this room. Her voice was different in Euskara — warmer, closer, the vowels longer. In Castilian she was a teacher. In Euskara she was something else. A woman speaking to a man who shared her language, or whose grandmother had, or whose bones remembered it even if his mouth had forgotten.

I caught words. Eskerrik asko — thank you. Zer — what. Zergatik — why. Nor — who. She was asking me a question in Euskara, in the language my grandmother had spoken, in the language of the women who taught their children the Hail Mary, and I could not answer because I had let the language go. I could not get it back. The sounds were in my ear but the words would not form in my mouth. My tongue sat heavy. My lips were sealed. I stood in a classroom in the Basque Country and a woman spoke to me in my grandmother's language and I could not answer. My hand went to my beret. I adjusted it. I dropped my hand.

She stopped. She looked at me. Her eyes held mine. In them I saw recognition. She had seen a man in a red beret who spoke Euskara, or whose grandmother had, and she had spoken to him in the language of the chart on the wall, and he had not been able to answer.

"Your grandmother," she said. In Castilian now. "She spoke Euskara."

"Yes."

"She taught you."

"Yes."

"And you are here. In a red beret. Fighting for the men who will ban it."

Her voice was still clear. Still measured. She was still teaching.

I had no answer for her. I said nothing.

She looked at me for a long time. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Her eyes held my face. Her hands were at her sides. Her breathing was steady. She was waiting for an answer I could not give.

Then she turned from me and walked to the door. Tomás stepped aside to let her pass. Her eyes were on the street. She walked through the doorway and into the sunlight and I heard her shoes on the stone and then I did not hear them anymore.

---

Tomás and I stood in the classroom. The light from the shuttered windows made lines on the floor. The overturned desks cast long shadows over ink pools cracked and dry. The crucifix watched.

"She's right," Tomás said.

I looked at him. He was still at the door, his arms crossed. His face was set, the jaw tight, the eyes narrow. His hands were under his arms, the knuckles pressed into his ribs.

"She's right," he said again. He did not look at me. He looked at the crucifix above the blackboard. "About the chart. About the school. About the language."

He uncrossed his arms. He put his hands in his pockets. He turned and walked out.

I stood alone in the room. The chart was still in my hand. I held it up to the light from the window. The letters were black, the vowels red. The child's flower was in pencil, five petals, a stem. The green crayon was bright. I folded it and put it back inside my jacket.

I walked to the wall where the map was pinned. The four provinces. Bizkaia, Gipuzkoa, Araba, Nafarroa. Pamplona was in Nafarroa — my province. Tolosa was in Gipuzkoa — her province. The border between them was a line drawn in ink. My finger was on the paper. The paper was on the wall. The room was empty and the chart was folded inside my jacket and the woman who made it was walking through the streets of a town that was no longer hers.

I looked at the crucifix. The corpus tilted its head. Its eyes were half-open. It watched the empty room with the expression it had watched the full one.

---

We found the soldier in the afternoon.

He was in a ditch at the edge of town, on the road that led north toward the mountains. A Basque Nationalist. His uniform was green-gray, the cloth torn at the shoulder, the trousers dark with blood from the hip down. He was young — twenty, maybe less. His face was pale, the skin around his eyes gray, his lips cracked and dry. His hands were on his stomach, pressing, the fingers red, the knuckles white. He was breathing in short gasps, each one a small sound, almost a word. His rifle was beside him in the mud, the stock cracked. His txapela, dark blue, had fallen beside his head. The wool was wet and matted.

Padre Joaquín reached him first. I was twenty paces behind. The padre knelt in the ditch, his cassock pooling in the mud, his hands on the soldier's face. The soldier's eyes were open. They moved to the padre's collar. They moved to the crucifix on the chain around his neck. His mouth opened. His lips moved.

"Padre," he said. His voice was thin, the breath shallow, each syllable a separate effort. "Confesión."

Padre Joaquín leaned close. His ear was near the soldier's mouth. The soldier spoke. I could not hear the words — only the rhythm of them, the rise and fall, the pauses where breath failed and was gathered again. His hand came off his stomach and found the padre's sleeve and held it. The fingers left red marks on the black cloth.

"Te absuelvo," Padre Joaquín said. His voice was low and steady, the voice of a man who had said these words in ditches and fields and hospitals and churches, who had said them to boys who were nineteen and men who were fifty, who had said them in the morning and at night and in the hours between when the light was gray and the world was neither dark nor bright. "In nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus Sancti."

His thumb moved on the soldier's forehead. The sign of the cross. The sign he had made over the requeté who died at the bridge. The same absolution. The same God who absolved the man who took the town and the man who lost it. I watched his hand. The thumb was small, the nail clean, the skin darkened by sun. It moved on the soldier's forehead with the precision of a man who has made that sign ten thousand times.

The soldier's eyes closed. His hand loosened on Padre Joaquín's sleeve. The red marks on the black cloth were the shape of fingers. His breath came once, twice, and then it did not come again. His hands slid off his stomach and lay in the mud, palms up, the fingers slowly uncurling.

Padre Joaquín stayed kneeling. His hand was still on the soldier's forehead. His eyes were closed. The mud soaked through his cassock at the knee. The road was quiet. A bird sang in the trees above the ditch — a blackbird, a song I had heard in the mountains above the town and in the Pyrenees above Pamplona, over the hardware store in the mornings when I was a boy and the world was a place where the king would come and the fueros would stand and the crucifix watched over rooms where children learned to read.

I stood on the road. Tomás stood beside me. Neither of us spoke. The bird sang again. The sun was on the mountains. The ditch smelled of mud and blood and the sweet green smell of crushed grass.

Padre Joaquín opened his eyes. He took his hand from the soldier's forehead. He stood. His cassock was heavy with mud. His hands were red. He looked at the soldier, then the road, then the mountains to the north, green and deep and quiet, the mountains that the soldier had been fighting toward when the bullet or the shrapnel or the piece of metal found him.

He did not speak. He walked past us, back toward the town. His limp was pronounced. His prayer book was in his hand, the leather cover dark with mud. His lips moved but no sound came. His eyes were on the road ahead of him, on the stones and the dust and the wheel ruts, on the ground he had walked for a year and would walk for a year more, carrying those words to the same kind of men in the same kind of ditch.

I looked at the soldier. His hands were open in the mud. His txapela was beside his head. His face was still. He looked like he was sleeping, except that sleeping men breathe.

---

That night I sat on the bench outside the billet. The stars were out. The mountains were black against the sky. A dog barked somewhere in the town, and another answered, and then silence. The river ran below, the water over stones, the same sound as the river in Pamplona, water moving south to the sea. A moth circled the lamp above the door. Its shadow moved on the wall, large and dark, circling.

Tomás came out and sat beside me. He was quiet for a long time. His hands were in his lap. His scapular was visible at his collar, the cloth frayed at the edge where his mother had sewn it, the stitching uneven, a woman's hand, not a tailor's, the thread pulled tight. He smelled of tobacco and soap. His hair was wet — he had washed at the pump, the water running down his neck, darkening his collar.

"The chart," he said. "You're keeping it."

"Yes."

He nodded. He looked at the stars. He looked at the mountains.

"She was a teacher," he said. "Before."

"Yes."

He was quiet. The dog barked again. The river ran.

"My mother taught me to read," he said. "In the kitchen. By the fire. She had a book — one book — and she held it on her lap and pointed to the words and I sounded them out. She was patient. She repeated them. Until I could hold them."

He put his hand on his scapular. His fingers moved on the cloth, the way a man moves his fingers on a rosary, bead by bead, the habit of touch.

"She made the scapular that year," he said. "She said it would keep me close to God. I was seven. I did not know what that meant. I know now she meant it would keep me close to her."

He was quiet for a long time. The moth circled the lamp. The river ran. The mountains were black and solid and did not move.

"We are here," he said. "And she is there. And her school is closed."

He stood. He went inside. The door closed behind him. I sat on the bench and listened to the river.

---

I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The hardware store is dark. The chart is in the drawer, folded once, the paper soft from handling, the ink faded where the sun hit it. The child's flower is there, in pencil, in the margin. Five petals. A stem.

I look at it and I hear her voice. Not the Castilian. The Euskara. The sounds entering the room, entering the ear, entering the place where the language lived before I let it go. Aita. Ama. Etxe. The words for father, mother, house. My grandmother's voice behind hers, shaping the sounds, patient, repeating them.

The pen moves. The boy moves with it. The woman stands in the doorway of the school. The desks are overturned. The chart is in his pocket. The crucifix remains.

Her hands are at her sides. Her fingers are slightly spread. She is waiting for the room to settle.
