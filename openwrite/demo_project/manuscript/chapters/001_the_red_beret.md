# The Red Beret

The inkwell is full. I filled it this morning before I sat down, as my father taught me: the funnel, the slow pour, the wipe of the rim with the rag. The rag is different. The funnel is different. But the table is his table, and the crack running through the wood from the left edge to just past the center is the crack my fingers found when I was six years old, tracing it while he wrote his orders for ironmongery to the warehouse in Barcelona. The crack is deeper now. The wood has dried. The table has been in this room since before I was born and it will be here after I am dead and the crack will be deeper still.

I am writing this in the back room of the hardware store on the Calle Mayor in Pamplona. The store is mine now. It has been mine since my father died in 1958, though I did not come back to it until 1962: the years between spent in a room I do not write about yet. The shelves hold what they always held. Bolts, hinges, screws in glass jars, twine on wooden spools, axes with ash handles, handsaw blades wrapped in oilcloth. The smell is iron and wood oil and the dust of plaster. When I breathe it in — when I lean over the table and the smell rises from the floorboards where decades of stock have shed their residue — the back room dissolves.

Not dissolves. The back room remains. I am here. The pen is in my hand. But the smell puts another room on top of this one — the front of the store, July 18, 1936, the morning the door opened and the runner came in and the world I had known since birth began its ending.

I was twenty-two. I had been up since five, as I was every morning, because my father believed that a man who slept past five did not respect the day. I had swept the front of the store. I had counted the stock — a habit he demanded, the inventory written in pencil on a sheet of paper pinned to the wall, each item in its column, the numbers exact. Bolts: forty-six. Hinges: twelve pair. Axes: three. The numbers were a kind of prayer. They said: the world has order. It can be counted. The count is honest.

My father was at the front counter, writing. He was always writing: orders, letters to the warehouse, letters to the Carlist junta, letters to men whose names I did not know. His handwriting was small and precise and slanted to the right and he held the pen far from the nib, the way a man holds a tool he has used for fifty years. His hands were large. Scarred across the knuckles from the store — a slipped chisel when he was thirty, a broken pane of glass when he was forty-five, the burn from a forge spark that left a white crescent on the back of his right hand. The hands did not match the writing. The hands were hardware hands. The writing was a clerk's.

The store smelled as it always smelled. Iron and oil and the dust. The morning light came through the front window and caught the dust motes and they turned in the air above the counter like something living. I was restocking the shelf of screws — glass jars, each labeled in my father's hand — when the door opened.

The runner was a boy. Seventeen, maybe eighteen. Sweating. His shirt was dark at the chest and under the arms. He wore a red beret pushed back on his head and he was breathing hard and he looked at my father and said, "Don Eusebio. The word has come. Mola has risen."

My father set down his pen.

That is what I remember first. Not the words — the pen being set down. The nib touching the paper and then lifting. My father's hand moving the pen to the side of the sheet, placing it parallel to the edge of the table, the way he always placed it. The deliberate steadiness of the gesture. As if the pen were a rifle being set in its rack. As if the act of placing it carefully were the first act of what was to come.

"When," my father said.

"This morning. The garrison. The garrison is with him."

My father looked at me. I was still holding the jar of screws — slotted brass, number eight, forty to the jar. My fingers were around the glass and the glass was cool and the runner was panting in the doorway and the dust motes were turning in the light and my father looked at me and I looked at him.

"Close the door," my father said to the runner. "Go to the next house."

The runner left. The door closed. The bell above it rang once and stopped.

My father stood. He was tall, taller than me by a head, and stooped, and when he stood from the counter his spine straightened like a tree in wind, not all at once but by degrees. He wore his dark jacket and his white shirt. He wore them every day. I do not remember a day he wore anything else.

"Put the jar down," he said.

I put the jar on the shelf.

"Come."

We walked through the back room — this room, the room where I sit now — and through the kitchen and into the dining room. The dining table was set for nothing. The cloth was on it because the cloth was always on it. The crucifix hung above the table on the wall. It was dark wood, walnut, my father said, though I do not know if he knew or if his father had told him and the telling had become knowledge. The corpus was hand-carved. The arms of the cross were slightly uneven, the left longer than the right, and the face of Christ was not serene but strained, the mouth open, the brow heavy. It had hung in three houses. My grandfather brought it back from the Third Carlist War — from the hills above Estella, where he had fought as a boy of nineteen and come home with a crucifix and a limp that never left him. My father grew up beneath it. I grew up beneath it. The wood was dark with age and with the smoke of candles.

My father reached up and took the crucifix from the wall. He held it in both hands. The base of the cross sat in his left palm. His right hand closed around the shaft. He held it as a man holds a tool, with the familiarity of long use. He looked at it. His thumbs moved across the wood. I watched his thumbs. They were broad and blunt and the nails were cut short and there was a line of black under them from the store — iron dust, ground in, permanent.

He held the crucifix out to me.

"Carry this," he said. "It has been in three wars."

I took it. The wood was warm from his hands. The weight was less than I expected — it was light enough to carry in a pocket, light enough to forget it was there, and heavy in a way that had nothing to do with its mass. I closed my hand around the shaft. My fingers overlapped where his had been.

Silence. He stood in the dining room with the empty space on the wall behind him and he looked at me and I looked at him and the silence was full of the things he held back. I know this now. I did not know it then. I thought the silence was strength. It was.

He reached for my head.

His hands were on my temples. He adjusted my beret — the red beret, the one I had worn on Sundays since I was sixteen, the wool soft from years of wearing, the color darkened by sweat and sun. He pulled it forward on the left side. He pressed the fold above my right ear. His fingers moved slowly, precisely, as his hands moved when fitting a hinge or setting a bolt: the sureness of a man who knew the object and knew the work and trusted his hands more than his words.

He stepped back.

That is what I carry from that room. Not the crucifix — I carry that too, in my pocket, in my hand, around my neck in the years that came after. But the step back. My father's hands on my head and then the step back. His arms at his sides. His face still. The empty space where the crucifix had been on the wall behind him.

We did not embrace. I want to be clear about this, because in the years since I have read accounts of men going to war and the accounts always include the embrace — the father holding the son, the tears, the words. We did not do this. It was not our way. The Carlist tradition does not embrace at the departure point. It prepares and equips and blesses.

Then I knew only that my father had given me the crucifix and adjusted my beret and I was ready. I was twenty-two and I was ready and the runner had said Mola has risen and the store was closed and the world was beginning and I walked out of the dining room and through the back room and through the store and out the front door into the street and the sun was on the stones of the Calle Mayor and the bells were ringing.

The bells. I want to say something about the bells. Every church in Pamplona was ringing. San Lorenzo, San Cernin, San Nicolás, the cathedral — I could barely tell which bells were which. They overlapped. They came in waves. The sound moved through the streets like water through a channel, filling the spaces, pressing against the walls, rising. The stones of the street vibrated. I could feel the vibration through the soles of my boots. The air was thick with it. I believed them. I believed every swing of every clapper in every tower in every church in the city.

The streets were full. Men in red berets. Dozens of them, then hundreds. They came from the side streets, from the houses on the Calle Mayor, from the alleys behind the cathedral, from the barrio de San Juan, from the Casco Viejo. They walked in twos and threes and some of them walked alone and they all wore the beret and they all moved in one direction: toward the cuartel, toward the Plaza del Castillo, toward the place where the battalion assembled. Their faces were the faces I knew. The butcher's son. The man who repaired shoes on the Calle Estafeta. The farmer from the valley who came to market on Saturdays. They were not strangers. They were Pamplona. They were Navarre. They were the red beret.

I walked with them. The crucifix was in my left hand. My right hand hung at my side. The beret was on my head and my father's hands were still on it — not literally, but the pressure remained, the place where his fingers had pressed the wool above my ear, and I carried that pressure as proof that something had happened, that the moment had weight, that the ordinary morning had become the morning that would not end.

The Plaza del Castillo was already full when I arrived. The red berets filled the square like water filling a basin: from the edges inward, filling the spaces between the lampposts, around the bandstand, along the facades of the cafés. The café tables were still out. The chairs were empty. The waiters stood in the doorways and watched. The sun was on the square and the stones were pale and the berets were red and the sound was the sound of hundreds of men standing in the same place, waiting for the same thing — the murmur of voices, the scrape of boots on stone, the creak of leather, the coughs, the clearing of throats. The sound of men assembling.

Padre Joaquín was on the steps of the bandstand. He wore his black cassock with the military blanket over his shoulders, the blanket he would carry for three years through every campaign. He was short and slight and his tonsure was visible through his thinning hair and his face was narrow and tanned and his hands were clasped in front of him and he was smiling. A narrow smile. A priest's smile, the smile that says: I see you. I know what you are doing. God knows what you are doing. It was the smile of a man who had spent twenty years in the confessional and who now stood before the largest congregation of his life, and the congregation stood rather than knelt, and prepared rather than prayed.

He raised his hands. The square quieted. The quiet spread from the bandstand outward, a circle of silence growing until it reached the edges of the plaza and the men at the back were still talking but the men in front were silent and the silence moved through the crowd as the bells had moved through the streets: a tide.

"Hijos," he said. Sons. Not soldiers. Sons. "The hour has come. The hour that your fathers prayed for. The hour that your grandfathers died for. The hour of Christ the King and of the king who will come."

His voice carried. The acoustics of the plaza helped; the facades of the buildings reflected the sound inward, held it, returned it.

"You go to restore what was taken. The faith. The fueros. The king. These are not abstractions. These are the walls of the house you live in. These are the stones of the street you walk on. These are the bones of your fathers. You go to put back what was broken. You go with God."

He blessed us. His hand moved through the air, the practiced gesture, the sign of the cross made large enough for the whole square. I watched his hand. It was small and the fingers were thin and the gesture was precise. The blessing fell on the red berets like rain on a field: each drop finding its place, each beret receiving its portion. I bowed my head. The beret pressed against my forehead. The crucifix was in my hand and the wood was warm and the blessing was on me and I believed.

I want to say that I believed and I want the saying to cost something — because in the years since I have learned to say it with distance, with irony, with the condescension of a man who knows what happened next. I will not do that. I believed. The belief was real. It was as real as the wood in my hand and the wool on my head and the stones under my feet and the bells in the air. I believed.

The assembly broke. Men moved toward the cuartel. Orders were shouted. Names were called. Lists were read. The square emptied into the streets and the streets emptied toward the barracks and I moved with the crowd, the crucifix in my hand, the beret on my head, the blessing still on me like a handprint.

I found my father at the edge of the plaza.

He was standing by the fountain — the fountain that has been there since before I was born, the stone basin dry in the summer heat. He stood apart from it. He wore his dark jacket and his white shirt and his hands were at his sides and he was watching. His face was the face I knew: long, lined, still. The face of a man who keeps his features still. The face of a man who has learned that the face is a thing to be controlled.

I stopped in front of him. The crowd moved around us. Men passed on both sides, their berets brushing my shoulders, their boots on the stones, their voices low and urgent. My father and I stood in the moving current and did not move.

I wanted him to say something. I wanted the words — the Carlist words, the words of the tradition, the words my grandfather had said to my father or had not said but that had been passed down through the silence and the objects and the crucifix above the table. I wanted him to say: the king will come. I wanted him to say: your grandfather would be proud. I wanted him to say: come home.

He said none of these things.

He looked at me. His eyes moved from my face to the beret and back. His hand came up — the right hand, the hand with the crescent burn — and he touched the beret again. The same adjustment. The same press above the ear. The same slow, precise movement of the fingers.

His hand dropped.

He stepped back.

He stood by the fountain and I stood before him and the space between us was the space of two men who did not have a language for what was happening and who had never had a language for it and who would never find one. I turned and walked toward the cuartel without looking back. Looking back was a weakness. The tradition does not look back. The tradition looks forward, toward the king, toward the fueros, toward the restoration of the house that was broken. I walked forward. The stones of the street were under my boots and the sun was on my neck and the crucifix was in my hand and the beret was on my head and my father's hands were still on it.

At the cuartel, the battalion was forming. Men lined up in the courtyard. Officers called rolls. Equipment was issued — rifles, ammunition, blankets, canteens. The courtyard was loud with the sound of organization — the clatter of metal on stone, the shouts of sergeants, the shuffle of men finding their places. I stood in line and received my rifle — a Mauser, heavy, the stock dark with oil — and I slung it over my shoulder and the weight settled against my back and the rifle was a fact. Not a symbol or an argument. A rifle.

A man came up beside me.

He was stocky. Broad across the shoulders. Dark hair that grew in every direction and was slightly too long, as if he had been called from something that did not involve mirrors. His face was round and open and the skin was dark from the sun and his hands were large — larger than mine, larger than my father's — and scarred across the palms from work I later learned was shepherding. A worn cord hung around his neck, the frayed end disappearing under his collar where a scapular would sit. He carried a pack that was already bulging and a blanket rolled tight and a canteen and a rifle and he was carrying all of it with the ease of a man who had been carrying things his whole life.

He looked at me. He grinned.

"I'm Tomás," he said. "I'm from Aoiz. I'll be beside you."

He said it like a man stating a fact about the weather. Not an offer. Not a promise. A fact. He would be beside me, the rifle on my back, the beret on my head, the crucifix in my hand. He said it and then he shifted his pack and settled his rifle and looked around the courtyard with the easy curiosity of a man who is interested in where he finds himself.

"You eat yet?" he asked.

I shook my head.

He reached into his pack and pulled out a piece of bread — round, flat, the crust dark. Navarrese bread. He tore it in half and gave me the larger half. He did this without asking and without ceremony, the way a man shares bread with a companion. The bread was good. Dense, slightly salt. I ate it standing in the courtyard of the cuartel with the rifle on my back and the crucifix in my pocket and the red beret on my head and Tomás Eguía beside me, eating his half, watching the men form up, his jaw working steadily, his eyes calm.

"First time away from home?" he asked.

"No."

"First time with a rifle?"

"Yes."

He nodded. "It's heavy at first. You get used to it." He took another bite. "My brothers said you get used to everything. I think they were lying about some of it. But the rifle part is true."

He had three older brothers. He told me their names — José María, Ignacio, Pedro — and that they were already with the battalion, somewhere in the forming column. He told me his father was a shepherd and his mother ran the smallholding and that he had been called from the sheep to the rifle and that the sheep would manage without him. He said this with the equanimity of a man who has never been the center of his own story and does not expect to be. He was the fourth son. The fourth son does not carry the weight of inheritance or the burden of expectation. The fourth son follows. He followed his brothers into the requeté as he had followed them up the hillside to the sheep: because they went and he was expected to go and the going was what you did.

We marched out of Pamplona in the late afternoon. The battalion moved through the southern gate and onto the road and the road was white with dust and the dust rose behind us and the men in front of me were silhouettes against the sky and the berets were dark against the light and the sound was the sound of five hundred men walking — the rhythm of boots, the creak of leather, the clink of metal, the breathing. The sun was on our left. The mountains were to the right. The road went south.

I looked back once.

I said I did not look back. I said looking back was not what we did. I was wrong. At the edge of the city, where the road curves and the walls of the old town give way to the fields, I turned my head. The walls were pale in the late light. The towers of the cathedral were dark against the sky. The bells had stopped. The city was quiet. I could see the rooftops, the line of the Calle Mayor, the gray stone of the buildings I had known since birth. I could not see my father. I could not see the store. The crucifix was in my pocket and the wall was bare.

I turned forward.

Tomás was beside me. His pack was high on his shoulders and his rifle was across his back and his stride was steady, the stride of a man who walks as he breathes, without thinking about it. He looked at me and said nothing and needed to say nothing. His presence was enough. His presence said: I am here. The bread in my stomach said: someone is beside you. The weight of the rifle was real. The wool of the beret was on my skull. The road was under my feet.

The boy is marching. I am writing this at the table and the inkwell is full and the pen is in my hand and the boy is marching south on a white road in the late sun and the beret is on his head and the crucifix is in his pocket and the man beside him is eating bread and the mountains are to the right and the dust is rising and the boy does not know.

He does not know that the stocky man beside him — the man who gave him the larger half of the bread, the man who said I'll be beside you — will die in his arms in a place he has not yet heard of, and will say words he will carry for the rest of his life, and will not finish the sentence.

The boy knows the road. The boy knows the beret. The boy knows the crucifix and the blessing. He knows that his father's hands are on his head and his grandfather's wood is in his hand and the tradition is a century old and the bells were ringing and the cause is just.

He is twenty-two. He is marching. The sun is on his neck.

I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The writing will be harder than the fighting, because the fighting required only the body and the writing requires the thing the body cannot provide: the willingness to look at what happened and call it what it was.

I am looking.
