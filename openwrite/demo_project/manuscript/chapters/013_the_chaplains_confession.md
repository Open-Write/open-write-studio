# Chapter 13 — The Chaplain's Confession

---

I am writing this in the back room of the hardware store. Inkwell full. I filled it this morning. Funnel, slow pour, my father's rag wiping the rim. A crack runs from the left edge of the table to just past center. My thumb rests in it. Pen in hand.

Padre Joaquín. Red beret. March south. Jarama. Unification. Northern campaign. Guernica. Teruel. Ridge. Tomás. His name was in all of it. Every step south. Every blessing. Every silence after.

January 1939. Barcelona fell on the twenty-sixth. News came by radio and through the officers' mess where Vidal stood with his map case and delivered the words in his flat voice. Men received them and set down their cups. A few nodded. None spoke. Machinery running since July 1936 was winding down. They could hear it. What would come after, none of them could say.

I was in a rear area behind the Ebro lines. Stone houses. A church with a roof, luxury after months without one. We had been pulled from the ridge in November — what was left of us. Sixty-three. Replacements had come, boys from Navarre, seventeen and eighteen, berets too new, boots too clean. They looked at us the way men look at something they refuse to become. I had looked at the veterans of Jarama like that in 1937.

Padre Joaquín was in the field hospital. That morning I walked there before dawn. Could not sleep. Had not slept well since the ridge. My body knew something the mind was still learning. That the war was ending. That what came after would need a different kind of endurance.

---

A farmhouse. Two stories, stone walls, tile roof patched with canvas where a shell had come through in November. Canvas flapping in wind from the north, from the Pyrenees. Wet stone and woodsmoke on the air. Carbolic underneath. Blood underneath that.

Gray light. Cold. Breath visible. Mud road, frozen on top, soft underneath. Boots broke through the crust and sank to the ankle, pulled free with a sound like cloth tearing. Farmhouse ahead, dark against a graying sky. One window lit. Smoke from a chimney, low, wind pressing it flat.

Olive trees along the road, bare in winter, branches black against gray sky. A dog barked somewhere far off. Sound carrying in cold air the way it does in mountains, bouncing off stone and coming back smaller. My breath hung in front of me, then faded. Boots broke through ice again.

I had walked past this farmhouse a hundred times since November. Never stopped. Field hospitals were for other men. Men who had taken shrapnel or caught a bullet or gone blind from a shell burst too close. Not for me. I had walked past and kept walking.

This morning I stopped. Door. Knock. Smell of iodine and old wool when it opened. An orderly looked at me, said nothing, pointed down a corridor with his chin.

His room was on the ground floor. Small. Iron bed against the wall, thin mattress, a table with a candle. Chair. Window facing a courtyard where ambulances parked and men came and went with stretchers. Slow, deliberate, carrying what they could. Beyond them, a wall. Beyond that, fields. Beyond that, mountains I could not see for cloud.

He sat on the bed. Not lying. Sitting. Feet on the floor, hands on his knees. Cassock with a military blanket over his shoulders, frayed, dark with old rain. Bare feet on stone. He had always gone barefoot in the field hospitals. I had never asked why. Some things a man carries for thirty years and never speaks.

He looked at me when I came in. Narrow face, narrower than I remembered. Deeper lines from nose to mouth, from mouth to jaw. Gray tan. Eyes open, seeing me, seeing somewhere else at once — a look I had seen on the ridge, on men in the holes too long. Body in one place, mind gone ahead where the body could not follow.

"Martín," he said.

Quiet voice. Close, almost whispered. His register for private rooms, for one listener. Not a question. My name stated, confirming a fact that had not changed while everything else had.

I sat in the chair. Wood creaked. I put my hands on my knees. He had his on his. Two men in a small room. Candle on the table. Canvas flapping above. Wind from the north.

A long time passed.

---

I will write about the quiet.

Between two men who had nothing left to say that the war had not already said for them. It held mortar rounds, machine guns, engines, carbolic, Tomás's weight against my chest. Only a silence that large could contain any of it.

I had been quiet since the ridge. Not choosing silence. Silence choosing me. Words had weight now, and weight had cost, and I had nothing left to spend. Men in the company talked, joked, cursed, complained. Sounds that passed for living. I listened and did not add to them. Joaquín was the first person in weeks I had spoken more than two words to.

His hands moved on his knees. A slight shift. Fingers opening, closing. Hands that had spent thirty years blessing. Berets, rifles, bullets, water, the dead. Hands that touched foreheads of dying men, held the chalice, signed the cross over bodies wrapped in blankets on blasted rock. On his knees. Still. Moving. Still again.

I watched them. Swollen knuckles. Cracked skin. Short nails bitten past the quick. A vein on the back of his left hand, blue, raised, pulsing faintly.

He noticed me looking. Did not move them. Let me look. A priest's hands are his instruments, and these instruments had been used hard. Thirty years of blessing. What remained was what I saw: cracked, swollen, still.

"Barcelona news," he said.

"Yes."

Canvas flapped. Candle flickered.

"It will be over soon."

"Yes."

Another pause. Longer. His breathing was slow, deliberate. A man choosing to breathe because his body had forgotten how on its own. I knew that breathing. Chest rising because it must. Lungs filling because they must. No thought in it. I had breathed like that on the ridge after Tomás died.

"I have been thinking," he said.

Quiet tone. An admission that thinking was unusual, that the war had made it a luxury the body could not afford.

"About blessings."

---

He did not look at me. He looked at his hands. Cracked skin, swollen knuckles. Reading a text written in his own palms.

"I blessed the bullets," he said.

Halting. Unfinished. Sentences breaking before they reached the period. I had heard it once before — a murmur in his tent, late at night, when he thought no one listened. I had walked past and kept walking. A man speaking to God or to himself, and the difference is a door that should not be opened.

"I blessed the bullets. I blessed the men who fired them."

His hands moved. Fingers opening. Fingers closing.

"I blessed the men who died from them."

A pause. Long. Candle flickered. Canvas flapped. Wind pressed against the window and glass rattled in its frame.

"I am not sure these are the same blessing."

---

I did not speak. Nothing to offer. My hand went to the crucifix in my pocket — my grandfather's, from above the dining table in Pamplona. Not to hold it up. Not to show it. My hand found it because it always finds it. Wood smooth. Worn by three hands across three generations. Man on the cross, arms out, head down.

I had carried it since I was fifteen. My father gave it to me the morning after my first Communion. Said: Your grandfather carried this in Morocco. His father carried it in Cuba. You will carry it wherever you go. He did not say why. He did not need to. A crucifix in the pocket is its own reason. I put it in my pocket that morning and it has not left since. Through school. Through the seminary I did not finish. Through the march south. Through Jarama. Through the ridge. Through this room. Through this table. Through this pen in my hand.

Padre Joaquín was speaking again.

---

"I told them God was with them," he said.

Quieter now. Almost inaudible. A man setting down a sentence he had carried for months, the way you set down a thing that might break.

"Before the attack. Before the crossing. Before the ridge." His hands were still. "I stood in front of them and said: God is with you. God sees your sacrifice. God knows your heart."

His breathing changed. Faster. Then slower. Then even.

"I was not sure God was with anyone."

His hands went flat on his knees. Pressed down. As if holding himself in place. As if without the pressure he might rise and float and not come back. He had said the unsayable. Now he sat with it. Room held it. Canvas held it. Wind carried it nowhere.

---

Room quiet. Canvas flapping. Wind pushing at the window. Candle guttered and held. I sat in the chair and he sat on the bed, a meter of air between us, and everything since July 1936 hung in that meter. Marching, fighting, dying. Blessings given and received, suspended between a priest's hand and a soldier's forehead, never landing.

I remembered Joaquín before the war. First time I saw him say Mass was in Pamplona, winter of 1935. San Lorenzo church. Packed. Men in berets, women with shawls, children fidgeting on the pews. He was not a large man even then. Voice carried without raising it. Clear. He spoke about mercy in a way that made the word sound like it was possible. After, outside in the cold, my father said: That man believes what he says. My father did not say that about many priests.

Before the march, Joaquín held a Mass for the requeté in Pamplona. Cathedral of Santa María. Full. Every pew. Men in red berets, sashes across their chests. Women clutching rosaries. Children sitting still for once. Joaquín at the altar. White vestments. Chalice raised. Voice carrying to every corner. He spoke about sacrifice and duty and God's will. Men wept. Not from fear. From certainty. From conviction that what they were doing was right and blessed and holy. I was among them. I wept. My father stood beside me, dry-eyed, jaw set. He had fought in Morocco. He did not weep. He had used up his tears somewhere in the Rif.

Six months later we marched south.

We marched out of Pamplona at dawn. Fog in the valley. Columns of men, red dots against white, stretching south along the road. Joaquín walked among us. Not at the front, not at the back. Among us. His cassock muddy by noon. His boots, he wore boots that day, not barefoot, were caked before Estella. He did not complain. He sang. A hymn I cannot remember. Men picked it up and carried it down the column until it faded into distance and fog.

I thought of the northern campaign. Padre Joaquín in the field outside Guernica — not during the bombing, days after, when the requeté had moved through and the dead filled the streets and churches burned unchecked. He knelt beside a body. Red sash, beret, scapular tangled in folded fingers. Mouth open. Flies on his lips. Joaquín gave him last rites. Same words he gave to the requeté. Same God.

I had stood ten meters away and watched. A door was being opened in him. I looked away.

He stayed kneeling for a long time. I counted his breaths. Twelve. Thirteen. He stood. Brushed dirt from his cassock. Walked to the next body. Knelt again. I followed at a distance, ten meters, never closer. He gave last rites to seven bodies that afternoon. I counted. Same words each time. Same hands on each forehead. His voice did not change. His hands did not shake. Mine shook for both of us.

He gave the blessing. He said the words. And that man died and the blessing went with him, as it had gone with Tomás on the ridge with his unfinished sentence and hands around nothing.

I did not speak. Quiet held.

---

Padre Joaquín looked up. Eyes found mine. Dark brown, ringed with red. A man who had slept little and seen much, looking at the one person in the room and seeing him fully.

"Martín. Your faith."

He stopped. Hands moved on his knees. Fingers opening, closing. Blessing habit, still in the muscles, still in the tendons.

"Your faith is not gone."

Close now. Quiet. Leaning in. Not a question. A thing he had observed in me without judgment, with the precision of a man who has spent thirty years reading souls.

"It is in a different place."

His eyes held mine. Candle flickered. Wind pressed canvas against the hole in the roof. Canvas held.

"I do not know where."

---

I am at the table. Inkwell full. Crack in the wood, left edge to just past center. Thumb in the crack. Pen in hand.

His words, set down in fragments, in pauses, in spaces between sentences where meaning lives. Bullets, blessings. Men who fired. Men who died. A blessing that was not a different blessing but had become one without changing its words. God present and absent. Faith not gone but elsewhere.

No answer to give. Crucifix in my pocket, wood smooth, weight small, held through cloth. Silence was the only honest thing.

What could I have said? That God was with us? I had believed that once. On the march south. In the Plaza del Castillo, morning we left, my father's hand on my shoulder, red beret on my head, grandfather's crucifix in my pocket. God was with us. King was with us. Fueros were with us. Catechism clear, sentences complete, words meaning what they meant.

I could have told him about that morning. Dawn. Fog lifting off the river. Hundreds of men, red berets like blood against fog. My father's hand on my shoulder. Heavier than usual. He knew what he was sending me into. He had been there. Morocco. Different country, different mud, different language. But a body dies the same in any country. He gripped my shoulder and said: Come back. Two words. Not God be with you. Not Viva Cristo Rey. Come back. That was his blessing. And I came back. Barely. But I came back.

On the ridge, words stopped meaning what they meant. Mortar came at six. Rounds walked east to west. Tomás lay in dust with his unfinished sentence and hands around nothing, and my crucifix was wood and nail. Dust and blood and Tomás's weight filled the space where God had been. That filling was permanent.

I held the crucifix. Held it as I held Tomás — with both hands, with my body around it. Holding changes nothing. Holding is all there is.

I knew this before Joaquín told me. Before the room. Before the quiet. My body knew it when Tomás died and I held him and did not let go until they pulled me away. My body knew it when I picked up the crucifix after the mortar and my hands were shaking and I could not stop them and the wood was the only solid thing in a world that had turned to dust. Holding is not faith. Holding is not doubt. Holding is what the hands do when the mind has nothing left.

On the ridge, after the mortar, I held Tomás for forty minutes. I know because someone told me later. Forty minutes. His weight changed as blood left him. Lighter. Then heavier as rigor set in. They had to pry my fingers from his jacket. My hands would not open. For three days after, my fingers curved in the shape of his collar. Holding leaves marks. On the hands. On everything.

---

We sat for a long time. Minutes. Perhaps an hour. Light changed. Early morning gray became late morning gray, clouds thinning, a sky deciding whether to clear. Candle burned lower. Wax pooled on the table. Canvas flapped, settled, flapped again.

His hands were still. Fingers interlaced, knuckles up — a man in prayer or a man who has stopped praying and does not know what to do with his hands. Eyes on the floor. Stone floor. Cracks between stones. Dust in the cracks.

Outside, a truck engine started. Stopped. Started again. Someone cursed. An orderly's voice, young, annoyed. Life continuing. Stretchers moving. Men being carried in and carried out. A war winding down does not stop for two men sitting in a room.

"I will say Mass tomorrow."

Different voice. Warm. Cadenced. He sounded like the man who stood in front of the requeté and said God is with you. His hands were on his knees and his fingers were not moving.

"I will say Mass and bless them and tell them God is with them."

He looked at me.

"And I will not know if I am telling the truth."

---

I am here again. Pen in hand. Crack in the wood. My thumb finds it.

I want to go back to that room. Sit in the chair. Feel wind from the north, hear canvas, see the candle gutter, hold the crucifix through cloth. Sit with Padre Joaquín in the quiet and not speak and not resolve anything. Because the quiet was the truest thing. Between two men who had lost what they carried, sitting was enough. Hands on knees. Breathing. Enough.

I was twenty-four. Sitting in a field hospital chair, crucifix in my pocket, a priest telling me the foundation had cracked. I shifted. Opened my mouth. Closed it. Catechism had no answer for a blessing that had become something its words no longer contained. Catechism had an answer for everything else.

I sat. Held the crucifix. Looked at Padre Joaquín, he looked at me. Room quiet. Wind from the north. Canvas flapping. Candle burning low. War ending. Faith in a different place. Neither of us knowing where.

It was enough. Not for theology. Not for catechism. Not for a bishop's letter or a general's speech. Enough for two men in a room with a candle and canvas flapping above and wind from the north. Enough for that morning. Enough for that war.

---

Before I left, I stood. Legs stiff. Chair had been hard. My father's chair in the hardware store is hard. Padre Joaquín had taught me that. A hard bench is an honest bench, the body should not be comfortable when it is speaking to God. He had said it about the prie-dieu, the kneeling bench. Body should feel wood and stone and weight. Comfort is a lie.

He believed that. Believed it with his body, with his habits, with his hands on his knees. Belief lived in his muscles, in how he stood barefoot on cold stone, in how he held a dying man's hand without flinching from the grip. What his mind believed, I could not say. His body believed enough.

I stood and he stood. Shorter than me. Slight man. Cassock hung on him. Blanket over his shoulders. Bare feet on stone. Face level with my chest. A man who had looked up at soldiers for three years. In trenches. In hospitals. At graves.

He reached out and took my hand. Small. Dry, cracked skin. Firm grip. He had held dying men's hands, living men's hands, frightened men's, faithful men's. He held mine and looked at me. Eyes clear.

"Thank you for coming."

"I will come again."

He nodded. Let go. Sat back on the bed. Hands on his knees. Fingers opening, closing. Blessing habit. Muscles remembering what the mind was forgetting.

I walked to the door. Stopped. Turned.

"Padre."

He looked at me.

"Tomorrow's blessing. It is enough that you give it. They need your hand and your words. They do not need you to know."

Where it came from, I cannot say. Not the catechism. Something from the ridge, from dust, from Tomás's weight against my chest. From quiet. From sitting. From holding a crucifix in a pocket while a priest told me the foundation had cracked.

He looked at me for a long moment. Eyes wet. He blinked. Once. Slowly.

"Yes," he said. "Perhaps."

---

I walked back to the village. Mud road. Sky clearing. Late morning light in Catalonia, pale, thin, a sky that had been gray for weeks deciding to open. Wind still from the north. Pyrenees behind clouds. Somewhere beyond them, Pamplona. Hardware store. Table. Inkwell. Crack in the wood.

Birds. I heard birds. Could not see them. Behind cloud, behind gray, behind weight of a sky pressing down on Catalonia. They sang anyway. Small voices. Three notes, repeating. I stood in the mud and listened. Could not name the species. Did not need to. A bird singing in January while the Republic collapsed. It did not know. It sang.

Crucifix in my pocket. Wood smooth. Weight small. Held through cloth, held as Tomás held his scapular — without thinking, without theology.

War ending. Barcelona had fallen. Republic collapsing. Requeté waiting. Replacements cleaning rifles. Officers reading maps. Machinery winding down, silence coming.

I walked. Mud thick. Boots making a sound no one heard. Village. Stone houses. Church with a roof. Barracks. Bed. Lying down. Ceiling. Body settling into the mattress, remembering the ridge, the hole in limestone, Tomás's weight, hands on knees in a small room, a voice saying *I am not sure these are the same blessing*.

Room. Candle. Canvas. Wind from the north. Hands on knees. A voice saying *your faith is not gone* and *it is in a different place*.

I held the crucifix. Held it as I hold it now, here, at the table. Wood smooth. Weight small. Man on the cross, arms out, head down. Body enduring. Enduring until the pocket wears through and the wood falls to the floor and a hand not yet formed picks it up.

Pen moves. Boy moves with it. Distance collapses. I am in the room. I am at the table. Holding the crucifix. Twenty-four. Fifty-six. Listening to a priest tell me the foundation has cracked. Telling him the blessing is enough. I said it. Saying is what remains.
