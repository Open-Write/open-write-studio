# Chapter 14 — The End

---

I am writing this at the table. Inkwell half-full. The crack in the wood runs left to center and my thumb finds it without looking, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. Pen in hand. The nib is new, the third this year, and the ink is the cheap kind that fades if you leave the page in the sun. Afternoon light through the small window, the one that faces the alley and not the street. My father chose this room for the same reason he chose the hard chair: work that matters should not face a window worth looking through.

March 1939. I was twenty-four. Aragon. A garrison town whose name I have written before and will write again — stone walls, tile roofs, a plaza with a fountain that had not worked since August. We had been there since January, pulled from the Ebro and sent west to wait. The war was a map on the officers' table, and the map was shrinking. Every day the line moved south and east, compressing. Barcelona had fallen. The Republic was a corridor, then a strip, then a rumor.

We waited. Men cleaned rifles that did not need cleaning. Played cards on ammunition crates. Wrote letters home and did not send them because the post had stopped running in February. The replacements, boys, seventeen, eighteen, berets stiff with new dye, stood at the edge of the courtyard and looked south and said nothing. They had come to fight. The fighting was finishing without them.

Vidal had maps spread on the mess hall table. The maps showed the Republic shrinking, the coastline from Tarragona to Valencia, a strip of Castellón, a pocket around Madrid that had become a mouth swallowing the capital. He moved his finger along the lines and said little. He did not need to say much. The maps said it. His face said it. The face of a professional soldier watching a war end the way wars end — in surrenders, retreats, radio announcements that grew longer as the territory shrank.

Padre Joaquín had stopped saying Mass. Since February. He blessed the men in the mornings — a hand on the head, a murmured word — but the altar in the church with a roof stayed bare. I had not asked him why. I knew why. The blessing was enough. The Mass required a certainty he no longer carried. Or perhaps the Mass required a congregation that still knew what it was asking for, and the men in the courtyard were not that congregation anymore.

I sat on the stone bench by the fountain and held the crucifix. Holding. The wood was smooth from three wars, my grandfather's, my father's, mine. The man on the cross, arms out, head down, his body enduring what the nails asked of it. I held him through the cloth of my pocket. I did not take him out. Taking him out would have been a statement. Holding him was enough.

---

The radio was in the mess hall. A wooden box with a cloth grille and a dial that whined when you turned it, picking up static and fragments and the voices of men broadcasting from cities we had fought toward for three years. We gathered around it because there was nowhere else to stand.

I was outside. By the wall. The mess hall door was open and the radio's voice came through it, tinny and flat, a voice travelling through wire, speaker, distance. The words were official. Franco had entered Madrid. The war was over. Victory.

Inside, the cheering started. A few voices first, sharp, disbelieving, men who had waited and did not trust the arrival. Then more. Then all of them. The sound filled the mess hall and came through the door and hit the courtyard wall and came back. Men were embracing. I could hear it — the slap of hands on backs, bodies colliding, the sounds men make when the blood rises faster than the mind can follow.

I stayed by the wall. Stone at my back. Afternoon sun on my face, warm for March, the kind of Aragonese spring that comes early and stays late and makes the fields smell like dust and thyme. I could hear the men inside. I could hear the radio still talking, the announcer's voice underneath the cheering, going on about the Caudillo and the victory and the new Spain. No one was listening anymore. They had what they needed. The war was over.

I held the crucifix. Smooth wood. The man on the cross. Arms out, head down.

A few men came outside. Eyes bright. Faces I had known for three years — since the march south, since Jarama, since the unification. Some of them had been Carlists. Now they were Nationalists, or Spaniards, or whatever the new word was. They grabbed my arm and shook it. One of them, I will not name him, he was young, he meant it, said, "We did it, Martín." I nodded. He went back inside.

I did not cheer. I did not weep. I stood by the wall and held the crucifix and listened to the sound of men celebrating a victory I had waited for and a victory I had not wanted, and for the first time those were the same thing and it changed nothing.

---

Evening came. Light going gold, then orange, then gray. The men had moved from the mess hall to the courtyard. Someone had found wine and they passed bottles and cups and drank and sang. Carlist songs first, the old ones about the king and the fueros. Then the songs shifted, Nationalist, Falangist. The yoke-and-arrows songs that Salazar had taught them. The men did not notice the shift. Or they noticed and did not care. The wine was warm and the war was over.

A bonfire in the courtyard. Someone had broken up an ammunition crate and the wood burned green and fast and the sparks went up into the dark. Men sat around it on stones, crates, the bare ground. Faces lit from below. Shadows long. The fire made them look older or younger — the light was not honest, it gave and took in equal measure. A man I had served with at the Ebro was crying. Sitting with tears on his face and a cup in his hand and his mouth open and no sound coming. Another man had his arm around him and was singing and did not notice. The fire cracked and spat and the sparks rose and went out, new ones taking their place. Celebration and something unnamed sat together that night.

I sat on the bench by the dead fountain. Crucifix in my pocket. Hands on my knees. The stone was cold through my trousers. Above, the sky was clear, stars coming out, the first ones faint, the bright ones not yet visible. Orion. The same stars over Pamplona, over the ridge, over the Ebro. The stars did not care about the war. They had been there before it and would be there after and their indifference was, in that moment, the only honest thing I could find.

Footsteps on stone. Boot heels. Deliberate. The walk of a man who has somewhere to be and does not hurry to get there.

Salazar.

He sat on the bench beside me. Not close. A man's width between us. He wore the blue shirt with the officer's jacket, the hybrid uniform of the unified party. His hair was combed. His hands were clean. His fingernails were clean. He had not fought. He had organized. He had integrated. He had carried the decree from battalion to battalion and made the Carlists wear the yoke-and-arrows and called it unity. He had done his job. He had done it well.

For a while neither of us spoke. The singing continued in the courtyard. A bottle broke somewhere. Laughter. The radio was still on inside the mess hall, still talking, a different voice now, someone reading a proclamation. The words were too far away to hear.

"We built a new Spain," Salazar said.

He did not look at me. He looked at the courtyard, at the men, at the stars. His voice was quiet. This was the voice I had heard once before, late at night, after the integration sessions had failed and the men had gone to bed and Salazar sat alone in the mess with José Antonio's collected speeches open in front of him and his hands flat on the table.

I said nothing.

He waited. The singing shifted. A different verse. The same melody.

"José Antonio said we would build a new Spain," Salazar said. "He did not say the new Spain would look like this."

I looked at him. He was still looking at the courtyard. His face was still. Something beyond expression — the face of a man who has delivered a parcel to the wrong address and cannot get it back.

He stood. Straightened his jacket. Buttoned the middle button. A Falangist habit — the body must be ordered even when the mind is not.

"Goodnight, Martín."

He walked away. Boot heels on stone. Deliberate. The walk of a man who has somewhere to be. I watched him go. He turned the corner. He was gone.

---

The crucifix was in my hand. I had taken it out without deciding to. The body does things the mind has not sanctioned. Wood smooth. Weight small. The man on the cross, arms out, head down. My grandfather had held him in the Third Carlist War. My father had held him in the twenties. I had held him at Jarama and on the ridge and in the hospital chair while Padre Joaquín told me the foundation had cracked.

I held him and sat on the bench and listened to the men sing. The songs were louder now. Drunker. Some of the words were wrong, men singing verses they had forgotten and replacing them with sounds that fit the meter. It did not matter. The war was over. The songs did not need to be right. They needed to be sung.

I thought about Tomás. Not on purpose. The body remembers a wound when the weight shifts. Tomás on the ridge. Tomás's weight against my chest. Tomás's unfinished sentence — *Tell my mother*, the words I would carry to Aoiz and deliver to a woman who sewed scapulars and sent her son to a war that did not need him and killed him on a ridge in Catalonia for ground the maps would not remember. I had carried those words for two years. They had not grown lighter.

He would have cheered. Tomás would have been in the mess hall with a cup of wine in his hand and his beret pushed back on his head and his arm around whoever was nearest. He would have sung. He would have said, "We did it, Martín," and meant it, and meant all of it, the war, the crusade, the king, the fueros, the return home. He would have meant it because Tomás meant everything he said. That was the thing about him. That was the thing I could not replace.

I held the crucifix. I did not pray. I did not speak. I sat on the bench by the dead fountain and listened to men sing songs that were no longer Carlist songs and looked at stars that had been there before the war and would be there after and knew that knowing changed nothing.

---

Morning. The next day, or the day after that. Time had loosened. The celebrations had gone on through the night and into the morning and now the men were sleeping it off in the barracks and the courtyard and the mess hall. Bottles on the ground. Cigarette ends. The smell of wine and sweat and the heaviness of men who had waited and now had it.

I was in the barracks. Sitting on my bunk. The barracks was a long room with two rows of iron beds and thin mattresses and footlockers at the end of each. The window faced east. Morning light came through it and fell on the floor in a long rectangle that moved as the sun rose. I watched it move.

My pack was on the floor. Open. I had not packed anything. There was no order to pack. But the men were packing. Slowly, without urgency. They rolled blankets and stuffed mess kits into canvas and strapped boots to the outside of packs where they would swing and clank on the march.

I watched them. From the bunk. Crucifix in my hand. The wood was warm from my palm.

Near the door, a man from Pamplona, older, thirty, a carpenter before the war, was rolling his blanket with the care he had once given to wood. Tight roll. Edges aligned. He strapped it and set it beside his pack and reached for his beret. He held it for a moment. Turned it in his hands — a man with a tool he has used every day for three years and will not use again. He ran his thumb along the edge. Then he folded it and put it in the pack and took out the cap and put it on his head. He did not look at anyone. He buckled the pack and moved to the next thing.

A man across from me, I will not name him, he was one of the replacements, a boy from Estella, eighteen, face still smooth, was folding his beret. I watched him do it. He held it in both hands. The red wool holding the shape of his head after three years of wearing. He folded it once, then again, then pressed it flat and slid it into his pack, between a rolled blanket and a spare shirt. He closed the pack. He buckled the straps.

On his head, he wore a cap. A cap with a badge — the yoke-and-arrows. The Falangist symbol. The symbol of the unified party. The symbol that had been pinned onto the berets after the unification decree and that now, on this morning, was the only symbol that mattered.

I looked around the barracks. Men packing. Berets going into packs. Caps with yoke-and-arrows staying on heads. The red wool disappearing into canvas, between blankets, under shirts. One by one. Man by man. A fact. The beret was being put away. The party was staying.

I held the crucifix. I did not pack. I sat on the bunk and watched the berets go into the packs and the caps stay on the heads and the morning light move across the floor and I knew, in the chest, in the hands, that the crusade was over and the thing that replaced it was not what we had fought for and that this was the end.

---

Later. Or earlier. The sun was higher. I was in the courtyard. The fountain had been dry since August and someone had thrown a cigarette end into the basin and it lay there on the dry stone, small and white and finished.

I had my beret in my hand. I had not packed it. I had not put it on. I held it the way I held the crucifix, just holding. The red wool. The shape of my head in it, worn from three years of sun, rain, sweat, dust. The yoke-and-arrows badge was pinned on the left side — where the Carlist star had been. Where the star had been, the wool was darker, the sun had faded around it for three years and the star had kept its patch shielded. A ghost. A square of darker red. I ran my thumb over it. The wool was raised there, the fibers standing up where the star's edges had pressed them down. I could read the shape of it with my fingers. Six points. The Carlist star. Gone. The shape remaining.

I folded the beret. Once. Twice. Pressed it flat. As the boy from Estella had done it. The way my father had done it when I was a child and watched him put his beret away after the campaigns of the twenties, folding it carefully, pressing the creases, sliding it onto the hook in the hallway where it hung until the next war.

I opened my pack. I put the beret inside. Between the blanket and the spare shirt. I closed the pack. I buckled the straps.

The cap stayed on my head. Yoke-and-arrows. The party.

---

I am here again. Pen in hand. Crack in the wood. Thumb in the crack. Afternoon light through the small window. The alley. The sound of a cart on the street, someone delivering goods to the shop next door. A dog barking. The ordinary sounds of a city that has been at peace for seventeen years and has forgotten what the waiting sounded like.

I want to go back to the courtyard. Stand there. Hold the beret. Watch the men pack. Watch the red wool disappear into canvas. Stand in the dry fountain's shadow and listen to the silence that came after the singing stopped, the absence of the thing that had been there for three years. I cannot go back. I know that. The pen knows it. The table knows it. But the hand that holds the pen was the hand that held the beret, and sometimes the hand does not know the difference.

The crusade. God, king, fueros. Three words. A century of men carrying them south to fight and die and carry them home again. And the war ended and the words were gone, just gone, the way a word goes when no one speaks it anymore. The king did not come. The fueros were abolished. God remained, but God was in a different place, the place Padre Joaquín had told me about, the place the body knows and the mind cannot name.

The pen moves. The boy moves with it. I am twenty-four in the courtyard, beret in hand. I am fifty-six at the table, pen in hand. The same hands. Separated by thirty-two years and a war and a peace and the thing that came after the peace — the slow settling of dust after a building falls.

The beret went into the pack. That is what happened. That is what I saw. The red wool folded and pressed and slid between blankets and shirts, buckled in. The cap stayed on the head. The yoke-and-arrows stayed on the cap. The party stayed. The tradition went into the dark.

I held the crucifix. I held it as I hold it now. Wood smooth. Weight small. Man on the cross, arms out, head down. Body enduring what was asked of it — enduring until the pocket wears through and the wood falls to the floor and a hand not yet formed picks it up and does not know what it holds.

The war ended. We had won. I stood in the courtyard with an empty hand where the beret had been, a cap on my head I had not chosen, a crucifix in my pocket.

Nothing else.
