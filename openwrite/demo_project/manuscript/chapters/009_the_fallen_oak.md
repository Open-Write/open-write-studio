# Chapter 9 — The Fallen Oak

---

The rain started in August and did not stop.

I can hear it now. Here, at this table, in the back room of the hardware store, the pen moves and the ear remembers what the hand cannot hold — the sound of rain on stone, on canvas, the metal of a helmet pushed forward on the brow. The mountains of Cantabria were not the mountains of Navarre. Navarre's mountains are green and close, the valleys narrow, the passes known. These mountains were higher, steeper, the ridgelines lost in cloud, the paths running with water that carved channels in the red clay and filled the ruts and turned every road into a river. The rain came from the north, off the Bay of Biscay, carrying the salt of the sea and the cold of the Atlantic. It fell on the requeté for weeks without stopping.

I was twenty-three. My boots were not waterproof. The leather had been good when I left Pamplona — my father's leather, from the store, oiled and stitched by a man who knew the trade. A year of marching had worn through the soles. The stitching at the left heel had split in June. I had tied it with wire. The wire had rusted. The rust had stained the leather orange. My socks were wet in the morning and wet at night. The skin of my feet was white and soft, peeling, the blisters forming and bursting, the body building its armor one layer of damaged skin at a time.

Tomás walked beside me. He had found a piece of oiled cloth somewhere — a tarpaulin from a supply truck, cut with his knife — and he wore it over his shoulders like a cape, the edges dripping, his red beret dark with water, a brass yoke-and-arrows pin on the band. His boots were worse than mine. He had wrapped his feet in rags. The rags were soaked by midmorning. He said: "The feet will dry when the rain stops." The rain did not stop.

We moved through the mountains in columns of four. The road was a track cut into the hillside, narrow, the drop on the left side steep and long, the trees above us dripping, the branches heavy, the leaves turning. August. The leaves should have been green. They were already yellow at the edges, the color spreading inward, as if the rain had carried autumn forward by weeks. The column moved slowly. The men were silent. The sound was boots on wet stone, the creak of leather, a canteen clanking against a rifle stock — and the rain. Always the rain. It filled the ears. It ran down the neck. It soaked through the collar and down the spine and into the waistband. It collected in the creases of the skin, in the folds of the cloth, in the spaces between the toes where the skin was white and soft and breaking down. The rain reached every part of the body.

I carried the crucifix in my breast pocket. The wood was dark from the moisture. The corpus pressed against my chest with each step, the weight slight but present, the way a man carries something he has been carrying so long the body expects it. I did not take it out. I felt it there, against the ribs, and I walked.

Padre Joaquín was ahead of me in the column. His cassock was heavy with water. He had abandoned the military blanket over his shoulders — it had become too heavy when wet, pulling him sideways — and given it to a man whose blanket had been lost in a river crossing. He walked with his head down, his limp more pronounced, his prayer book in the inner pocket of his cassock where the leather cover darkened and softened with each day of rain. His lips moved. I could see them from behind — the slight movement, the whisper, the words lost in the sound of water. He was praying. Or blessing. Or saying the words he had said for a year to men who were alive and men who were dead, the words running through him like the rain ran through the mountains, constant, wearing the stone.

---

The losses started in September.

Not the losses of battle — those had been coming since Madrid, since the first man I killed fell in the road outside that village with his arms spread and his face in the dirt. Those losses were particular. They had names. They had faces. They were the man beside you, the man whose tobacco you borrowed, the man who snored in the billet and kept you awake and whose snoring you would have given anything to hear again.

The losses in the mountains were different. They came from the terrain. A man slipped on a wet path and fell and the drop was three hundred feet, the body not recovered. They sent two men down with rope. They came back with the rope still coiled and their faces gray. A man crossed a river in flood and the current took him, and he was found downstream two days later, his face blue, his fingers still curled around the strap of his pack. A man caught a fever from the wet and the cold, the exhaustion. He lay in a field hospital in a farmhouse, the ceiling low, the walls sweating with damp, the blankets too thin. He died in the night. His boots were taken for another man whose boots had worn through. The mountains took men the way the mountains took water — slowly, steadily, without drama, the loss so constant it became part of the landscape. I stopped learning their names. That was the worst of it. In Madrid I had known every man in the platoon. In the mountains the replacements came and the replacements went and the column was the same size or smaller and the faces changed and the feet kept moving.

Tomás said nothing about the losses. He walked beside me. He adjusted his oiled cloth. He wrapped his feet in fresh rags when he could find them. He ate what was given. He slept where he could. He avoided the cause, the king, the fueros. He talked about food. He talked about sleep. He said: "When we get to the coast, I'm going to swim in the sea." He said: "My mother makes a stew with peppers and lamb. When I get home I'm going to eat the whole pot." He said these things. I heard them. The words were small and warm, the only warmth in the mountains.

The column moved south through Gipuzkoa and into territory held by the Basque Nationalists. The towns were empty. The people had fled or they had stayed, standing in doorways, watching the requeté pass, their faces the faces of the people in the town where Amaia's school was — silent, closed, the eyes following without moving the head. I did not look at them. I looked at the road, the boots of the man in front of me, the red beret dark with rain, the wool heavy.

In one town — I do not remember the name — the church was intact. The door was open. I went in. The air was cold and still, the smell of damp stone and candle wax, the residue of incense burned days or weeks before. The crucifix above the altar was large, carved from wood, the corpus painted, the blood red and the skin pale. The same God. The same cross. The same half-open eyes. I stood in the nave. The rain on the roof. The water in the gutters outside. The silence of a church where no one was praying.

Padre Joaquín came in behind me. He stood at the entrance, his hand on the doorframe, his eyes adjusting to the dark. He walked to the altar. He knelt. His cassock pooled on the stone floor, the hem dark with mud. He made the sign of the cross. He stayed kneeling for a long time. His lips moved. His hands were clasped. His eyes were closed.

When he stood, he turned to me. His face was thinner than it had been in Pamplona. The lines around his mouth were deeper. His eyes were set back in his skull, the skin around them gray.

"The town is empty," he said.

"Yes."

"They left before we arrived."

"Yes."

He looked at the crucifix. He looked at the floor. His hands were at his sides. His fingers moved — the slight, constant motion of a man whose hands were accustomed to blessing and were now still.

"I said Mass this morning," he said. "For the men. In the rain. Under a tarpaulin."

"I know. I was there."

"The homily was short."

I said nothing. The homilies had been getting shorter since June. In Pamplona, Padre Joaquín's homilies had lasted ten minutes, sometimes fifteen. He spoke about the crusade, the king, the fueros and the faith and the century of sacrifice. He spoke with the voice of a man who believed every word. Now the homilies were three minutes. Sometimes two. "God is with you. Go with God." The men did not notice. They were tired. They were wet. A short homily was a mercy. I noticed.

He walked past me and out of the church. His limp left prints in the mud on the stone floor. I stood in the empty nave and listened to the rain. The candle on the altar had burned to a stub. The wax had run down the holder and pooled on the stone and hardened in white rivulets. The crucifix watched. The half-open eyes watched the empty pews, the mud prints, the water running in the gutters outside. I made the sign of the cross. The body does it. The body remembers what the mind has started to question.

---

The letter from my father arrived in the last week of September.

I was sitting on a stone wall at the edge of a village — a handful of houses, a church, a barn where the requeté were billeted. The rain had stopped for an hour. The clouds were low and gray, the mountains hidden. Tomás was beside me, his oiled cloth folded on his lap, his hands working a piece of bread into smaller pieces. A supply truck had reached the village that morning. The bread was fresh. The men had eaten. The warmth of food was in the belly, the body for a moment at rest.

The runner brought the mail. Three letters for the battalion. One for me. I recognized the handwriting — my father's, the script of a man taught penmanship in the old style, the letters formed with care, each one separate, the ink dark. The envelope was addressed to Martín Javier Echeverría, Requeté de Navarra.

I opened it. The paper inside was folded once. My father's writing. Three paragraphs. The first about the store. The second about the town. The third shorter than the others.

The store was open. Business was slow. The war had disrupted the supply chains. He could not get iron fittings from the Basque Country because the Basque Country was now the front. He could not get nails from Vitoria because the roads were blocked. He was selling what he had. When it was gone he would have nothing to sell. The hardware store on the Calle Mayor, open for forty years, which my grandfather had opened and my father had run, was running out of hardware.

The town was quiet. The requeté who had marched south were not all coming back. The families of the dead received letters from the Church, not from the army: "Your son died for Christ the King. His sacrifice is honored." The letters did not say how or where. The mothers read the letters and folded them and put them in drawers. I knew this because my mother had been dead ten years and the letters she left were in a drawer in the back room of the store, folded the same way, the paper yellowing, the ink fading. The commemorations at the Plaza del Castillo continued. The red berets were still worn. The flags were still raised. The voices were lower.

The third paragraph. I read it once. I read it again. The words did not change.

"Your grandfather fought for the fueros. Your father wore the beret for the fueros. You are fighting for the men who will abolish them."

I folded the letter. I put it in my breast pocket, beside the crucifix. The paper was thin. The wood was hard. I pressed my hand against the pocket. Both edges — paper and wood — against the palm. The paper would soften with time, with the sweat of the body, with the damp of the mountains. The wood would hold. The paper would hold too, but differently — the way a man holds a thing he keeps reading, the fold becoming a crease, the crease becoming a tear, the tear becoming a hole where the words used to be.

Tomás looked at me. He had a piece of bread in his hand. His fingers were dusty with crumbs.

"From your father," he said.

"Yes."

"He's well?"

"The store is slow."

Tomás nodded. He put the bread in his mouth. He chewed. He looked at the mountains where the clouds were.

"Mothers and fathers," he said. "They worry."

He said it simply. He finished his bread. He wiped his hands on his trousers. He put on his oiled cloth and went to check on the men in the barn.

I sat on the wall. The letter was in my pocket. Your grandfather fought for the fueros. You are fighting for the men who will abolish them.

I did not write back that week. I did not write back the following week, when another letter arrived — shorter, the handwriting less careful, the ink lighter, as if the pen were running dry or the hand were tired. The second letter was two sentences. The store was the same. The town was the same. No third paragraph.

I did not write back. I put the letter in my pocket. I walked. I carried the rifle. I listened to the rain. I slept in barns, in ditches, under trees. The rain soaked through everything. The body was wet, cold, tired. The mind was the place where the letter lived, turning the words over, not answering them — no answer that was not a lie or a betrayal or a silence, and I had chosen silence.

---

October came.

The advance moved through the mountains toward Asturias. The terrain was steeper now, the paths narrower, the drops more sudden. The column moved in single file along tracks cut into cliff faces, the men pressing against the rock, packs scraping stone, rifles slung across backs catching on outcrops. The rain was heavier. The wind came from the north and drove the rain sideways. It hit the face like thrown gravel. The ears rang. The skin of the cheeks was raw. The lips cracked and bleeding, the blood mixing with rainwater — iron and salt.

The fighting was harder. The Asturians fought in the mountains the way the Navarrese fight in the valleys — they knew the terrain, the passes, where the tracks led and where they ended. They set ambushes in the gorges where the column could not deploy, where the men were strung out in single file and the rifles were useless against a target above and behind. They fired from the ridgelines and disappeared before the requeté could reach them. They blew bridges, cut roads, left mines in the paths — a tin box packed with dynamite, buried under loose stone, the trigger wire running up the hillside to a man who was already gone. The column moved forward and men fell and the column moved forward again.

Tomás stopped joking. He walked beside me, his face set, his eyes moving over the ridgelines, the tree lines, the places where the path turned. He carried his rifle in his hands now, not on his shoulder. His jaw was tight. His hands were steady. When he spoke, he spoke in the register I knew from combat — the flat, direct, stripped voice.

"Watch the ridge," he said. "The path turns ahead. There's a draw on the left."

I watched the ridge. The draw. The path. The man in front of me. The sky where the clouds hung low and gray and the rain fell. The mountains rose on either side. The column moved through them like a thread through a needle's eye, narrow, drawn tight.

We took a town at the end of October. I do not remember its name. The Asturians had held it for weeks and the requeté took it in a morning. The fighting was in the streets — short, brutal, the sound of rifles and grenades and the shouting of men in two languages that were almost the same language, the Basque underneath the Castilian like a current under ice. The town was small. A church. A plaza. Stone houses with tile roofs. A school. The school was closed. The door was locked.

I stood in the plaza after the fighting. The rain had stopped. The clouds were breaking. A strip of blue sky to the north, over the mountains, the first blue I had seen in weeks. The light was pale and thin. It fell on the plaza, the houses, the church, and on the thing that stood at the center of the square.

The oak tree.

It was old. The trunk was thick — three men could not have joined hands around it. The bark was deep-furrowed, the ridges dark, the valleys between them holding moss and lichen, green and gold and gray. The branches had spread wide. The canopy, before it was taken, must have covered half the plaza — a roof of leaves in summer, a skeleton of branches in winter, a place where the town gathered, the elders sat, the children played in the shade. You could see the wear on the roots where generations had sat, the bark smoothed by backs, by hands, by the idle touch of people waiting for the market to open or the Mass to end. A Basque oak. Like the Gernikako Arbola — every Basque town had one.

This one had been cut down.

The stump was raw. The wood was pale where the saw had passed, the grain exposed, the rings visible — many, tight and close, a century of growth compressed into a hand's width. The cut was clean. A saw, not an axe. Two men, at least, working from opposite sides, the blade passing through the heartwood and out the other side. The tree had fallen to the north. The stump was a circle of fresh wood in the gray stone of the plaza. Sap had pooled in the center, dark, the color of old honey. It had not yet dried.

The roots were exposed. On the side where the tree had fallen, the roots had been torn from the earth — a wall of roots, the main roots thick as a man's arm, the smaller roots branching in tangles, soil clinging to them, red clay and stones, the dark of the underworld that had never seen light. The roots were pale, the color of bone. They reached into the air, into the earth, holding nothing. The soil beneath them was a pit, a wound in the ground, the depth of the root system now visible — as deep as the tree was tall, the mirror image, the hidden part exposed to rain and light and the eyes of men standing in the plaza.

The Republicans had done this. Before they retreated, before they left the town to the requeté, they had taken a saw to the oak. They had cut it down. They had left the stump, the roots, the fallen trunk in the street, the branches broken, the canopy crushed against the cobblestones. The leaves were still green. They lay on the stones, green and dying. Someone had tried to clear the trunk from the road — the marks of an axe on the end grain, the bark stripped in patches. They had given up. The trunk was too thick. It would lie there until someone came with a saw and the patience to cut it into firewood, and the firewood would be stacked and burned and the heat would go up the chimney and the smoke would go up into the sky and the oak would be gone.

I stood in the plaza. Tomás stood beside me. Neither of us spoke. The light was thin and pale. The clouds moving north. The blue sky widening. The mountains visible now, the ridgelines sharp, the trees on the slopes dark green and brown and the yellow of early autumn.

My hand went to my breast pocket. The crucifix was there. The letter was there. My fingers touched the edge of paper, the edge of wood.

Tomás looked at the stump. At the roots. At the trunk lying in the street, the branches reaching into the air like arms, the leaves green and curling. His round face was set. His red beret dark with the day's rain. The mouth was a line — not the mouth that joked about stew and swimming in the sea.

"A tree," he said.

The stump. The roots. The sap pooling in the center. The rings counting the years that no one would count. The trunk in the street, the branches reaching, the leaves dying on the green.

Padre Joaquín came into the plaza from the church. His cassock was dry — he had been inside, in the dry dark, kneeling. He saw the tree. He stopped. His hand went to his chest. His fingers found the crucifix on the chain and held it. He looked at the stump for a long time. His mouth moved. No sound came.

He walked to the stump. He knelt. His cassock pooled on the wet stone. He put his hand on the cut surface. His palm flat on the wood. His fingers spread. He stayed like that — kneeling, his hand on the stump, his head bowed, his lips moving. I did not hear what he said. The movement of his lips might have been prayer, or habit, or the muscle memory of a man who had blessed everything and was now blessing a stump. His hand was small on the pale wood. His fingers dark against the grain. The rings under his palm. The sap between his fingers.

He stood. He took his hand away. The palm was wet. He looked at it. He wiped it on his cassock and the stain was dark on the black cloth.

He walked past us without speaking. His limp was heavy. His prayer book was in his hand. He did not open it. He walked out of the plaza and down the road and I watched him go — his back curved, his steps small, the cassock moving around his legs like water.

---

That night I sat in the barn with the letter in my hand.

The barn was dry. The roof was intact. The hay was old and sweet, the smell of it the barns of Navarre in autumn, the harvest stored, the fields empty, the mountains white with the first snow. The walls were stone, thick, the mortar crumbling in places where the rain had worked at it for years. A cat slept in the corner, curled on a sack of grain, its tail over its nose. Tomás was asleep beside me. His breathing was slow and even. His scapular was visible at his collar. His hands were under his head. His face in sleep was the face of the boy from Aoiz — young, unlined, the mouth soft. Outside, a mule shifted in its stall. The sound of hooves on packed earth. Then quiet.

I held the letter. The paper was soft from weeks in my pocket. The ink was faded where the fold had rubbed. My father's handwriting. Three paragraphs. The store. The town. The fueros.

I read the third paragraph again. Your grandfather fought for the fueros. Your father wore the beret for the fueros. You are fighting for the men who will abolish them.

My grandfather's crucifix was in my pocket. The wood was smooth from three wars — the Third Carlist War, the mobilizations of the Restoration, this one. My grandfather had carried it in the mountains of Navarre in the 1870s, fighting for Don Carlos, the fueros, the right of the Basque-Navarrese to govern themselves under a king who respected their laws. He had slept in barns like this one. He had eaten bread in the field. He had cleaned his rifle by candlelight while the rain fell on a roof that leaked. My father had carried the crucifix in the commemorations, in the Plaza del Castillo, on the feast of San Fermín, wearing the red beret, standing with the elders, affirming the tradition. I had carried it from Pamplona to Madrid to Guadalajara to the northern campaign, through the rain, the mountains, the mud. It was in my pocket now, beside the letter.

I did not know what my father knew.

I folded the letter. I put it in my pocket. I lay down on the hay. Tomás was beside me. The barn was dark. The rain had started again — on the roof, on the tiles, on the stone walls outside. The same rain it had been in August. The same mountains. The same body lying in the dark, wet, tired, the feet soft, the hands calloused.

I closed my eyes. The oak tree. The stump. The roots. The sap pooling in the center. The rings. The trunk lying in the street, the branches reaching, the leaves green and dying.

Padre Joaquín's hand on the stump. His palm flat. His fingers spread. The blessing his lips held.

The rain on the roof. The hay under my body. Tomás breathing beside me. The letter in my pocket. The crucifix in my pocket. The oak tree behind my eyes, the stump raw, the roots pale, the palm on the wood.

I slept. I dreamed of nothing. The rain fell. The mountains held.
