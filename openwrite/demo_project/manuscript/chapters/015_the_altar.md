# Chapter 15 — The Altar

---

The inkwell is full. I filled it this morning before I sat down, as my father taught me — funnel in the neck of the bottle, slow pour. A rag beside it. A crack in the wood where my thumb fits. I am fifty-six, sitting in the back room of the hardware store, trying to write about the day I came home.

Pen moves. April moves with it. 1939. War over. Road from Zaragoza to Pamplona — I remember dust first. Dust in the throat, dust on the rifle oil, dust and heat and truck engine knocking. Men in the back not singing anymore, just sitting with their packs between their knees and their berets on their heads and the road unwinding behind them like a thread pulled from a garment. One man slept with his mouth open. Another counted rosary beads without moving his lips.

We had been told to assemble at the cuartel by six in the morning. Trucks would take us to the outskirts and we would form up and march into the city. A victory parade. That word had been used. I had not heard it before, not applied to us, not applied to anything I had done. Victory was something from Padre Joaquín's sermons before the attacks, a coin tossed into the air and never landing. Now it had landed. We had won.

I sat in the back of the truck with the crucifix in my pocket and the beret on my head and watched the landscape change from brown flat of Aragon to green fold of Navarre and did not feel what I was supposed to feel. Men around me felt it. Quieter than usual — quiet of men who have been waiting for something and now have it and do not know what to do with their hands. A man across from me, one of the replacements from the Ebro, young, twenty, turned his beret in his hands. Red wool. Yoke-and-arrows badge. He turned it and turned it and did not put it on.

Outskirts. Cuartel. Forming up. Battalion — what was left of it. Sixty-three men walked out of the Ebro line in October and some had been transferred, some discharged, some in hospital. What remained was forty-one men, two officers, and the standard-bearer carrying the requeté flag, red and white, Sacred Heart on the field of red. We formed up in the street outside the cuartel and Teniente Vidal walked the line and said nothing and came back and said, "Forward." Drums started.

---

Pamplona's streets. I had walked them as a boy. I had walked them the morning I left, July 18, 1936, bells ringing and men filling the streets and red berets everywhere, a tide of red moving toward the Plaza del Castillo. Now berets were fewer. Flags were more. Falangist flags — yoke-and-arrows on red and black — hung from the balconies and lampposts and windows of the Calle Mayor. They outnumbered the berets three to one. Red and black moved in the wind and red berets moved beneath them and drums kept the beat and people stood on the sidewalks and cheered. A woman threw flowers from a balcony. Petals landed on the cobblestones and were trampled by boots.

I marched. Left, right. Beret on my head. Yoke-and-arrows badge pinned where the Carlist star had been — ghost of the star beneath it, wool darker where the sun had been kept out for three years. My boots on the cobblestones. Crowd cheered and I looked and they were the same people who had stood on these sidewalks when we left — women, children, old men with hands in their pockets. But the flags were different. Flags had changed.

On the Calle Mayor, three blocks from the Plaza del Castillo, I saw my father.

He stood at the edge of the sidewalk. Not at the front — behind women and children, back against the wall of the hardware store, his store, our store. Dark jacket. White shirt. Collar buttoned. That face — long, lined, still. Hands at his sides. He did not wave. Did not cheer. Watched me march past and his eyes followed me and his mouth did not open, and then I was past him and crowd closed between us and drums carried me on.

I did not look back. Kept my eyes forward. Marched. Beret on my head. Crucifix in my pocket. Weight of both.

---

Aoiz. Three days later. I borrowed a bicycle from the cuartel and rode south through the valley. Road followed the river. Fields were green — spring green, green of Navarre in April, green I had not seen in three years because the meseta was brown and the mountains were gray and the Ebro valley was dust. Green of grass after rain. Poplars along the river, new leaves catching light. Green that meant home. My hands on the handlebars were brown from three years of sun. They had not held a bicycle since I left.

Tomás's family lived in a stone house on the edge of town. Two stories. Kitchen garden behind, fenced with wire. A dog that barked once and went quiet. I had been there before the war — Tomás had brought me home on leave once, in the first autumn, and his mother had fed us lamb and his father had poured wine and kitchen had smelled of woodsmoke and soap. I remembered the scapular on the wall by the door — brown cloth, Virgin on one side, Sacred Heart on the other. Tomás had worn it around his neck from the day he enlisted. He wore it when the mortar round found him on the ridge. I had cut it from his neck before I wrapped him in the blanket. I had tried to get the blood out. It had set.

I leaned the bicycle against the wall. Dog watched me from the doorway. Its tail did not move. I knocked. Sound of footsteps inside, slow, on stone.

His mother opened the door. Shorter than I remembered. Hair gray. Her hands were hands of a woman who had sewn scapulars and baked bread and washed clothes and now folded them alone. She looked at me and at my beret and at my face and her mouth opened and then closed and she stepped aside and let me in.

Kitchen. A table. A stove. Scapular no longer by the door — a space where it had been, a nail, a rectangle of cleaner stone. Pots on the shelf. A basin with water. A window overlooking the kitchen garden, where early peas grew along a wire. I sat down. She sat across from me. Did not offer food or drink. She waited.

I reached into my pocket and took out the scapular. Brown cloth. Virgin on one side. Sacred Heart on the other. Cloth dark in places — ridge had been wet, blanket I wrapped him in had been damp, scapular had been against his chest for three days before I cut it free. Stains would not come out. I had tried.

I set it on the table between us.

"He was brave," I said. "He was my friend."

She looked at the scapular. Did not touch it. Hands in her lap. I watched her not touch it. Two years I had carried his words — *Tell my mother.* I had delivered them. I had not said the rest. I had not said: *He died on a ridge in Catalonia for ground the maps will not remember. He died in the rain. He died for a sentence he could not finish.* I sat in the kitchen and words I had not said hung in the air and his mother sat across from me with hands in her lap and scapular on the table between us and stove ticking as it cooled.

"He was brave," I said again. Because I had nothing else.

She reached across the table and took the scapular. Held it in both hands. Brought it to her chest and held it there and her face did not change and her eyes did not leave the table, and I sat across from her and did not move. Outside, a bird sang in the kitchen garden. Neither of us turned to look.

Dog came in from the doorway and lay down under the table. Stove ticked. Kitchen smelled of woodsmoke — old woodsmoke, kind that lives in the walls and never leaves.

I stood. She did not stand. I walked to the door. Stopped. Turned. Light from the kitchen window fell across her shoulders. She did not move.

"Señora," I said.

She did not look up. Held the scapular. I went through the door and got on the bicycle and rode back to Pamplona through the green valley with the river beside me and poplars moving in the wind and weight of two years lighter by one sentence and heavier by everything it had not said.

---

Dining room. Evening. I had been home for four days. House was unchanged — stone walls, wooden beams, hallway with the hook where the beret hung. Smell of cooking from the kitchen. A clock in the hallway ticking. My father's beret had hung there when I was a child, after the campaigns of the twenties, folded and pressed and slid onto the hook where it waited for the next war. Hook was empty now. I had not yet hung my own.

I stood in the dining room. Table set for two — my father and me. Plates. Glasses. Bread on the board. Wine in the jug. Candle lit. Crucifix above the table. My grandfather's crucifix, from the Third Carlist War, 1872 to 1876. Wood dark with age. Man on the cross, arms out, head down. Body enduring what was asked of it. I had carried it in my pocket for three years. Through Madrid and Jarama and the Basque Country and Teruel and the Ebro. Through cold and heat and killing and waiting. Through Tomás's death. Through the end. I had held it in the courtyard by the dry fountain the night the war ended, held it in the barracks the morning the berets went into the packs, held it on the truck home, held it when I marched past my father on the Calle Mayor, held it in Aoiz in Tomás's mother's kitchen.

It hung on the wall now. Above the table. Where it had always been.

My father came in. Don Eusebio. Tall. Stooped. White hair cropped close. Dark jacket. White shirt. That face — long, lined, still. He looked at me. Looked at the crucifix. Sat down at the head of the table.

I sat across from him. We ate. Bread broke under our fingers. Wine dark in the glasses. Lamb — he had cooked it himself, as my mother had taught him before she died, with peppers and salt and nothing else. We ate in silence. Candle moved. Crucifix above us. Man on the cross, arms out, head down.

When we finished, my father stood. His chair scraped on the stone. Walked to the wall. Reached up and took the crucifix from the hook. Held it in both hands. His hands were large, scarred from the hardware store. He held the crucifix as he had held it when he gave it to me — morning of July 18, morning I left, morning he said, "Carry this. It has been in three wars." He held it and looked at it and his thumb moved across the wood, smoothing the grain, reading the surface with his fingers.

He hung it back on the hook. Straightened it. Stepped back and looked at it and his hands went to his sides and he stood there. Said nothing. Face unchanged. Eyes on the crucifix. Candle moved behind him and his shadow shifted on the wall and the crucifix held its place.

He turned. Walked past me. Did not touch my shoulder. Did not adjust my beret. Went through the doorway and down the hallway and I heard his footsteps on the stairs, slow, one at a time, and then the door of his room closing and the house was quiet.

---

Hallway. I stood by the front door. Hook there — iron, set into the stone wall, paint worn where the beret's edge rubbed. My father's beret had hung here. My grandfather's before that — men of the house, red wool, hook waiting between wars.

I held my beret. In my hands. Red wool, faded at the fold. Shape of my head in it, worn from three years of sun and rain and sweat and dust. Yoke-and-arrows badge pinned on the left side — where the Carlist star had been. Ghost of the star beneath it, wool darker, fibers standing up where the star's edges had pressed them down. I could read the shape with my fingers. Six points. Gone. Shape remaining.

I folded beret. Once. Twice. Pressed it flat against my knee. As my father had taught me. As the man in the barracks had done it — carpenter from Pamplona, rolling his blanket with the care he had once given to wood. As the boy from Estella had done it — holding it in both hands, red wool holding the shape of his head, pressing it flat and sliding it away.

I hung the beret on the hook. It settled. Weight pulling the wool straight. Badge catching light from the hallway window — yoke-and-arrows, gold on red, party's symbol, symbol that had replaced the star, symbol that would hang on the hook now, in the space where tradition had been. I smelled the wool. Sun and sweat and rain. Three years of weather held in the fibers.

I looked at it. Beret on the hook. Hook in the wall. Wall of the house where my father lived and my grandfather had lived and where I had grown up watching beret hang between wars, waiting for the next one. Next war. Next call. Next morning when a man would take beret from hook and put it on his head and walk out the door and into the street and south toward whatever God and the king required. I had done it. My father had done it. My grandfather before him.

Hook was full now. Beret on it. War over. No next war — not for this beret, not for this hook. Tradition that had hung here for three generations was hung up. Stored. As men in the barracks had stored their berets — between blankets and shirts, buckled in, in the dark.

I went upstairs.

---

My room. Bed with iron frame. Window facing the alley. A crack in the plaster above the door, following the line of the beam. Small table by the bed — where I had kept my catechism as a boy, where I had kept my breviary during the seminary years, where I had kept the letters from the front during the war. Table bare now, except for one thing.

A letter. On the pillow. I almost missed it — pale envelope against pale linen. Envelope — forwarded from the battalion, stamps and marks of the military post, censors' ink. Handwriting on the front was a hand I knew. Not my father's — his hand was broad, slow, hand of a man who wrote hardware orders and junta minutes. This hand was narrower. Precise. Hand of a woman who shaped words for children — carefully, each letter placed, each word chosen.

I sat on the bed. Picked up the envelope. Felt its weight — lighter than I expected, for something that had traveled so far. Opened it.

Letter on thin paper. Two pages. Handwriting — teacher's hand, Basque woman's clarity. Dated February 1939. Two months before. It had been traveling.

*Martín,*

*This may not reach you. Battalion's address was the last I had. If you are reading this, the war is over, and you are alive, and you are home.*

*I am in France. Bayonne. I crossed the border in January with what remained of the school's records and the children's drawings and the crucifix from the classroom — crucifix you looked at when you stood in the room where I used to teach. I brought it because it was ours. Because it was theirs. Because men who closed the school did not want it, and I did not want them to have it.*

*School is closed. Language is banned. Children are here, some of them, ones whose families could cross. Others are in San Sebastián. Others are elsewhere. I teach what I can, in a room that is not a school, in a language that is not allowed, to children who miss their homes.*

*You won. The war is over. Franco is in Madrid. Fueros are gone. Basque statute is abolished. Schools are closed. Language is banned. Church has blessed the Caudillo. Bishops have issued their letters. Pope has sent his congratulations.*

*I hope the king you fought for was worth it.*

*I do not say this to wound you. I say it because it is true. You fought for a king. King did not come. You fought for the fueros. Fueros are gone. You fought for Christ the King. Christ is still on the cross. His arms are still out. His head is still down. He has not moved. Men who closed my school spoke His name when they did it.*

*I am not writing to accuse you. I am writing because you stood in my classroom and looked at the crucifix and did not speak, and I thought: he knows. He knows what he has done. And if he knows, then the letter is not accusation. It is testimony.*

*School is closed. Language is banned. You won.*

*Amaia*

---

I read the letter twice. Paper thin. Ink black. Handwriting — precise, careful, letters placed one by one, as a teacher writes when she knows the reader will need to read slowly. I read it once standing. Then I sat on the bed and read it again. Bayonne. A room that is not a school. Children who miss their homes.

I folded the letter. Put it back in the envelope. Placed envelope on the table by the bed — where the catechism had been, where the breviary had been, where letters from the front had been. Envelope lay flat on the wood. Brown paper, softened by travel. Handwriting on the front. Military stamps. Censors' ink.

I looked at the room. Bed. Window. Alley outside. Sound of a cart on the street. A dog barking. Ordinary sounds.

I looked at the table. Letter on it. Crucifix on the wall above — not my grandfather's crucifix, that one was downstairs, above the dining table, where my father had hung it back. Smaller crucifix, one that had been in my room since I was a boy. Wood. Dark. Man on the cross. Arms out. Head down.

Crucifix on the wall. Letter on the table. Beret on the hook downstairs.

I sat on the bed. Did not move. Evening came through the window and room darkened and crucifix on the wall held its place and letter on the table held its place and downstairs, in the hallway, beret on the hook held its place.

---

I am here again. At the table. Inkwell. Crack. Pen. Afternoon light through the small window, falling across the wood in a slant I know by heart. Alley outside. Cart sound. A dog. Ordinary sounds of an ordinary afternoon.

I have written what I remember. Parade. Streets. My father on the sidewalk. Aoiz. Kitchen. Scapular. Dining room. Crucifix in my father's hands. Beret on the hook. Letter on the table. Silence. I have written them in order, as they came, as they still come when I close my eyes and let them.

I have not written what it meant. Because I do not know what it meant. Crucifix is on the wall. It has been there since my grandfather brought it home from the Third Carlist War. My father hung it back. It is still there. Man on the cross has not moved. Arms out. Head down. He endures what is asked of him. He has endured it for as long as I have been alive and as long as my father was alive and as long as the house has stood on the Calle Mayor in Pamplona. He will endure it after I am gone.

Beret is on the hook. Red wool. Yoke-and-arrows badge. Ghost of the Carlist star beneath it. It hangs in the hallway where my father's beret hung and his father's before that. Hook is iron. Paint worn. Tradition stored. Between wars, beret waits.

Letter is on the table. Amaia's hand. Amaia's words. *School is closed. Language is banned. You won. I hope the king you fought for was worth it.* Letter has been on the table for seventeen years. I read it sometimes. Not often. When light is right, house quiet, pen still, inkwell full, crack in wood holding my thumb, afternoon coming through the window. Alley silent. Dog sleeping. Cart passed. I read it and fold it and put it back.

I do not reply. I have no reply. School is closed. Language is banned. King did not come. Fueros are gone. Church has blessed the Caudillo. Crucifix is on the wall. Beret is on the hook. Letter is on the table.

Silence is in the house. It has been here since the day I came home. My father's silence. Dining room silence. Hallway silence where the beret hangs. Bedroom silence where the letter waits.

My father died in 1958. Nineteen years after the war. He died in his room, in bed, with the crucifix above him — smaller one, from my room, because my grandfather's crucifix was downstairs and he could not get downstairs at the end. He died in April, as the poplars along the Arga were coming green. He died with his hands on the blanket and his face toward the window and his mouth closed. He said nothing at the end. He had said nothing for nineteen years. Not about the war. Not about the king. Not about the fueros. Not about the beret on the hook or the crucifix on the wall or the son who sat across from him at the table every evening and ate bread and drank wine and did not speak about what had happened.

He held the crucifix once, in the dining room, the night I came home. Took it from the wall and held it and hung it back. That was all. Hands. Weight. Hanging. Silence.

King did not come.
