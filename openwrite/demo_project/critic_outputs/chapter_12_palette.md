## Palette Achievement

Both required emotions are achieved and sustained across the chapter. They operate at different scales — Grief is specific (Tomás), Desolation is structural (the ridge, the war, the landscape) — and the chapter interweaves them without collapsing into sentimentality.

### Grief

Grief is rendered almost entirely through physical accumulation rather than named emotion. The chapter builds a specific man through detail — his breathing rhythm, his habit of carrying others' loads, his letters dictated to Martín, the scapular he touches before the mortar — and then removes him. The removal is two sentences:

> Shrapnel had torn through his left side — the ribs, the lung. I could see it. I will not describe it further. (line 119)

The restraint here is the chapter's strongest craft decision. The refusal to render the wound in detail is itself an act of grief — the narrator cannot look, and the prose respects that.

**Key grief passages:**

- **"His blood was warm. It was the warmest thing on the ridge."** (line 121) — The specificity of warmth against the cold ridge. The superlative carries the weight of the loss without naming it.

- **"Sleep is a weight that holds itself. Death is a weight that does not."** (line 137) — The distinction is rendered through the body, not the mind. Martín knows Tomás is dead because the weight changed. This is interiority as perception.

- **"I want to hold him here. In this paragraph. In this moment."** (line 71) — The narrator breaks the temporal frame to express the desire to stop time. This is the closest the chapter comes to direct emotional statement, and it works because it is specific to the act of writing — the narrator is holding Tomás in prose because he cannot hold him in life.

- **The unfinished sentence.** "Tell my mother —" / He did not finish the sentence. (lines 129–131) — The outline specified this. The chapter delivers it without embellishment. The dash is not an AI tic; it is the sound of a man stopping.

- **The visit to Aoiz.** (lines 183–197) — The grief extends beyond the death into the aftermath. The mother's hands closing around the scapular. "She did not weep." The door closing. The wheat field in November — stubble, not wheat. The man who wanted to see it will not see it. This sequence earns its length through specificity.

- **"The scapular is still in my pocket. Next to the crucifix. Wood and wool."** (line 199) — The two objects carry the entire weight of the novel's loss. The scapular from a brother, the crucifix from a father. The pairing is earned across twelve chapters.

### Desolation

Desolation operates at two levels: the landscape and the arithmetic.

**Landscape desolation:**

The Ebro valley is rendered as a place stripped of everything that makes a place livable:

> The trees were gone — cut by shellfire, burned by flame, reduced to stumps that jutted from the rock like broken teeth. The grass was gone. The soil was gone. What remained was stone — white, gray, pale yellow — and dust. (line 29)

The description earns its length because it is specific — not "the landscape was devastated" but the enumeration of what was removed and what remained. The dust is particularly effective: "Red dust. Iron dust. It covered everything." (line 29) The monosyllables land.

**Arithmetic desolation:**

> Sixty-three. Of the hundred and twenty who entered the line in July, sixty-three walked out. (lines 173–175)

The number is stated without commentary. The chapter has already rendered what those fifty-seven missing men represent — the mortar, the snipers, the counterattack, the dead left on the slope for three days. The count is the desolation made visible.

**Sound desolation:**

> The silence, when it came — a lull between barrages, a gap in the air traffic — was worse. Silence meant something was about to happen. (line 33)

The inversion — silence as threat, not peace — is desolation rendered through the ear rather than the eye. The chapter's soundscape (artillery, machine guns, engines, mortar walks) makes the silences carry weight.

## Emotional Tension

The outline specified Grief and Desolation in tension. The chapter achieves this by making Tomás the specific vessel of grief and the ridge the general vessel of desolation. The tension operates in the space between them: a man who organizes water, writes letters, digs drainage channels, and says "At least it's not Teruel" — against a landscape that is trying to kill him and eventually does.

The strongest tension point is the October passage:

> I want to hold him here. In this paragraph. In this moment. Alive, warm, his shoulder against mine, the blankets over both of us... I want to hold him here because what comes next is what always comes next, and the holding changes nothing, and the writing changes nothing, and the ink on this page is no substitute for the man. (line 71)

This is grief reaching forward into desolation — the narrator knows what is coming and cannot stop it. The prose enacts the helplessness it describes.

## Rendering Depth

The chapter is predominantly scene (85%+). Summary is used sparingly and earns its place:

- **"This was our life. I am not going to make it into something it was not."** (line 39) — Summary that compresses four months of mortar walks into a single statement. Earned because it follows the detailed mortar sequence.
- **"I will not write about the next days."** (line 169) — Summary by refusal. The narrator chooses not to render what is not Tomás's story. This is restraint, not laziness.
- **"The rest — the continued fighting, the rotation, the relief in late October — is the machinery of war doing what the machinery of war does."** (line 169) — The phrase "machinery of war" is close to cliché but is saved by the trailing clause and by the narrator's established voice.

The Aoiz visit (lines 183–197) is rendered at compressed lyric distance — an entire visit in fifteen lines. The compression works because the scene's emotional weight comes from what is not said, not from the duration of the encounter.

## Specificity

The chapter's specificity is its primary craft strength. Key instances:

- **The mortar walk:** "The first round would land fifty meters east of our position. The second, thirty meters. The third, ten." (line 37) — The counting is specific, physical, behavioral. It is how a man survives, not how a narrator describes survival.
- **Tomás's water schedule:** "He knew the hours when the snipers were most active — midmorning, when the sun was behind the Nationalist positions and the glare made the Republican scopes useless, was the safest time." (line 45) — Tactical detail that renders Tomás's competence without telling the reader he is competent.
- **The drainage channel:** "a small trench leading from our hole to a crack in the rock where the water could drain. It took him four hours with a pick and a broken piece of shrapnel used as a trowel." (line 67) — The detail of the shrapnel-trowel is the kind of specific physical invention that marks lived experience.
- **The scapular:** "Brown wool, frayed at the edges, the image of the Virgin faded by sweat and rain and the oil of his skin." (line 145) — The three agents of fading (sweat, rain, oil) render the object's history without exposition.

## Restraint

The chapter's restraint is exemplary. The most emotionally charged moments are rendered with the fewest words:

- Tomás's death: two sentences of physical description, then "I held him." (line 135)
- The wrapping: "I wrapped him the way you wrap a man who is finished with the world — carefully, because care is what remains when everything else has been taken." (line 143) — The dash is earned; the clause after it is the chapter's moral center.
- The mother: "She closed the door." (line 195) — Four words. The grief is in the closing, not in what precedes it.
- Vidal: "He was a good soldier." (line 163) — The flatness is the point. The professional voice cannot hold what Tomás was.

The chapter does not tell the reader to grieve. It builds a man, kills him, holds him, wraps him, and delivers his scapular to his mother. The grief is the reader's to supply. The prose provides the material; the reader provides the feeling. This is the correct relationship.

## Minor Observations

1. **"I am looking. I am calling it."** appears twice (lines 73 and 201). This is a deliberate structural refrain — the narrator marking the moments where he forces himself to face what he would rather avoid. The repetition is intentional and works.

2. **"The body does not forget. The body dumps its memories on the floor and says: sort it out."** (line 205) — This is the closest the chapter comes to a prose-level weakness. The second sentence is slightly more colloquial than the established register. It is not a failure, but it is a shift.

3. **The opening frame** (lines 5–7) — The hardware store, the inkwell, the crack in the wood. This frame returns at line 181. The recurrence is effective — it grounds the war memory in a present-tense physical space, reminding the reader that this is a man writing, not a narrator performing.

4. **No named emotions.** The chapter contains zero instances of named emotional states. Grief is rendered through blood warmth, blanket weight, scapular texture, unfinished sentences, and closed doors. This is the craft standard met.

## Verdict

**PASS.** The required palette of Grief and Desolation is achieved, sustained, and held in tension throughout the chapter. Grief is specific (Tomás, his scapular, his unfinished sentence). Desolation is structural (the ridge, the dust, the count of sixty-three). The rendering is predominantly scene, the specificity is high, the restraint is exemplary, and the emotional tension between the two palette elements is maintained from the July crossing to the November return. A contest reader would feel this chapter. The grief does not announce itself; it accumulates. The desolation is not described; it is counted, measured, and walked through. The palette lands.
