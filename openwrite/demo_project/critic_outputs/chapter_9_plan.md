# Chapter 9 Plan — "The Fallen Oak" (END OF ACT THREE)

---

## 1. Chapter Number and Title
Chapter 9 — "The Fallen Oak"

## 2. Characters Present
- **Martín Javier Echeverría** (POV) — active registers: The Soldier, The Fracture (emerging)
- **Tomás Eguía** — active register: The Companion
- **Padre Joaquín** — active register: The Doubt (bleeding into pastoral)
- **Don Eusebio** — through letters only (bitter, worsening)

## 3. POV Character
Martín Javier Echeverría. First-person retrospective with immediacy technique.

## 4. Narrative Distance
Close to middle distance. The northern campaign continues — mountains, rain, the slow grinding advance. The prose must render the exhaustion and the moral deepening.

## 5. Consciousness Markers
Martín notices:
- **The landscape.** Mountains. Rain. The green of the Basque Country giving way to autumn. The terrain of the advance — steep, wet, exhausting.
- **The letters.** Don Eusebio's letters grow shorter and bitterer. The Carlist content is no longer between the lines — it is in them.
- **The oak tree.** A Basque oak — like the Gernikako Arbola — cut down by the retreating Republicans. The stump is raw. The roots are exposed.
- **The silence.** Martín does not reply to his father's letters. The gap between them is growing.

## 6. Outline Summary
August–October 1937. The northern campaign continues — Santander falls (August), Asturias falls (October). The requeté fight through mountains and rain. The losses mount. The moral crisis does not resolve — it deepens. Martín's letters to his father grow shorter. Don Eusebio's letters grow bitter. "Your grandfather fought for the fueros. You are fighting for the men who will abolish them." Martín does not reply. The chapter ends with the entire northern coast under Nationalist control. The Carlists have won the northern campaign. The cost: they have killed Catholic men fighting for the same fueros, and they have helped deliver the Basque Country to a regime that will ban the language, close the schools, and abolish the autonomy the Basques fought for. The chapter's final image: Martín stands in a village square. An old oak tree — a Basque oak, like the Gernikako Arbola — has been cut down by the retreating Republicans. The stump is raw. The roots are exposed. Martín looks at it and does not speak.

## 7. State Changes
- **Before:** The moral catastrophe was visible (Ch7) and personal (Ch8). Amaia has appeared.
- **After:** The moral catastrophe has deepened. The northern campaign is won. The cost is permanent. The oak tree — the symbol of Basque liberty — is cut down.

## 8. Callbacks
- **The oak tree** — seeded here (Ch9). The Gernikako Arbola. The tree of liberty. Cut down. (Payoff: Ch15)
- **Don Eusebio's letters** — growing bitter. "Your grandfather fought for the fueros. You are fighting for the men who will abolish them." (Payoff: Ch11, Ch15)
- **The crucifix** — held at night. The question growing.
- **Padre Joaquín's blessings** — rote now. The homilies shorter. The doubt visible.

## 9. Emotional Palette
**Exhaustion** and **Moral collapse.** The reader should feel the weight of what has been done — and the silence of a man who cannot articulate what it means. Exhaustion is primary (the body in the mountains, the rain, the losses). Moral collapse is secondary (the fueros being abolished, the oak tree cut down, the silence).

## 10. Key Beats
1. **The northern campaign** — Mountains. Rain. The advance through Gipuzkoa and into Santander and Asturias.
2. **Don Eusebio's letters** — Growing bitter. "Your grandfather fought for the fueros. You are fighting for the men who will abolish them."
3. **Martín's silence** — He does not reply. The gap between father and son.
4. **The losses** — Men dying. The cost mounting.
5. **The oak tree** — Cut down by retreating Republicans. The stump. The roots. Martín looks at it and does not speak.
6. **The narrator's bridge** — The boy does not know the fueros will be abolished. The oak tree is the sign.

## 11. Timeline
August–October 1937. Santander falls (August). Asturias falls (October). The entire northern coast under Nationalist control.

## Notes for the Prose Writer

1. **The oak tree is the chapter's central image.** A Basque oak — like the Gernikako Arbola — cut down. The stump. The roots. Martín looks at it and does not speak. Render with extreme close-up.
2. **Don Eusebio's letters are the chapter's emotional anchor.** The bitterness is now in the words, not between them. "Your grandfather fought for the fueros. You are fighting for the men who will abolish them."
3. **The exhaustion must be physical.** Mountains. Rain. The body in the terrain. The losses mounting.
4. **Padre Joaquín's blessings are rote now.** The homilies shorter. The voice changing. Render through behavior.
5. **No narrator explanation.** The oak tree speaks. The letters speak. The silence speaks.
6. **Watch meta-critic patterns.** All the usual constraints.
