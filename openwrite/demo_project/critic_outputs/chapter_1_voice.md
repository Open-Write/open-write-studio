### Don Eusebio — Line 1: "When."
Register: The Patriarch | Assessment: Pass
Notes: One word. Declarative, final, closing rather than opening. The profile specifies "short sentences that close rather than open" for this register. The absence of a question mark (the text renders it as a statement, not "When?") transforms an interrogative into a command: *tell me*. The brevity carries the weight of a man who has waited his entire life for this news. Only Don Eusebio could compress a century of Carlist anticipation into a single syllable.

### Don Eusebio — Line 2: "Close the door. Go to the next house."
Register: The Patriarch | Assessment: Pass
Notes: Two imperatives. No explanation, no hedge, no warmth. The syntax is that of a man accustomed to issuing orders — the junta elder, the store owner, the patriarch. The command to close the door before sending the runner away is itself an act of control: the news is received, processed, and contained within the household. The Patriarch register does not invite follow-up.

### Don Eusebio — Line 3: "Put the jar down."
Register: The Patriarch | Assessment: Pass
Notes: Single imperative. Physical, precise — he names the object, names the action. This matches the profile's note that Don Eusebio is "a man of objects: the crucifix, the red beret, the store." He commands through things, not abstractions.

### Don Eusebio — Line 4: "Come."
Register: The Patriarch | Assessment: Pass
Notes: The most stripped-down utterance in the chapter. One syllable. The tradition does not waste words on what is understood. Don Eusebio does not say "come with me to the dining room" or "we need to talk." He says "Come" and the son follows because that is what the son does. The line is irreplaceable — no other character could deliver it with this weight.

### Don Eusebio — Line 5: "Carry this. It has been in three wars."
Register: The Patriarch, with The Father bleeding through | Assessment: Pass
Notes: The first sentence is pure Patriarch — imperative, short, physical. The second sentence ("It has been in three wars") carries the weight of inheritance and the century of Carlist sacrifice. It is declarative and final, matching the Patriarch register, but the content — the counting of wars, the passing of an object from hand to hand — reveals The Father register bleeding through. The profile states The Father "surfaces in private with Martín — and only with Martín." This is a private moment. The bleed-through is intentional and precisely calibrated: the Patriarch speaks the command, The Father speaks the history. The subtext is that Don Eusebio is saying *you are the third generation* without saying it.

### Padre Joaquín — Line 1: "Hijos. The hour has come. The hour that your fathers prayed for. The hour that your grandfathers died for. The hour of Christ the King and of the king who will come."
Register: The Pastor | Assessment: Pass
Notes: "Hijos" — sons, not soldiers. This matches the profile's specification that Padre Joaquín uses "hijo" when addressing the requeté, "not 'soldado' (soldider)." The word reframes the military assembly as a family sending. The repeated "The hour" structure is liturgical anaphora — the cadences of homily that the profile assigns to The Pastor register. The content moves from fathers to grandfathers to Christ the King to the temporal king, mapping the Carlist theological frame (divine right, historical sacrifice, the coming restoration) onto a single rhetorical arc. The voice is warm, measured, pastoral — exactly as specified. The closing "The hour of Christ the King and of the king who will come" fuses the theological and the political, which the profile identifies as the core of Padre Joaquín's Carlist theology: "the faith is not separate from the state; the state is the faith's instrument." Only Padre Joaquín could deliver this address.

### Padre Joaquín — Line 2: "You go to restore what was taken. The faith. The fueros. The king. These are not abstractions. These are the walls of the house you live in. These are the stones of the street you walk on. These are the bones of your fathers. You go to put back what was broken. You go with God."
Register: The Pastor | Assessment: Pass
Notes: Continuation of the public address. The triadic structure (faith, fueros, king) is homiletic — the priest's rhetorical training at work. The pivot from abstraction ("These are not abstractions") to concrete metaphor ("walls," "stones," "bones") is a pastoral technique: grounding theology in the physical world the men know. The anaphora ("These are... These are... These are...") mirrors the liturgical repetition of the first line. The closing "You go with God" is the benediction formula — the Pastor's register reaching its natural conclusion. The voice is consistent throughout: warm, measured, elevated but not ornamental. The subtext of "You go to restore what was taken" is that something has been stolen — the language of crusade, not civil war. This is Padre Joaquín's theological frame made audible.

### Tomás — Line 1: "I'm Tomás. I'm from Aoiz. I'll be beside you."
Register: The Companion | Assessment: Pass
Notes: Easy, direct, factual. The narrator observes he said it "the way a man says a fact about the weather. Not an offer. Not a promise. A fact." This matches the profile's Companion register: "easy, colloquial, half-sentences." The self-introduction is without ceremony — name, origin, statement of position. "I'll be beside you" is not a vow; it is a spatial declaration, the way a man might say "the road goes south." The subtext is companionship offered without sentimentality: *you are not alone, and I am not making a big deal about it.* Only Tomás would introduce himself this way — Don Eusebio would not introduce himself at all (his identity precedes him), Padre Joaquín would offer a blessing, and the unnamed Runner would not stop moving.

### Tomás — Line 2: "You eat yet?"
Register: The Companion | Assessment: Pass
Notes: Colloquial, truncated — "eat yet" rather than "have you eaten yet." The dropped auxiliary is a marker of informal register and potentially dialect (Navarrese/Basque-inflected Spanish). The question is practical, physical — food, not ideology. The profile specifies: "He talks about food, sleep, women, home." This is the opening move of a man who reads situations through the body's needs. The subtext is care expressed as practicality: *I notice whether you've taken care of yourself.* Only Tomás would lead with this question when meeting a stranger at a military forming-up point.

### Tomás — Line 3: "First time away from home?" / "First time with a rifle?"
Register: The Companion | Assessment: Pass
Notes: Two short, observational questions. He is reading Martín — the hardware store boy, the seminary dropout — and calibrating his approach. The questions are physical and personal without being intrusive. They match the profile's description of a man who "speaks in fragments and contractions" and "does not complete sentences when the meaning is clear." Tomás does not ask "Is this your first time away from home?" — he drops the opening, making it conversational, not interrogational. The questions also reveal Tomás's own experience: he is comfortable asking because he has done this before (three older brothers, the family tradition of enlistment). The voice is distinct — Don Eusebio would not ask such questions (he already knows his son's experience), Padre Joaquín would ask about the soul.

### Tomás — Line 4: "It's heavy at first. You get used to it. My brothers said you get used to everything. I think they were lying about some of it. But the rifle part is true."
Register: The Companion | Assessment: Pass
Notes: Excellent Tomás voice. The sentence structure — short, declarative, with a wry kicker ("But the rifle part is true") — is distinctly his. The humor ("I think they were lying about some of it") deflects seriousness, matching the profile's specification that "he deflects seriousness with humor and discomfort with silence." The admission of uncertainty ("lying about some of it") is delivered without weight — it is observation, not confession. The reference to his brothers is natural and unprompted, matching the profile's note that Tomás "is the fourth son. The fourth son does not carry the weight of inheritance or the burden of expectation. The fourth son follows." The subtext is honest uncertainty delivered with humor: *the world is harder than we were told, but we can handle this part.* This line could only belong to Tomás.

## Narrative Voice Review (Martín)

### Opening — The Inkwell / Grounding Location (lines 3–7)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "The inkwell is full. I filled it this morning before I sat down, the way my father taught me — the funnel, the slow pour, the wipe of the rim with the rag." This is the Grounding Location exactly as the locked voice spec defines it: "The narrator writes at his father's table in the hardware store in Pamplona. The inkwell, the funnel, the rag, the crack in the wood — these are the physical anchors of the act of writing." Present tense for the writing act, past for the remembered. The detail of "the way my father taught me" establishes the generational frame that will structure the entire chapter. The crack in the wood — "deeper now" — is the first instance of the narrator marking time's passage through physical objects.

### The Smell Bridge — Immediacy Collapse (lines 5–7)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "When I breathe it in — when I lean over the table and the smell rises from the floorboards — the back room dissolves. Not dissolves. That is not the right word. The back room remains. I am here. The pen is in my hand. But the smell puts another room on top of this one." This is the voice spec's core technique — the Immediacy Collapse — executed precisely. The sensory bridge (smell) collapses the distance between the man at the table and the boy in the store. The self-correction ("Not dissolves. That is not the right word.") shows the narrator sorting through what he remembers and what he's invented — "the syntax of someone sorting through what they remember and what they've invented," as the spec describes. The technique varies (here smell, not touch), which the spec requires: "Never the same bridge twice in a chapter."

### Father Description (lines 11–13)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "His hands were large. Scarred across the knuckles from the store — a slipped chisel when he was thirty, a broken pane of glass when he was forty-five, the burn from a forge spark that left a white crescent on the back of his right hand. The hands did not match the writing. The hands were hardware hands. The writing was a clerk's. He was both men in one body — the patriarch and the bookkeeper." The narration stays in Martín's consciousness — the boy's eyes seeing his father's hands, but the framing ("He was both men in one body") is the older narrator's understanding layered over the younger man's observation. The concrete nouns (hands, pen, chisel, glass, forge spark) match the vocabulary spec's directive for "concrete nouns" and "the language of a man who has read his Bible and his breviary and has no patience for ornament." The sentence rhythm — long observational sentences broken by short declaratives ("The hands were hardware hands. The writing was a clerk's.") — matches the spec's rhythm description: "Longer sentences that carry the freight of reflection, broken by short declaratives when memory sharpens into image."

### The Pen Set Down — Selective Memory (lines 19–20)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "That is what I remember first. Not the words — the pen being set down. The nib touching the paper and then lifting. My father's hand moving the pen to the side of the sheet, placing it parallel to the edge of the table." The body's memory selects the physical detail over the informational content — the older narrator remembers the pen, not the words. This is the Immediacy Collapse at work: the sensory detail (nib, paper, the parallel placement) bridges thirty years. "That is what I remember first" is the narrator acknowledging the selectivity of memory, which is the reckoning frame — the writing-as-reckoning labor of looking at what the body preserved.

### The Crucifix Scene — Layered Understanding (lines 39–49)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "I did not know it then. Then I thought the silence was the silence of a man preparing to send his son to war — the dignified silence, the Carlist silence... I thought the silence was strength. It was. But it was also the silence of a man who had already lost one son to tuberculosis and a wife to influenza and who was now giving the last thing he had to a war he believed would deliver the king." This is the voice spec's signature move: the older narrator who knows what the younger man didn't. "I did not know it then. Then I thought... I thought the silence was strength. It was. But it was also..." The sentence structure carries the freight of reflection — the understanding layered through self-correction and expansion. The concrete nouns (crucifix, palm, shaft, wood, tool) maintain the vocabulary discipline. The narrator does not editorialize — he renders the silence and then, from the vantage of thirty years, names what it contained.

### The Beret Adjustment — Extreme Close-Up (lines 51–53)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "His hands were on my temples. He adjusted my beret — the red beret, the one I had worn on Sundays since I was sixteen, the wool soft from years of wearing, the color darkened by sweat and sun. He pulled it forward on the left side. He pressed the fold above my right ear. His fingers moved slowly, precisely, the way his hands moved when he was fitting a hinge or setting a bolt." The physical detail is extreme close-up — fingers, wool, the fold above the ear. The analogy to hardware work ("fitting a hinge or setting a bolt") keeps the narration in Martín's consciousness: this is a hardware store boy comparing his father's tenderness to his father's trade work. The observation is the boy's; the understanding is the man's. The sentence is long, freighted with sensory observation, then breaks: "He stepped back." The spec's rhythm, executed precisely.

### "We Did Not Embrace" — Reckoning Frame (lines 59)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "We did not embrace. I want to be clear about this, because in the years since I have read accounts of men going to war and the accounts always include the embrace... We did not do this. It was not our way. The Carlist tradition does not embrace at the departure point. It prepares. It equips. It blesses. The hands on the beret were the blessing. The crucifix in the hand was the embrace. The step back was the letting go." This is the reckoning frame at its most explicit — the older narrator who has "read accounts" and is now correcting them with his own truth. The triadic structure ("It prepares. It equips. It blesses.") and the parallel sentences ("The hands on the beret were the blessing. The crucifix in the hand was the embrace. The step back was the letting go.") are elevated but earned. The spec permits elevation "when the subject demands it" — the departure of a son to war demands it. The closing triad is the chapter's emotional thesis: objects and gestures as the Carlist language of love.

### The Bells — Liturgical Anaphora (lines 63)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "The bells. I want to say something about the bells. Every church in Pamplona was ringing... The bells said: this is the day. The bells said: the crusade has come. The bells said: God is with you. I believed them. I believed every swing of every clapper in every tower in every church in the city. The sound was not metaphor. The sound was fact." The narrator pauses to address the reader directly ("I want to say something about the bells") — the reckoning frame, the writer at the table marking what he needs to say. The anaphora ("The bells said... The bells said... The bells said...") mirrors Padre Joaquín's liturgical rhythm, which is thematically appropriate: the bells are the Church's voice, and the Church speaks in repetition. The escalating specificity ("every swing of every clapper in every tower in every church in the city") is the young Martín's certainty rendered through accumulation. "The sound was not metaphor. The sound was fact" — the declarative certainty of a man who has not yet been tested.

### The Belief Declaration — Reckoning Without Irony (lines 83–85)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "I want to say that I believed and I want the saying to cost something — because in the years since I have learned to say it with distance, with irony, with the condescension of a man who knows what happened next. I will not do that. I believed. The belief was real... A man would have to be more than human to stand in that square and not believe. I was not more than human. I believed." This is the voice spec's central instruction executed: "The narrator does not tell the reader what to feel. He renders what happened." The self-awareness ("I want to say that I believed and I want the saying to cost something") is the reckoning frame stated explicitly. The refusal to condescend ("I will not do that") is the narrator guarding against the spec's warning against editorializing. The closing pair — "I was not more than human. I believed." — is a fragment-pair that carries enormous weight, matching the spec's rhythm of "longer sentences that carry the freight of reflection, broken by short declaratives when memory sharpens into image." The closing "I believed" is two words that cannot be improved.

### The Second Beret Adjustment — The Father's Need (lines 99)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "His hand came up — the right hand, the hand with the crescent burn — and he touched the beret again. The same adjustment. The same press above the ear. The same slow, precise movement of the fingers. As if he had already done it once and the doing had not been enough and the hands needed to do it again, needed to feel the wool and the skull beneath the wool, needed to fix the beret in place one more time before the beret and the son wearing it walked out of reach." The repetition ("The same adjustment. The same press... The same slow, precise movement") is the body's memory insisting — the older narrator remembering the repetition of the gesture and rendering it as the father's need, not the narrator's decoration. The long final sentence builds through "needed to... needed to... needed to..." — anaphora in the service of emotion, not ornament. The extreme close-up (wool, skull, the fold above the ear) maintains the physical vocabulary discipline. "Walked out of reach" is the narrator's phrase, not the boy's — the older man naming what the father feared.

### "I Looked Back Once" — Self-Correction (lines 139–143)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "I looked back once. I said I did not look back. I said looking back was not what we did. I was wrong." The narrator corrects himself — the reckoning frame at its most compressed. Four short sentences that crack open the Carlist stoicism the chapter has built. "I was wrong" is the narrator doing the work of the reckoning: admitting that the tradition's code was broken in the moment it mattered most. The self-correction is honest and brief — the spec warns against self-pity, and this passage avoids it entirely. It is observation, not confession.

### Tomás Death Foreshadowing — The Narrator's Knowledge (line 149)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "He does not know that the stocky man beside him — the man who gave him the larger half of the bread, the man who said I'll be beside you the way a man says a fact about the weather — will die in his arms in a place he has not yet heard of, and will say words he will carry for the rest of his life, and will not finish the sentence." The older narrator knowing what the boy doesn't — this is the spec's directive to "render the irony of a man who now knows what the crusade became, looking at the boy who believed in it." The embedded references ("the larger half of the bread," "the way a man says a fact about the weather") recall the earlier Tomás scene precisely, binding the chapter's two halves. The trailing "and will not finish the sentence" is a fragment that carries the weight of Tomás's future death without explaining it — the spec's fragment technique at work.

### Final Meditation — Writing-as-Reckoning (lines 147–157)
Assessment: Pass
Notes: "The boy is marching. I am writing this at the table and the inkwell is full and the pen is in my hand and the boy is marching south..." The shift from "I" (narrator) to "the boy" (remembered self) creates the temporal distance the spec requires while maintaining immediacy through physical detail. "He does not know about the unification. He does not know about the yoke and the arrows. He does not know that the king will not come." The repeated "He does not know" is the narrator's knowledge laid against the boy's innocence — the reckoning frame made structural. "The writing will be harder than the fighting, because the fighting required only the body and the writing requires the thing the body cannot provide: the willingness to look at what happened and call it what it was. I am looking." This is the Writing-as-Reckoning frame — the voice spec's central metaphor made explicit. "I am looking" closes the chapter as the spec's "I am looking. I am calling it." rendered in its simplest, most irreducible form.

## Overall Assessment

### Voice Consistency
This chapter demonstrates exceptional voice consistency across all four speaking characters and the narrator. Every character speaks from within their defined registers, with no unauthorized bleed-through, no register confusion, and no moments where a line could belong to another character. The voice spec's techniques — the Immediacy Collapse, the Grounding Location, the Writing-as-Reckoning frame, the fragment rhythm, the concrete vocabulary — are deployed throughout the narration with discipline and variation.

### Register Function
Each register functions as designed:
- **Don Eusebio's Patriarch** register delivers authority, finality, and the weight of the Carlist century. The single controlled bleed-through to The Father register (the crucifix handoff) is the chapter's most powerful character moment.
- **Padre Joaquín's Pastor** register delivers the theological frame — warm, liturgical, homiletic. "Hijos" not "soldados" is the register's thesis in a single word.
- **Tomás's Companion** register delivers humanity, practicality, and humor. His voice is the chapter's most immediately distinctive — colloquial, physical, warm without sentimentality.
- **Martín's remembered registers** (The Son, The Soldier emerging) are appropriately minimal in dialogue and rich in narration. The young man says little; the older man says everything.

### Subtext Quality
Subtext operates at high density throughout. Don Eusebio's "When" compresses a century of waiting into a syllable. The second beret adjustment says nothing and says everything. Tomás's "You eat yet?" expresses care as practicality. Padre Joaquín's "Hijos" reframes a military assembly as a family sending. The crucifix — passed, carried, held — is the chapter's central subtext object: it means inheritance, duty, faith, and (in the narrator's knowing frame) loss, all at once.

### POV Narration Consistency
The narrator never slips out of Martín's consciousness. Every observation is filtered through a hardware store boy's eyes (comparing his father's hands to tools, noting the crack in the wood, counting the inventory) or through the older Martín's reckoning frame (the self-corrections, the "I did not know it then" layering, the refusal to condescend). The temporal shift between "I" (narrator at the table) and "the boy" (remembered self on the road) is handled cleanly throughout. The final section's shift to "the boy" in third person is the narrator creating the distance he needs to deliver the foreshadowing — and it works because the chapter has earned the shift through consistent first-person immersion.

### One Note
The chapter's voice work is not merely consistent — it is the foundation on which the novel's entire emotional architecture will rest. The objects introduced here (the beret, the crucifix, the crack in the wood, the inkwell) will carry meaning across every subsequent chapter. The registers established here (the Patriarch's silence, the Pastor's homily, the Companion's practicality, the Son's deference) are the baselines against which future erosion will be measured. This is voice work that serves the novel, not the chapter.
