# Show-Don't-Tell Review: Chapter 1

## Summary
Total: 13 | Critical: 4 | Moderate: 5 | Minor: 4

**Note on POV structure:** This chapter uses a dual-frame narration — an older Martín writing in 1962 about the morning of July 18, 1936. The retrospective frame is intentional and structurally justified. However, the format rules (Section IX) specify close third with no omniscient access to other characters' interiority. Several violations involve the older narrator explaining what Don Eusebio felt or meant — knowledge the narrator could not possess regardless of the frame. The retrospective frame permits the narrator to reflect on his own experience; it does not permit him to access his father's interiority.

---

## Violations

### 1. Line 39–41 — Omniscience / Authorial Intrusion (Critical)
**Text:** "He was both men in one body — the patriarch and the bookkeeper, the requeté elder and the merchant of bolts and hinges."
**Issue:** The narrator names Don Eusebio's roles and synthesizes them into an interpretive label. "The patriarch and the bookkeeper" is the author explaining the character, not Martín perceiving his father. The preceding physical detail (hands scarred, writing precise, pen held far from the nib) already renders this duality. The sentence tells what the detail already showed.
**Suggestion:** Cut the sentence. The paragraph ending on "He held the pen far from the nib, the way a man holds a tool he has used for fifty years" is stronger without the narrator's interpretive gloss.

### 2. Line 49 — Omniscience / Authorial Intrusion (Critical)
**Text:** "It was also the silence of a man who had already lost one son to tuberculosis and a wife to influenza and who was now giving the last thing he had to a war he believed would deliver the king, and the silence was the only structure that held the grief in place."
**Issue:** The narrator explains Don Eusebio's interiority — his grief, his reasoning, his emotional architecture. The younger Martín could not have known this ("I did not know it then"). The older Martín could infer it, but the inference is stated as fact, not rendered as speculation. More critically, the preceding silence — "the space between us was the space of two men who did not have a language for what was happening" — already does this work through the objects (crucifix, beret, step back). The explanatory sentence deflates the silence it claims to interpret.
**Suggestion:** End the paragraph at "I know this now. I did not know it then." Let the silence remain unexplained. The objects (crucifix, beret, step back) are the language — trust them.

### 3. Line 59 — Telling-not-Rendering / Authorial Intrusion (Critical)
**Text:** "The hands on the beret were the blessing. The crucifix in the hand was the embrace. The step back was the letting go."
**Issue:** The narrator interprets his own father's gestures as symbolic equivalents. "The letting go" names the emotion the father may have felt. The preceding sentence — "We did not embrace. I want to be clear about this" — is already borderline meta-address. The three-beat interpretation that follows is the narrator doing the reader's work. The gestures are already rendered with enough specificity that the reader can feel their weight without the narrator decoding them.
**Suggestion:** Cut from "The hands on the beret were the blessing" through "The step back was the letting go." The paragraph ending on "It was not our way. The Carlist tradition does not embrace at the departure point. It prepares. It equips. It blesses." is sufficient. The reader has the hands, the crucifix, the step back — let the reader do the work.

### 4. Lines 99–105 — Omniscience / Authorial Intrusion (Critical)
**Text:** "As if he had already done it once and the doing had not been enough and the hands needed to do it again, needed to feel the wool and the skull beneath the wool, needed to fix the beret in place one more time before the beret and the son wearing it walked out of reach."
**Issue:** The narrator explains Don Eusebio's motivation — "the hands needed to do it again," "needed to feel the wool and the skull." This is the father's interiority, which Martín cannot access. The physical gesture — the same adjustment, the same press above the ear — is already rendered. The narrator's interpretation ("walked out of reach") names the fear behind the gesture rather than letting the repetition speak for itself.
**Suggestion:** End the paragraph at "The same slow, precise movement of the fingers." The repetition is the point. Let the reader feel the compulsive quality of the gesture without the narrator explaining why the father's hands did it.

### 5. Line 25 — Telling-not-Rendering (Moderate)
**Text:** "and my father looked at me and I looked at him and the store was silent."
**Issue:** "The store was silent" is a flat statement after a rendered moment. The preceding detail — fingers around the glass, the runner panting, dust motes turning — already conveys the silence through sensory absence (no one speaking, no movement). Naming the silence is redundant.
**Suggestion:** Cut "and the store was silent." The image of father and son looking at each other while the runner pants in the doorway is already the silence.

### 6. Line 63 — Over-described / Telling-not-Rendering (Moderate)
**Text:** "The bells said: this is the day. The bells said: the crusade has come. The bells said: God is with you. I believed them. I believed every swing of every clapper in every tower in every church in the city. The sound was not metaphor. The sound was fact. The fact was: the Church was blessing this. The Church was blessing us. The Church was ringing its bells for the requeté and the requeté were answering."
**Issue:** The first half of the passage (vibration through boots, air thick with sound) is excellent rendering. But "The bells said: this is the day. The bells said: the crusade has come. The bells said: God is with you" is the narrator interpreting the bells as verbal messages. Then "The sound was not metaphor. The sound was fact" is meta-commentary on the narrator's own prose. The passage explains what the sound meant rather than letting the physical rendering (vibration, boots, air) carry the weight.
**Suggestion:** Keep the physical rendering up to "I could feel the vibration through the soles of my boots. The air was thick with it." Then cut from "The bells said" through "the requeté were answering." The physical details already convey the bells' authority. Let "I believed them" follow directly — the belief is the character's response, which is scene, not interpretation.

### 7. Line 77 — Over-described / Authorial Intrusion (Moderate)
**Text:** "But it was not the acoustics. It was the voice. The voice of a man who believed what he was saying. The voice of a man who had studied the Carlist theology at the seminary in Estella and who had preached it in the parishes of Navarre for twenty years and who now preached it to the men who would carry it south on their backs and in their blood."
**Issue:** "The voice of a man who believed what he was saying" is telling. The narrator then provides biographical context (seminary, twenty years) that Martín would know from living in Pamplona, but frames it as interpretation rather than perception. The sentence about carrying it "on their backs and in their blood" is the narrator's lyric gloss, not Martín's perception.
**Suggestion:** End the paragraph at "It carried." The next sentence — "The acoustics of the plaza helped" — begins a new thought. Cut the interpretive block. If the seminary detail matters, render it as Martín's memory: "I had heard him preach in the parish since I was a boy."

### 8. Lines 83–85 — Named Emotion / Telling-not-Rendering (Moderate)
**Text:** "The belief was not naive. It was not ignorant. It was the belief of a man who had been raised in a tradition that was a century old, whose grandfather had fought for the same cause and lost, whose father had prepared for the same cause and waited, and who now stood in a square full of men who shared the belief and the tradition and the red beret. The belief had weight. The belief had history. The belief had the Church's blessing ringing from every tower in the city. A man would have to be more than human to stand in that square and not believe. / I was not more than human. I believed."
**Issue:** The narrator names and qualifies his own belief, defends it against anticipated retrospective irony ("I have learned to say it with distance, with irony"), and then asserts its weight and history. "The belief had weight. The belief had history" is named emotion with qualifiers. The passage is the narrator arguing with his future self rather than rendering the moment. The physical details — wood in hand, wool on head, stones under feet, bells in air — are already doing the work in the preceding sentence.
**Suggestion:** Cut from "The belief was not naive" through "I was not more than human." Keep "I believed." The list of physical details ("as real as the wood in my hand and the wool on my head and the stones under my feet and the bells in the air") already grounds the belief in the body. "I believed" after that list is earned. The defensive argument undermines it.

### 9. Line 95 — Named Emotion / Character saying what they mean (Moderate)
**Text:** "I wanted him to say something. I wanted the words — the Carlist words, the words of the tradition, the words my grandfather had said to my father or had not said but that had been passed down through the silence and the objects and the crucifix above the table. I wanted him to say: the king will come. I wanted him to say: your grandfather would be proud. I wanted him to say: come home."
**Issue:** The narrator names his desires explicitly. "I wanted him to say" repeated three times is the character stating what he wants rather than the prose rendering the wanting through behavior or perception. The final line — "come home" — is the most vulnerable, but it's named, not shown.
**Suggestion:** Compress to one rendered moment. The narrator standing before his father, the crowd moving around them, the silence — that is the wanting. Replace the named desires with a physical detail: what Martín's hands are doing, where his eyes go, what he hears instead of the words. The three "I wanted him to say" lines could become one: "I wanted him to say something" — then let the silence do the rest.

### 10. Line 105 — Telling-not-Rendering / Interpretive gloss (Minor)
**Text:** "The crucifix was the language. The beret was the language. The step back was the language."
**Issue:** Three-beat interpretive summary. The narrator names the symbolic weight of objects already rendered with physical specificity. This is a milder version of Violation #3 — the narrator decoding the gestures rather than trusting the rendering.
**Suggestion:** Cut the three sentences. The paragraph already contains the physical details: hands on head, crucifix in hand, step back. The reader does not need the narrator to label them as "language."

### 11. Lines 147–153 — Pure Summary / Retrospective foreshadowing (Minor)
**Text:** "He does not know about the unification. He does not know about the yoke and the arrows. He does not know that the king will not come. He does not know about the Basque towns he will walk through with a rifle while Catholic men he has never met die in the ditches beside the road. He does not know about the Ebro. He does not know that the stocky man beside him — the man who gave him the larger half of the bread, the man who said I'll be beside you the way a man says a fact about the weather — will die in his arms in a place he has not yet heard of, and will say words he will carry for the rest of his life, and will not finish the sentence."
**Issue:** Pure summary of future events delivered as dramatic irony. The narrator compresses the entire novel's arc into a list of what the boy does not know. While structurally intentional (foreshadowing), it is telling-not-rendering by definition — the narrator is summarizing events that have not yet occurred. The Tomás detail ("will die in his arms") is the most effective element, but it is still summary.
**Suggestion:** This is a borderline case. The retrospective frame justifies the narrator's knowledge. If kept, tighten to 2–3 lines maximum. The Tomás foreshadowing is the strongest beat — keep that, cut the political items (unification, yoke and arrows) that the novel will render in scene.

### 12. Line 155 — Meta-address (Minor)
**Text:** "I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The writing will be harder than the fighting, because the fighting required only the body and the writing requires the thing the body cannot provide: the willingness to look at what happened and call it what it was."
**Issue:** The narrator steps back to comment on the act of writing the novel itself. "The writing will be harder than the fighting" is the narrator reflecting on his own creative process. While thematically resonant, it is meta-address — the narrator explaining the novel's own project to the reader.
**Suggestion:** The first two sentences — "I am at the table. The inkwell is full." — are fine as a frame return. Cut the explanation. The chapter has already demonstrated the difficulty of the writing through the self-corrections, the hesitations, the "I know this now" moments. Trust the reader to have felt it.

### 13. Lines 39 — Over-described passage (Minor)
**Text:** "It was dark wood — walnut, my father said, though I do not know if he knew or if his father had told him and the telling had become knowledge. The corpus was hand-carved. The arms of the cross were slightly uneven, the left longer than the right, and the face of Christ was not serene but strained, the mouth open, the brow heavy. It had hung in three houses. My grandfather brought it back from the Third Carlist War — from the hills above Estella, where he had fought as a boy of nineteen and come home with a crucifix and a limp that never left him. My father grew up beneath it. I grew up beneath it. The wood was dark with age and with the smoke of candles and with the accumulated attention of three generations of men who had looked at it before going to do the thing they believed they had to do."
**Issue:** This is a long narrative block (8 lines) that functions as backstory exposition. While the physical detail is strong (uneven arms, strained face, walnut), the passage accumulates symbolic weight through explicit statement — "three generations of men who had looked at it before going to do the thing they believed they had to do." The narrator is telling the reader what the crucifix means rather than letting its physical presence and the grandfather's story do the work. The final clause ("the thing they believed they had to do") is interpretive gloss.
**Suggestion:** The physical description (uneven arms, strained face) and the grandfather's story are strong. Cut the final sentence — "The wood was dark with age and with the smoke of candles and with the accumulated attention of three generations of men who had looked at it before going to do the thing they believed they had to do." The reader already has three generations of history. The "accumulated attention" is the narrator naming what the reader has already assembled.

## Clean Passages (evidence of critical reading)

### > "My father set down his pen. / That is what I remember first. Not the words — the pen being set down. The nib touching the paper and then lifting. My father's hand moving the pen to the side of the sheet, placing it parallel to the edge of the table, the way he always placed it." (Lines 17–19)
Passes because the narrator renders the gesture in extreme close-up — nib, hand, parallel placement — without naming the emotion it carries. The specificity of the physical detail (parallel to the edge, the way he always placed it) conveys the father's control and the weight of the moment without the narrator explaining it.

### > "He was stocky. Broad across the shoulders. Dark hair that grew in every direction and was slightly too long, as if he had been called from something that did not involve mirrors." (Lines 111)
Passes because Tomás is rendered entirely through physical detail. The narrator does not tell us Tomás is easygoing, unpretentious, or a shepherd — the hair, the grin, the pack "already bulging" do that work. The "as if" is earned (one instance, character-specific).

### > "He reached into his pack and pulled out a piece of bread — round, flat, the crust dark. Navarrese bread. He tore it in half and gave me the larger half. He did this without asking and without ceremony, the way a man shares bread with a companion." (Lines 122–123)
Passes because the action renders Tomás's character through behavior. "Gave me the larger half" is the detail — generosity rendered as fact, not labeled. The narrator does not say Tomás is generous or kind. The bread says it.

### > "The morning light came through the front window and caught the dust motes and they turned in the air above the counter like something living." (Line 13)
Passes because the simile is specific and sensory — dust motes in light, rendered as movement. The narrator does not say the store was peaceful or beautiful. The turning motes are the peace.

### > "I could feel the vibration through the soles of my boots. The air was thick with it." (Line 63, partial)
Passes because the bells are rendered as physical sensation — vibration in the feet, thickness in the air. The body receives the sound. This is extreme close-up, body-anchored perception.

### > "The wood was warm from his hands. The weight was less than I expected — it was light enough to carry in a pocket, light enough to forget it was there, and heavy in a way that had nothing to do with its mass." (Lines 47)
Passes because the weight is rendered through the body's expectation and surprise. "Heavy in a way that had nothing to do with its mass" is the narrator's perception, not his interpretation — he is describing what his hands feel, not what the crucifix means.

### > "His hands were on my temples. He adjusted my beret — the red beret, the one I had worn on Sundays since I was sixteen, the wool soft from years of wearing, the color darkened by sweat and sun. He pulled it forward on the left side. He pressed the fold above my right ear." (Lines 52–53)
Passes because the blessing is rendered as physical action — temples, wool, pull, press. The narrator does not say "my father blessed me." The hands on the beret are the blessing. This is the cleanest passage in the chapter.

---

## Verdict

**NEEDS REVISION** — 4 critical violations, all involving the narrator explaining Don Eusebio's interiority or interpreting rendered gestures. The chapter's greatest strength is its physical specificity (the pen, the beret, the bread, the dust motes) — and its greatest weakness is the narrator's impulse to explain what those physical details already convey. The retrospective frame is justified but over-extended: it permits the narrator to reflect on his own experience, but several passages cross into accessing the father's interiority, which the POV contract forbids. The fix is subtraction, not addition — the prose is strongest when it trusts the objects and the gestures to carry the weight without the narrator's interpretive gloss.
