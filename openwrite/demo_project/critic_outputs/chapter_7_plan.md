## 3. POV Character
Martín Javier Echeverría. First-person retrospective with immediacy technique. The remembered Martín is 22, entering the Basque Country — the moral catastrophe.

## 4. Narrative Distance
First-person retrospective with immediacy collapse. This chapter demands close distance — the green mountains, the Basque towns, the recognition. The landscape is the moral mirror. The prose must render the terrain as Martín experiences it: familiar, alien, home-like, enemy.

## 5. Consciousness Markers
The remembered Martín notices:
- **The landscape.** Green mountains. Trees. Rivers. It looks like Navarre. It looks like home. The terrain is the same terrain he grew up in — and it is enemy territory.
- **The town.** Catholic, traditional, with a church and a plaza and a school. The townspeople are silent. They do not cheer. They do not resist. They watch from doorways.
- **The crucifix in the church.** The same crucifix that hangs in every Navarrese church. The same God. The same sacrament.
- **The Euskara alphabet chart in the school.** Torn from the wall. The language his grandmother spoke.
- **The ikurriña.** The Basque flag, torn down and left in the street.

## 6. POV Constraints
- The narrator knows the northern campaign's full moral weight. The boy does not.
- The Basque townspeople are rendered only through what Martín can see — their faces, their silence, their doors.
- The moral catastrophe is felt, not explained. The reader must assemble it from the physical details.

## 7. Outline Summary
June 1937. After the fall of Bilbao (June 19), the requeté battalion is moved north for the campaign through Gipuzkoa and into the remaining Basque territory. The landscape changes — from the brown meseta to the green mountains. Martín recognizes the terrain. It looks like Navarre. It looks like home. The battalion enters a Basque town — Catholic, traditional, with a church and a plaza and a school. The town has been held by Basque Nationalists. The requeté take it. The townspeople are silent. They do not cheer. They do not resist. They watch from doorways as the men in red berets walk their streets. Martín sees a crucifix in the church — the same crucifix that hangs in every Navarrese church. He sees an Euskara alphabet chart in the school. He sees a Basque flag (ikurriña) that has been torn down and left in the street. The moral catastrophe begins here: these people are Catholic. They are Basque. They had the fueros. And the Carlists have taken them.

## 8. State Changes
- **Before:** The enemy was distant (Madrid siege, Jarama trenches). The enemy was "godless Reds."
- **After:** The enemy is Catholic, Basque, and has the same crucifix. The moral ground has shifted. The crusade has a hole in it.
- **The landscape:** From brown meseta to green mountains. From alien terrain to home-like terrain. The moral mirror.

**Misdirection collapsed.** The reader now understands the moral catastrophe. The Carlists are fighting Catholics for the same fueros. The crusade has a hole in it. The reader has been watching a man march toward this recognition and calling it heroism.

## 12. Reader-State Guidance
- **Must convey:** The landscape as moral mirror. The town as a mirror of Pamplona. The crucifix. The alphabet chart. The ikurriña. The silence.
- **Must NOT convey:** The moral catastrophe through narrator explanation. The reader must feel it through the physical details. The narrator does not explain what the crucifix means. The crucifix is there. The reader does the work.
- **The reader must feel:** Recognition. Horror. The ground shifting.

## 13. Emotional Palette
**Recognition** and **Horror.** The reader should feel the moral ground shift — the moment when the enemy is revealed as a mirror. Recognition is primary (the landscape, the crucifix, the language — all familiar). Horror is secondary (the Carlists have taken this. The crusade has a hole in it).
