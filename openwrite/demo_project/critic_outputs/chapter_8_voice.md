## 1. Voice Spec Compliance (V1-A — Immediate Reckoning)

**POV:** First-person retrospective. PASS. Martín narrates throughout. No omniscient leakage. Amaia's interiority is rendered entirely through behavior, voice, and what Martín perceives. Tomás's interiority is rendered through posture and silence. Padre Joaquín through sacramental action. Clean.

**Tense:** Past for remembered events, present for writing frame. PASS. The chapter opens in past ("The town had a school") and closes in present ("I am at the table. The inkwell is full. The hardware store is dark."). The writing frame at L191-197 is the chapter's strongest immediacy collapse: "The pen moves. The boy moves with it. The woman stands in the doorway of the school."

**Register:** Confessional, intimate, weight of hindsight. PASS. The narrator's tone is measured, retrospective — a man who now knows what the crusade cost, looking at the moment when the cost became visible. He does not editorialize. He renders the schoolroom, the chart, the woman, the crucifix. The moral weight is carried by objects and dialogue, not by narrator explanation.

**Immediacy Collapse:** PRESENT, varied, earned. Three distinct bridges:
- Touch: "The paper was warm from my hands. The letters were the same letters my grandmother had on her wall" (L21) — the body bridges thirty years through the chart's paper.
- Sound: "The sounds came from her mouth and entered the room and I recognized them" (L85) — the Euskara bridges to the grandmother's kitchen.
- Writing frame: "The pen moves. The boy moves with it." (L195) — the present-tense hand on the table collapses to the past-tense hand on the chart.

No repetition of technique. Each bridge uses a different sense. PASS.

**Numbness Insight:** Not foregrounded — appropriate for Act III, where the moral vertigo is primary. The chapter's center is recognition, not numbness. Martín is not yet numb to what he sees; he is stunned by it.

**Rhythm:** PASS. Long reflective sentences broken by short declaratives: "I had nothing to say. I said: 'Martín. Requeté.'" / "She did not look at me." / "I said nothing." / "She turned from me and walked to the door." The fragments are earned — they surface at moments of moral confrontation, not as default rhythm.

**Vocabulary:** PASS. Concrete nouns dominate: the chart, the desks, the crucifix, the chalk, the padlock, the beret, the ink pools, the child's slate, the pencil stub. No false elevation. No ornament. The language of a hardware-store clerk's son who sees objects before abstractions.

## 2. Character Voice Differentiation — Special Focus: Amaia

### Amaia Etxeberria — FIRST APPEARANCE

**PASS — Distinct, fully realized, the Teacher register lands with precision.**

Amaia's voice is the chapter's achievement. She speaks approximately 10 lines of dialogue across the chapter, and every line is calibrated to her registers:

**Register 1: The Teacher (declarative, instructional)**
- "You found the door." (L37) — Opens with a statement, not a question. A teacher observing a student who has arrived late.
- "I am Amaia Etxeberria. I taught here." (L41) — Declarative. Past tense. The school is closed. She does not explain; she states.
- "The children sat here. Twenty-three of them. Ages six to twelve." (L49) — Precise numbers. A teacher's accounting of what was taken.
- "I taught them to read in Euskara and in Castilian. I taught them arithmetic. I taught them the catechism. I taught them to write their names. I taught them the names of the mountains." (L49) — Five-fold anaphora. This is the Teacher register's signature: she lists, she inventories, she accounts. The repetition is not decorative — it is pedagogical. She is teaching Martín what the school was.
- "Do you know what the fueros are?" (L57) — The pedagogical question. Not accusation — instruction. A teacher asking a student who has not yet understood.
- "Do you know what you have taken?" (L63) — The same register, turned. The question is devastating because it is the same measured, instructional voice she used to describe the children.
- "Her voice was steady. It was the same clear, measured voice she had used to tell me she taught twenty-three children to read. She was stating. The way a teacher states a fact to a student who has not yet understood it." (L65) — The narrator identifies the register correctly. This is not the narrator explaining; this is Martín recognizing what she is doing.

**Register 2: The Basque (Euskara, warmer, direct)**
- She switches to Euskara at L83-85. The register shift is described through Martín's perception: "Her voice was different in Euskara — warmer, closer, the consonants rounder, the vowels longer. In Castilian she was a teacher. In Euskara she was something else."
- The Euskara is the common ground — "the language of the kitchen, of the grandmother, of the chart on the wall." She speaks it to connect, not to exclude.
- Martín cannot answer. "My tongue sat heavy. My lips were sealed." The language she offers is the language he let go. This is the chapter's emotional center.

**Register 3: The Private Woman (quiet, restrained, direct)**
- "Your grandmother. She spoke Euskara." (L93) — Quiet. Personal. Not instructional.
- "And you are here. In a red beret. Fighting for the men who will ban it." (L01) — Not rage. Not pleading. Statement. The Private Woman register surfaces as devastating clarity. Three short sentences. No elaboration. The precision is the weapon.
- Her silence: "She looked at me for a long time. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Her eyes held mine." (L107) — The Private Woman register rendered through absence of speech. She is waiting for an answer he cannot give.

**Distinctiveness:** Amaia sounds like no other character in the manuscript.
- vs. Martín: He is concrete, physical, body-anchored. She is precise, verbal, instructional. He reaches for nouns; she reaches for facts.
- vs. Tomás: He deflects with humor and silence. She confronts with clarity.
- vs. Padre Joaquín: He is pastoral, warm, blessing. She is declarative, measured, accounting.
- vs. Salazar: He is ideological, persuasive. She is factual, pedagogical.
- vs. Vidal: He is military, clipped. She is measured, patient.

No other character uses the interrogative as pedagogy. No other character inventories what was lost with this precision. No other character speaks Euskara to Martín. She is unique.

**The fixed dialogue:** "We are Catholic. We are Basque. We had the fueros. You took them." (L75) — This is the novel's most important fixed dialogue. It lands as fact, not as accusation. Four short declaratives. No hedging. No ornament. The Teacher register at its most devastating: she is stating what Martín has not yet been forced to hear. The chapter delivers this line exactly as the outline specified.

### Tomás Eguía

**PASS.** Minimal dialogue, all in character.
- "She's right." (L115, L119) — Companion register. Blunt. He does not elaborate. He states the obvious because the obvious needs to be stated.
- "The chart. You're keeping it." (L165) — Notices the physical object. Companion register.
- "She was a teacher. Before." (L171) — Half-sentence. The "Before" carries the weight. Tomás does not complete sentences when the meaning is clear.
- "My mother taught me to read. In the kitchen. By the fire." (L177) — The Son register surfacing. He connects Amaia to his mother through the act of teaching. His voice softens. "She was patient. She repeated them. Until I could hold them." — This is the most vulnerable Tomás has been in the manuscript.
- "We are here. And she is there. And her school is closed." (L185) — Three sentences. The polysyndeton is earned — it's the Companion register at its most honest, stating the irreconcilable.

### Padre Joaquín

**PASS.** Dialogue limited to sacramental formula, rendered through action.
- "Te absuelvo." / "In nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus Sancti." (L143) — The Pastor register, full, rote, the voice of a man who has said these words in "ditches and fields and hospitals and churches."
- The physical rendering is the chapter's strength: "His thumb moved on the soldier's forehead. The sign of the cross. The same sign he had made over the requeté who died at the bridge. The same words. The same absolution. The same God who absolved the man who took the town and the man who lost it." (L145) — The "same" refrain extends to Padre Joaquín's sacrament. The same blessing for both sides.
- His silence after the death: "He did not speak. He walked past us, back toward the town. His lips moved but no sound came." (L155) — The doubt register surfacing through behavior. Not through words — through the absence of words.

### Martín (narrator / remembered self)

**PASS.** The remembered Martín speaks little — appropriate for a chapter driven by observation and confrontation. When he speaks, it is in the Catechism register fading: "Martín. Requeté." (L43) — identification, not conviction. "I know." (L61) — answering the teacher's question. The narrator's register is consistent: the man who has already reckoned, writing at the table.

## 5. Additional Findings

### Amaia's Physical Description — Body Anchor Consistency
"Her hands were at her sides, the fingers slightly spread, the way a teacher stands when she is waiting for the room to settle" (L31). This recurs at L197: "Her hands are at her sides. Her fingers are slightly spread. She is waiting for the room to settle." The callback is clean — the writing frame past-tense observation becomes present-tense image. The body anchor is Amaia's signature. **PASS.**

### The "She Looked At" Anaphora (L33)
"She looked at the overturned desks. She looked at the spilled ink. She looked at the chalk tray with its two pieces of chalk. She looked at the crucifix above the blackboard. She looked at the chart in my jacket." — Five instances of "She looked at." This is Amaia's entrance — she surveys the destruction with the methodical attention of a teacher taking inventory. The five-fold repetition is earned: it renders her perception as cataloguing, not as emotion. She does not react. She documents. **PASS — do not cut.**

### The Euskara Rendering
The chapter does not render Amaia's Euskara dialogue directly — Martín catches only fragments: "Eskerrik asko — thank you. Zer — what. Zergatik — why. Nor — who." This is correct: Martín cannot speak Euskara, so he cannot report it verbatim. The sounds are rendered through his perception: "the soft consonants, the vowels that opened and closed like hands, the rhythm of a sentence in Euskara, the way it moved forward and then paused and then moved forward again, like a man walking through hills, like water over stones." The two similes ("like a man walking through hills, like water over stones") are earned — they render the rhythm of a language the narrator can hear but not speak. **PASS.**

### Interiority — Levels 4-5 Throughout
No named emotions. All interiority rendered through behavior and perception:
