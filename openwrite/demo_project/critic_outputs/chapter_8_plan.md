# Chapter 8 Plan — "The Schoolteacher"

---

## 1. Chapter Number and Title
Chapter 8 — "The Schoolteacher"

## 2. Characters Present
- **Martín Javier Echeverría** (POV) — active registers: The Soldier, The Fracture (emerging)
- **Amaia Etxeberria** — active registers: The Teacher, The Basque, The Private Woman — **NEW CHARACTER, first appearance**
- **Tomás Eguía** — active registers: The Companion
- **Padre Joaquín** — active register: The Pastor (doubt deepening)

## 3. POV Character
Martín Javier Echeverría. First-person retrospective with immediacy technique.

## 4. Narrative Distance
Close. This chapter demands extreme close-up — the encounter with Amaia must be rendered through Martín's perception: her face, her voice, her hands, the schoolroom. The moral catastrophe becomes personal.

## 5. Consciousness Markers
Martín notices:
- **Amaia's face.** Angular, serious, still. Her eyes. The way she speaks — slowly, precisely, choosing words the way she chooses them for students.
- **The schoolroom.** Desks overturned. The Euskara alphabet chart torn from the wall. The crucifix above the blackboard — the same crucifix.
- **Her Euskara.** The language his grandmother spoke. The sounds. The familiarity.
- **Her question.** "Do you know what the fueros are? Do you know what you have taken?"

## 6. POV Constraints
- Amaia's interiority is visible only through behavior, voice, and what Martín can read.
- The moral catastrophe is rendered through the schoolroom's objects and Amaia's words — not through narrator explanation.

## 7. Outline Summary
July 1937. The battalion is billeted in the occupied town. Martín meets Amaia Etxeberria — a Basque schoolteacher whose school has been closed by the occupation. She is 28, Catholic, PNV, a speaker of Euskara. She does not argue politics. She teaches. She shows Martín the closed school. The desks are overturned. The Euskara alphabet chart is torn from the wall. The crucifix above the blackboard is the same crucifix that hangs in every Navarrese classroom. "We are Catholic. We are Basque. We had the fueros. You took them." Martín does not answer. He cannot answer. Amaia speaks Euskara to him — the same language his grandmother spoke. The common ground makes the political division incomprehensible. Tomás sees the encounter and says nothing. Padre Joaquín administers last rites to a dying Basque Nationalist soldier — a Catholic man praying for absolution. The same sacrament. The same God.

## 8. State Changes
- **Before:** The moral catastrophe was visible through objects (Ch7). The enemy was a mirror.
- **After:** The moral catastrophe has a name, a face, a voice. Amaia is Catholic, Basque, traditional — everything the Carlists claim to be. And she is on the other side.

## 9. Callbacks Landing

- **The Euskara alphabet chart** (Ch7) — now shown to Martín by Amaia. The language his grandmother spoke.

## 11. Reader-Belief Phase
**Misdirection collapsed, deepening.** The reader now sees the moral catastrophe as a person. Amaia is not an abstraction — she is a woman whose school has been closed, whose language has been banned, whose flag has been torn down. The reader must feel the weight of her presence.

## 12. Emotional Palette
**Moral vertigo** and **Grief.** The reader should feel the ground give way — the realization that the crusade has a hole in it that cannot be patched. Moral vertigo is primary (the recognition that the enemy is a mirror). Grief is secondary (the cost — the closed school, the torn chart, the woman who teaches and has no school).

## Notes for the Prose Writer

1. **Amaia is the novel's moral mirror.** She must be rendered with extreme specificity — her face, her voice, her hands, her precision. She is not a symbol. She is a woman.
2. **"We are Catholic. We are Basque. We had the fueros. You took them."** This is the novel's most important fixed dialogue. It must land as fact, not as accusation. Amaia states. She does not argue.
3. **The schoolroom is the chapter's physical anchor.** Desks overturned. Chart torn. Crucifix remaining. Render each object with extreme close-up.
4. **Amaia speaks Euskara to Martín.** The language his grandmother spoke. The common ground makes the division incomprehensible. Render the sounds, the recognition, the impossibility.
5. **Padre Joaquín's last rites for a Basque soldier** — the same sacrament. The same God. Render through behavior, not theology.
6. **No narrator explanation of the moral catastrophe.** The objects speak. Amaia speaks. The narrator does not explain what they mean.
7. **Watch meta-critic patterns:** Chapter endings (no interpretive commentary), "I did not know" (max 1), "the way" (≤2/1k), polysyndeton (≤25%), negative density (≤10/1k), "The" starters (≤15%), em-dashes (≤2/250w), "the same" (≤12).
