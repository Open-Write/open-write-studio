# THE RECKONING — Run 3

I have tried to write this down before. Three times I have sat at the table in the back room of the hardware store — the same table where my father kept his ledgers, the same inkwell, the same chair with the broken arm that he never repaired because he said a man should be uncomfortable when he works — and three times I have put the pen down and walked away. Not because the memories are painful, though they are. Because the memories are not reliable. I know what happened. I do not always know what I felt when it happened, and the feeling is the part that matters, and the feeling is the part that changes every time I try to pin it down.

But I will try again. I will write what I know, and when I come to a place where I am not sure, I will say so, and when I come to a place where the memory has been shaped by what came after — by the unification, by the northern campaign, by Teruel, by the Ebro, by the silence that followed the victory — I will try to scrape the later layers away and find what was underneath.

This is what I know.

We arrived at the position on the evening of July twenty-third, 1936. Five days after the rising. Six days after I had walked into the requeté recruitment office on the Calle de la Merced in Pamplona and given my name and my father's name and the name of the parish where I was baptized, and the man behind the desk — a former military officer with a mustache and the calm efficiency of someone processing cattle — had written it all down and handed me a red beret and told me to report to the Plaza del Castillo at six the following morning.

The beret was new. Not my father's — that one was still hanging on the hook in the hallway, and it would stay there until I came back for it. This one was stiff, the wool still carrying the lanolin smell of the factory, and it sat on my head like a helmet rather than like a garment. My father's beret was soft from years of wear, shaped to his skull, carrying the accumulated sweat and dust and cedar of two decades of Carlist meetings and requeté reunions. This new beret had none of that. It had only the future.

I was not the only one. There were forty-three of us who reported to the Plaza del Castillo that morning, and by noon we were on the road south, and the road was white with dust and the sun was the feast of Santiago and the men around me were singing and I was singing with them and the beret was on my head and the rifle — a Mauser, long and heavy, the stock scarred with the marks of whoever had carried it before me — was on my shoulder and the whole thing felt like a procession. Not a march to war. A procession. The kind of thing that happens on the feast of San Fermín, when the streets are full and the music is playing and everyone knows where they are going and why.

Three days of marching. The dust got into everything — the rifle, the bread, the folds of the beret, the creases of my hands. Tomás walked beside me and said little, because Tomás was a man of few words and those words were usually complaints about the quality of the food or the distance to the next water stop, and I was grateful for his silence because it meant I could think. I thought about the king. I thought about Don Jaime, who was old and in exile and waiting for the call that would bring him back to Spain. I thought about the fueros — the ancient rights of Navarre and the Basque provinces, the rights that the Republic had been eroding and that the crusade would restore. I thought about my grandfather, who had fought in the Third Carlist War and who had died before I was born but whose crucifix hung above the dining table and whose name — Martín, the same as mine — was carved into the base.

I thought about these things because they were the things I had been taught to think about. The Carlist catechism: God, king, fueros. The trinity of the traditionalist cause. The three pillars that had held up a century of resistance against the liberal state, and that would hold up this war, and that would hold up the Spain that would emerge from it.

I believed all of it. Not in the way that a man believes a political argument — with reservations, with qualifications, with the knowledge that the other side has points worth considering. I believed it the way I believed that the sun would rise. It was not a matter of opinion. It was a condition of the world.

The position was a trench. I keep using the word because there is no better one, but what I mean when I say it now is a ditch — a shallow, crumbling, poorly built ditch on the side of a hill that overlooked a valley I did not know the name of. The parapet was stones and sandbags. The floor was dirt. There was a tin can for a latrine and a canvas screen that flapped in the wind and a smell that I would learn to recognize as the smell of men living in the ground.

Erice — the sergeant — put me on watch at dawn. "Keep your head down. Watch the ridge. If you see movement, report. If you hear a shot, drop."

I climbed up. I looked out.

The valley was green and the light was early and the ridge on the far side was a line of dark trees against the brightening sky, and on the ridge there were buildings — a farmhouse, a wall, a church tower. The church tower had a cross at its peak. The cross caught the light.

I thought: there are men behind those buildings.

I thought: those men are enemies.

I thought: those men are Spaniards.

The last thought was the one I pushed away. I pushed it away because it did not belong in the catechism. The catechism did not have a place for the enemy's humanity. The enemy was the Republic. The enemy was the atheism and the masonry and the destruction of the Church. The enemy was the thing that stood between Spain and its restoration. The enemy was not a man behind a wall on a ridge, looking through a sight at the place where I stood.

The shot came.

I have described the sound before — to Tomás, to my father in a letter I never sent, to the priest at confession in 1940, to myself in the dark of the back room of the hardware store. The sound was a crack. The bullet was close enough that I felt the air move. The parapet jumped. A piece of stone hit my face.

I dropped. My body dropped before my mind agreed to it. I was in the bottom of the trench with my hands over my head and the dirt in my mouth and my heart beating so hard I could hear it in my ears, and I was afraid. I was afraid in a way I had never been afraid before — not the fear of the dark, which I had outgrown at twelve, not the fear of my father's anger, which was a fear of disappointing, not of harm. This was the fear of being killed. The fear of a bullet entering my body. The fear of the specific and mechanical fact that a piece of metal had been fired from a rifle a thousand meters away and had passed close enough to my head to chip the stone beside it, and that the next one might not miss.

I lay in the dirt. I said a prayer. I do not remember which prayer — it may have been the Our Father, it may have been the Hail Mary, it may have been something without words, the kind of prayer that is only the movement of the mind toward God without the structure of language. I said it and I lay there and I waited for the shaking to stop.

It did not stop for a long time.

Tomás sat down beside me. He did not say "the first one is the worst" or "you get used to it" or any of the things that soldiers say to other soldiers in the first minutes of the first time under fire. He sat down and he took out a cigarette and he lit it and he smoked it in silence, and the smoke curled up out of the trench and into the morning air, and I watched it rise and I thought about how thin it was, how easily it was carried away by the wind, and I thought about the bullet and the thinness of the air it had crossed, and I thought about the distance between the ridge and the trench — a thousand meters, maybe less — and how short that distance was, and how a man could cover it in minutes, and how the only thing between me and the men on the ridge was the ditch and the stones and the sandbags and the tin and the canvas and the fact that they had not decided, yet, to try again.

I got up. I put my hands on the rifle. I looked at the ridge.

The church tower was still there. The cross was still there. The light was on it.

I stayed at the parapet. I did not drop again.
