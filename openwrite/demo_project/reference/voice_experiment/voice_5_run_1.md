# THE SPARSE — Run 1

He did not sleep.

The ground was hard. The night was cold. The men around him snored and shifted and coughed and the coughing echoed in the trench and the trench was shallow and the parapet was low and the stars were above the parapet and the stars were very far away.

He lay on his back. The beret was on his head. The blanket was at his chin. The rifle was beside him with the barrel pointing away from the sleeping men.

He listened to the dark. The dark was quiet. The quiet was the war, waiting.

The men around him were shapes. A shoulder here. A boot there. The dull shine of a rifle barrel catching starlight. He knew their names — Tomás, who slept with his beret over his face; Ignacio, the boy from Tudela, who slept with his mouth open; an older man whose name he had not caught, who slept with his rosary in his hands.

He did not know the name of the valley. He did not know the name of the ridge. He knew Pamplona. He knew the Calle Mayor. He knew the house where his father slept and the hook in the hallway where his father's beret hung and the crucifix above the dining table and the name carved into the base.

Three days of marching. The road south from Logroño was white with dust. The sun was the feast of Santiago. The men sang the requeté hymn in the first hour and fell silent in the third. By the second day the berets were the color of the road. By the third day the men were the color of the road. The only red left was the flash of the lining when the wind caught it.

The rifle on his shoulder grew heavier each day. The pack dug into his hips. The boots were new and the boots were stiff and the boots raised blisters on his heels that burst and bled and hardened into calluses that would last the war. He walked beside Tomás and Tomás did not complain and he was grateful for that and he was ashamed of the gratitude.

He closed his eyes. The dark was the same with his eyes closed. The ridges of the packed earth pressed into his back — the base of the skull, the shoulder blades, the small of the back, the hips. Six points of pressure. Six places where the body met the ground and the ground refused to yield.

In the morning the light came fast. The sky went from black to gray to the pale dry color of the trench walls. The stones of the parapet emerged. The sandbags. The corrugated tin. The canvas that flapped in the wind.

He sat up. His spine cracked. His neck was stiff. He rolled his shoulders and the joints popped and the sound was loud in the quiet of the trench.

Tomás was awake. Tomás was cleaning his rifle with a rag. The rag had been a shirt once. The oil smell was sharp — gun solvent and metal and the sourness of use.

"Morning," Tomás said.

He did not answer. He reached for the canteen and drank. The water was warm and tasted of metal. He drank again. He clipped the canteen to his belt.

His hands were dirty. The dirt was under his nails and in the creases of his knuckles. He picked at the dirt with his thumbnail and the dirt came away in small dark crescents that fell onto the blanket. The dirt was the color of the trench walls — pale, dry, the dirt of the Castilian meseta, nothing like the dark wet soil of Navarre.

Erice came. The sergeant moved through the trench with his head below the parapet. His boots found the same spots on the same stones. He stopped at Martín's section.

"Parapet. Watch."

Two words. Martín picked up the rifle. The Mauser was long and heavy and the stock was scarred with use. He checked the chamber. Loaded. He checked the safety. On. He moved to the firing step. Climbed up. Put his elbows on the stones.

The valley.

Green. After three days of dust, the valley was green. Fields running down to a river. The river thin and bright. The far slope rising to a ridge. Trees on the ridge. Buildings among the trees.

A farmhouse. Stone. No roof. The rafters dark against the sky. The walls blackened at the top where fire had been.

A wall. Low. Running along the ridge. A gap in it — stones fallen or pushed or hit.

A church tower. Pale. Slender. Older than the farmhouse. Older than the wall.

A cross. At the top of the tower. Iron or wood. At this distance he could not tell.

He watched the cross. The cross caught the light. The light was new — the sun had just cleared the ridge. The cross held the light the way a cup holds water.

He thought about his father. He thought about the hook in the hallway where his father's beret hung. He thought about the dining table and the crucifix above it and the name carved into the base — Martín, his grandfather's name, his name. He thought about the morning he left and his father's hand on his shoulder and the weight of it and his father's words: "You will wear the beret."

Not "be careful." Not "come home." You will wear the beret. As if the beret were the whole of it. As if the wool and the tradition it carried were enough.

He watched the ridge. Nothing moved. The farmhouse was still. The wall was still. The tower was still. The trees were dark and motionless. The terraced fields were empty. The river glinted.

A bird called. A lizard ran along the parapet and stopped and lifted its head and ran again. The sun climbed. The heat built. Sweat formed under the beret. He felt it — a dampness at the hairline, a trickle down the temple, the wool of the band growing wet.

An hour passed. The sun moved. The shadows shortened. The valley filled with light and the light was hard and white and the buildings on the ridge shimmered faintly in the heat.

He could hear, from somewhere down the line, the sound of a man singing. A hymn. Faint. The words indistinct. The melody one he recognized from the marches. The singing stopped. The quiet came back.

The shot came.

The sound was a crack. Sharp. Flat. The bullet passed his head and he heard it — a snap, a tearing, the sound of air being broken. A chip of stone flew from the parapet. The chip hit his cheek. Blood. A thin line running toward the jaw.

He dropped.

The body folded. Knees. Waist. Shoulders. He was in the trench on his back. The rifle across his chest. The sky above him. Blue. Empty. Far away.

His hands shook. He looked at them. They shook. He gripped the rifle. The shaking did not stop. He put his hands on the dirt. The dirt was cool. He pressed his palms into it. The shaking slowed.

Tomás crouched beside him. Tomás's hand on his arm. Tomás's face — calm, steady, the eyes of a man who had been here before.

"Are you hit?"

He touched his cheek. His fingers came away with blood. The chip. The stone. The parapet.

"No."

"Breathe."

He breathed. The air went in. The air came out. The heart slowed. The hands steadied.

He sat up. He looked at the rifle. He looked at his hands. The hands were still. The dirt was on his palms — cool, dry, the dirt of Castile, the dirt that was nothing like the dark wet soil of Navarre. He wiped the dirt on his trousers. He picked up the rifle. Checked the chamber. Loaded. Checked the safety. On.

He looked at Tomás. Tomás was sitting against the wall with his cigarette and his calm eyes and the rag still in his hand from the morning cleaning. Tomás had been here for two weeks. Two weeks was, at that moment, an eternity. Two weeks of mornings and parapets and shots and drops and the return to the parapet.

"Does it get easier?" Martín asked.

Tomás smoked. He blew the smoke at the sky. "The shooting does. The rest does not."

He did not ask what "the rest" meant. He would learn.

He moved to the firing step. Climbed up. Put his elbows on the parapet.

The ridge was there. The farmhouse was there. The wall was there. The tower was there.

The cross was there.

A chip in the stone. Six inches from where his head had been. He looked at the chip. He looked at the place where his head had been. Six inches. The width of his hand. The distance between the bullet being in the stone and the bullet being in his skull.

He watched. The sun climbed higher. The heat built. The sweat ran down his face and dripped from his chin onto the stones of the parapet. The stones absorbed the sweat. Dark circles appeared where the drops landed.

The beret was on his head. The rifle was in his hands. The cross was on the tower.

He thought: the bullet crossed a thousand meters in less than a second.

He thought: six inches is the width of my hand.

He thought: the next one might not miss.

He watched. The morning passed. The afternoon came. The war continued.

He watched.
