# THE CHRONICLER — Run 1

The requeté of the tercio of Navarre reached the forward positions on the evening of July 23, 1936, five days after the rising that would become the Spanish Civil War. They had marched from the railhead at Logroño in three days of heat and dust, following the road south through the Rioja and into Castile, singing the hymns of the Carlist cause and carrying rifles that had been oiled and sighted and blessed by the parish priests of Pamplona. Among them was Martín Javier Echeverría, twenty-two years old, second son of Don Eusebio Echeverría, a Carlist elder and former requeté. Martín wore a new red beret — not his father's, which hung on a hook in the hallway of the house on the Calle Mayor, but a fresh one, stiff with lanolin, issued to him at the recruitment office on the Calle de la Merced five days earlier. He carried a Mauser 98, the standard infantry rifle, with a bayonet that had been sharpened by the armorer at the barracks in Pamplona. He had been assigned to the third squad of the second platoon, under Sergeant Erice, a veteran of the Moroccan campaigns who had the quiet authority of a man who had seen the thing the younger men were about to see and had decided not to describe it in advance.

The position was a trench — though the word overstates the construction. It was a shallow excavation on the forward slope of a hill that overlooked a valley whose name Martín did not learn until later and which is not recorded here because the name is not the point. The point is the geometry: the trench faced west across the valley toward a ridge approximately one thousand meters distant, and on that ridge the Republic had established a defensive position among the buildings of an abandoned farmhouse and the ruins of a low stone wall. A church tower rose above the wall, its cross catching the evening light when the requeté arrived and the morning light when they woke.

Martín did not sleep. This was not unusual — few men sleep well the first night at the front, and the ground was hard and the night was cold and the sounds of the position — the coughing, the shifting, the occasional shot from a nervous sentry down the line — kept the men on the surface of consciousness without granting them the mercy of full rest. He lay on his back with his beret on his head and his rifle beside him and his blanket pulled to his chin and he listened to the dark for the sound of the war, and the war was quiet, and the quiet was the first lesson: the war does not announce itself. It waits.

Dawn came early, as it does on the Castilian meseta in summer — sudden, without gradation, the sky going from black to pale blue in the space of twenty minutes. Sergeant Erice moved through the trench at first light, waking the men who had slept and assigning the morning watch. Martín was sent to the parapet. He climbed the firing step, put his elbows on the stones, and looked out at the valley for the first time in full daylight.

The valley was green. After three days of white dust on the road, the green was a shock — terraced fields of olive and grain running down to a river that caught the early sun and threw it back in a thin line of light. The far ridge rose steeply, dark with trees, and on the ridge the buildings of the enemy position were visible: the farmhouse, its roof gone, its walls blackened by fire; the stone wall, breached in two places; the church tower, slender and pale, with the cross at its peak holding the light.

Martín looked at the cross. He would later think about this moment — in the trenches at Guadalajara, in the hospital at Zaragoza after Teruel, in the silence of his father's house after the war — and he would try to remember what he had felt. What he remembered, consistently, was the cross. The cross on the tower across the valley was the same cross that hung above the dining table in Pamplona, the same cross that the requeté wore on their standards, the same cross that his grandfather had fought under in the Third Carlist War. The men behind that wall — the men of the Republic, the men of the atheistic government that had burned churches and murdered priests and driven the king into exile — those men lived beneath a cross, and the cross had not saved the church from burning, and the cross had not prevented the wall from being breached, and the cross was still there, on the tower, in the morning light, indifferent to the armies that moved beneath it.

He watched for an hour. The sun climbed. The heat built. Sweat formed on his forehead beneath the beret and ran down his temples and dripped from his jaw onto the stones of the parapet. He watched the ridge and the ridge watched him and nothing moved.

At approximately seven hundred hours — the exact time is not recorded, but the sun was well above the ridge and the shadows were short — a shot was fired from the enemy position. The bullet passed Martín's head at a distance that he later estimated, with the imprecision of a man who has no training in ballistics, at less than half a meter. It struck the parapet to his left and dislodged a chip of stone that struck his cheek and drew blood.

He dropped into the trench. The motion was instinctive — the body's refusal to remain in the path of a projectile, older than training, older than discipline, older than faith. He landed on his back with his rifle across his chest and his hands shaking and his heart rate elevated to a degree that he could feel in his temples and his wrists and the hollow of his throat.

Tomás — his surname is not recorded in this account, as he will be referred to throughout by his Christian name, in the manner of the requeté, who knew each other as brothers — Tomás sat down beside him and said nothing for a full minute. Then he offered a cigarette. Martín took it. His hands were shaking and the cigarette trembled between his fingers and the smoke was harsh in his throat and he coughed, and Tomás said: "The first one is the worst."

It was not true. The first one was not the worst. The worst came later — at Guadalajara, where the artillery turned the earth inside out; at the Ebro, where the water ran red for three days; at Teruel, where the cold killed more men than the bullets. The first one was the easiest, because it missed, and because Martín did not yet know how many would follow.

But in the trench, on the morning of July 24, 1936, with the dust of the march still on his beret and the blood of the stone chip on his cheek and the cigarette trembling in his hand, the first shot was the worst thing that had ever happened to him. He sat in the dirt and he prayed — the Our Father, the Hail Mary, the prayer of the requeté that begins "Lord, who hast called us to this crusade" — and he waited for the shaking to stop.

It stopped. The body learned, as the body always learns, to contain the fear. The hands steadied. The breath came back. He picked up the rifle and moved to the firing step and climbed up and put his elbows on the parapet and looked at the ridge.

The farmhouse was there. The wall was there. The church tower was there. The cross was there.

He watched. He would continue to watch for three years, through the campaigns of the war, through the unification decree that took the red beret and gave it to Franco, through the northern campaign where he fought Catholic men fighting for the same cause from the other side, through the grinding attrition of Teruel and the Ebro, and through the victory that did not feel like a victory because the king did not come and the fueros were abolished and the Church blessed a dictator who had no use for the things the Church was supposed to protect.

On this morning, though, the watching was simple. The ridge was the enemy. The cross was the cause. The beret was on his head. The rifle was in his hands. The war was ahead of him, and the war was what the beret was for.

He watched until Erice relieved him, and then he sat down in the trench and cleaned his rifle and ate a piece of bread that was already stale and drank water from the canteen that tasted of metal, and the morning passed, and the afternoon came, and the war continued, and Martín Javier Echeverría, twenty-two years old, requeté of the tercio of Navarre, son of Don Eusebio, grandson of the Martín who fought in the Third Carlist War, was a soldier.
