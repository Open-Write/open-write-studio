# THE RECKONING — Run 2

The first thing I remember is the smell. Not the sound of the shot, not the sight of the ridge, not the weight of the rifle — the smell. Damp earth and cordite from a practice round someone had fired the night before, and beneath that, the sour reek of men who had been wearing the same clothes for four days. I have read that smell is the sense most closely tied to memory, and I believe it, because even now — even after everything — if I catch the smell of wet dirt I am back in that trench with the beret on my head and the future still intact.

We reached the position on the evening of the twenty-third of July. I know the date because it was the day after the feast of the Magdalene, and I had said the Magdalene's prayer that morning on the road, walking beside Tomás, who was humming a march I did not recognize. The prayer seemed appropriate. The Magdalene was a sinner who became a saint. We were sinners who were going to war to restore the faith. The parallel felt clean and satisfying in a way that I would not be able to sustain for very long.

The trench was a shallow gouge in the hillside. I use the word "trench" because there is no better word, but it was not a trench in the way that the engineers build them — there were no revetments, no fire steps, no traverses. It was a ditch. Men had dug it with shovels and lined the forward edge with stones and filled sandbags with the excavated dirt, and the result was something that would stop a bullet if the bullet was not moving very fast and did not hit the part of the parapet where the sandbags had rotted and collapsed and been replaced by a corrugated sheet of tin that someone had taken from a barn.

Sergeant Erice showed me where to lie. He was a compact man with a mustache that needed trimming and the quiet authority of someone who had been doing this long enough to stop explaining why. "Keep your head down after dark," he said. "They fire at muzzle flashes and sounds. If you need to piss, use the can." He pointed to a tin can at the end of the trench. "If you need to do more than piss, wait until daylight and use the sap."

I did not know what a sap was. I learned the next morning that it was a shallow trench that ran behind the main position to a latrine pit that was screened from enemy view by a pile of dirt and a piece of canvas. The canvas had been stolen from a supply truck, and the supply truck had been stolen from the Republic, and the Republic had received it from the French, who had sold it to both sides because that is what the French do. The war was full of these circuits of theft and reuse, objects that changed hands and allegiances, that carried no loyalty to the cause they served.

I slept badly. The ground was hard and the night was cold — the Castilian meseta loses its heat fast once the sun goes down, and we had no blankets, only our greatcoats and the clothes we wore. Tomás slept beside me with his beret pulled over his face and his hands tucked into his armpits, and he slept well, because Tomás could sleep anywhere, a skill I envied and resented in equal measure.

Before dawn, Erice shook my shoulder. "Your turn on the parapet. Stay low. Watch the ridge. Report anything that moves."

I climbed up. The parapet was waist-high, and I crouched behind it with my rifle across the top and my chin resting on my forearms, and I looked out at the valley for the first time in the light.

It was beautiful. I know that is not what a man is supposed to say about the place where he will try to kill other men, but the valley was beautiful. The light was the color of pale honey and it lay across the fields and the river and the far slope like a cloth, and the ridge on the far side was dark with trees — olives, I thought, or maybe holm oaks — and the buildings on the ridge were the same pale stone as the buildings around Pamplona, and the church tower — yes, it was a church tower — rose above the trees with a cross at its peak that caught the first light and held it.

Somewhere behind those buildings, men were waking up. Men were eating the same hard bread and drinking the same bitter coffee and checking the same rifles. Men who believed — and I have to say this, because it is true, and because the forgetting of it is the thing I most regret — men who believed they were right. Not evil. Not deluded. Right. As right as I was. With the same certainty and the same prayers and the same conviction that God was on their side.

The shot came at full light. The sun was over the ridge by then and the valley was bright and I had been watching for an hour and seen nothing — no movement, no flash, no smoke. The sound was a crack, sharp and immediate, and I heard the bullet pass my head — not the Hollywood whistle, but a flat, vicious snap, like a branch breaking close to the ear. A piece of the parapet jumped. A chip of stone flew up and hit my cheek and drew blood, though I did not feel it until later.

I dropped into the trench. My legs folded under me and I was sitting in the dirt with my back against the wall and my rifle across my knees and my breath coming in short, hard gasps that I could not control. My hands were shaking. I could see them shaking. I put them flat on the dirt floor to make them stop, and the dirt was cool and damp and I pressed my palms into it and waited.

Tomás was there. He did not say anything at first. He sat down beside me and took out a cigarette and lit it and blew the smoke at the sky, and then he said, "The first one is the worst."

"That was the first one," I said.

"I know." He offered me the cigarette. I took it. My hands were still shaking and the cigarette trembled between my fingers and the smoke was hot in my throat and I coughed, and Tomás said, "You get used to it."

I did not believe him. I did not believe that a man could get used to the sound of a bullet passing his head. I did not believe that the body could learn to accept the proximity of its own death as a condition of daily life. I did not believe that the shaking would stop.

It stopped. Not that morning — that morning I sat in the trench for a long time with my hands pressed into the dirt and my heart beating in my throat and the beret hot on my head and the thought, repeating, that someone had tried to kill me and that I was alive, and the aliveness was sharp and vivid in a way that nothing before it had ever been. But by the next day the shaking was less, and by the day after that it was only my hands, and by the end of the week it was nothing. You get used to it. Tomás was right.

The farmhouse across the valley. The church tower with the cross. The bullet that missed. The dirt in my palms. The cigarette that trembled.

I think about that morning now and what I see is the boy I was — twenty-two years old, with a beret his father had worn and a rifle he had been given by a sergeant who would be dead before October and a faith that was complete and undamaged and had not yet been asked to account for itself. The boy is crouching behind a parapet and looking at a church tower across a valley and thinking about the king and the fueros and the crusade and the rightness of what he is doing, and he does not know that the rightness is the thing that will break.

I would like to say that I wish someone had told him. But no one could have told him. And if they had, he would not have listened, because the beret was on his head and the rifle was in his hands and the cause was in his blood and the boy was not built for doubt. He was built for exactly this — for the crouch behind the parapet and the eye on the ridge and the certainty that what he was doing mattered, and that God was watching, and that the king would come.

The king did not come.
