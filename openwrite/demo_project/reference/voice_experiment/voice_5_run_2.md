# THE SPARSE — Run 2

The can was full. He carried it down the sap — a shallow trench, barely knee-deep, screened by a mound of dirt and a piece of canvas that flapped in the morning breeze. Behind the canvas was a pit. He poured the can into the pit and covered it with dirt using a shovel that had been left for this purpose. The shovel was caked with the same dirt and the same purpose. He wiped his hands on his trousers.

The war smelled like this. Not cordite. Not blood. This.

He walked back to the trench. The sun was up. The heat was building. The sandbags were damp. The stones were warm. The corrugated tin shimmered faintly. The trench was thirty meters long and in those thirty meters there were twelve men and the twelve men were doing what men in trenches do in the morning — cleaning rifles, eating bread, drinking water, smoking cigarettes, sleeping, staring at nothing.

Tomás was cleaning the rifle. Tomás always cleaned the rifle. The rag was gray — it had been white once, or at least lighter. The oil was in a small tin bottle. The smell of the oil was sharp and he would never smell it again without thinking of this trench, this morning, the way Tomás's hands moved over the metal with the care of a man polishing a chalice.

"Sleep?" Tomás asked.

"No."

"Nobody does. Not the first night."

He sat down. He took the beret off — the first time since yesterday evening — and wiped the sweatband with his hand and put it back on. The wool was damp. It would be damp all day. It would be damp every day until the rains came in October, and then the damp would become wet and the wet would become cold and the cold would become the thing that killed more men than the bullets.

Erice came. The sergeant stopped. Looked down.

"Parapet."

He picked up the Mauser. Checked the chamber. Loaded. Checked the safety. On. Moved to the firing step. Climbed up. The firing step was a ledge of packed earth, eighteen inches high, wide enough for his boots. He stepped up. The parapet was at his waist. He put his elbows on the stones and the stones were rough and the sandbags were damp and the corrugated tin was warm where the sun touched it.

The valley.

Green fields. A river at the bottom. Thin. Bright. The far slope rising to a ridge. A thousand meters. Maybe more. The air was clear and the clear air made the ridge look close — close enough to see the stones of the wall, the shape of the farmhouse, the outline of the church tower. But the distance was real. The distance was a thousand meters of open ground and green fields and the thin bright river and the air itself.

On the ridge: a farmhouse. Stone. Honey-colored. No roof. The rafters bare. The walls blackened at the top. The fire was recent — the charring was still black, not yet gray, not yet weathered by the seasons.

A wall. Low. Running along the ridge. A gap where stones had fallen. The gap was wide enough for a man to walk through.

A church tower. Pale. Slender. Older than the farmhouse. Older than the wall. The stone was lighter — as if the tower had been kept clean while the other buildings had been allowed to weather.

A cross. At the top.

He rested the rifle on the parapet. Looked through the sights at the cross. The cross was small at this distance. A dark shape against the sky. The sun was behind it and the sun turned the cross to a silhouette and the silhouette was sharp and clear.

He thought: the men behind that wall look at that cross every day.

He thought: the cross is the same cross that hangs above my father's table.

He thought: the cross does not know which side its tower is on.

He watched. The sun moved. The shadows shortened. The heat built. Sweat ran under the beret. Down the temples. Along the jaw. Dripped from the chin onto the stones. The stones absorbed it. Dark circles.

Nothing moved on the ridge. The farmhouse was still. The wall was still. The tower was still. The trees were dark and motionless. The terraced fields were empty. The river glinted at the bottom of the valley, thin as a thread.

A bird. High above the valley. Singing. The song was thin and clear and far away. He did not know what kind of bird. He did not try to find out. He watched the ridge.

He could smell the trench from up here — the earth, the sweat, the gun oil, the faint sweetness of rotting burlap. He could feel the heat building on the back of his neck where the beret's band pressed against the skin. He could hear, from somewhere down the line, the sound of a man coughing. The cough echoed. Then silence.

An hour. The sun was above the ridge now. The light was hard. White. The valley shimmered faintly. The buildings on the ridge shimmered. The cross shimmered.

The shot.

A crack. Flat. Dry. The bullet passed his head. He heard it — a snap, a tearing. The parapet jumped. Stone dust rose. A chip hit his cheek. The sting. The wetness. Blood.

He dropped.

The body went first. The mind followed. He was on his back in the trench. The rifle on his chest. The sky above. Blue. Far. Indifferent.

His hands. He held them up. They shook. The fingers trembled. He could see it — the fine, rapid movement of the muscles, the shaking of the hands that had held the rifle and the shovel and the canteen and the crucifix and the hand of his father on the morning he left.

He put his hands on the dirt. The dirt was cool. He pressed. The dirt held. The shaking slowed.

Tomás was there. Tomás crouched. Tomás's hand on his arm. The grip firm. The hand warm.

"First time?"

"Yes."

"It passes."

He did not believe it. He did not believe the body could learn this. The crack. The snap. The chip. The blood. He did not believe a man could sit in a trench and eat bread and drink water while across a valley someone aimed a rifle at his head.

But he sat up. He picked up the rifle. He checked the chamber. Loaded. He checked the safety. On. He looked at his hands. The shaking was less. He put his hands on the dirt floor of the trench and the dirt was cool and the coolness was a thing to hold onto and he held onto it.

He looked at Tomás. Tomás was sitting against the wall with the rag in his hand and the tin of oil beside him and the cigarette between his lips. Tomás had been here for two weeks. Tomás had seen men come and go. Tomás had heard the shot a hundred times and had dropped a hundred times and had returned to the parapet a hundred times.

"What happens after the first time?" Martín asked.

Tomás smoked. He blew the smoke at the sky. "You watch. You wait. You drop. You get up. You watch again."

"And then?"

"Then they shoot again."

He moved to the firing step.

The ridge. The farmhouse. The wall. The tower. The cross.

He climbed up. He watched.

The sun was higher. The valley was bright. The distance was a thousand meters. The distance was what kept him alive. The distance was what would, eventually, close. That is what wars do. They close distances. They eat the space between men. They bring the rifle and the target together until the bullet does not have to fly so far.

He watched. A lizard ran along the parapet. The lizard stopped. The lizard lifted its head. The lizard ran again. The lizard did not know about the bullet. The lizard did not know about the rifle. The lizard knew the stones and the sun and the heat and the insects and that was enough.

He envied the lizard.

He thought: the bullet crossed a thousand meters in less than a second.

He thought: the next one might not miss.

He thought: I will watch anyway.

He thought about his father. The morning he left. The hand on the shoulder. The weight of it. "You will wear the beret." The words were not a command. They were a description. A fact. The way a man says "the sun rises in the east" or "the river runs to the sea." You will wear the beret. Because that is what the beret is for. Because that is what you are for.

The beret was hot. The rifle was warm. The cross held the light.

He did not take the beret off.
