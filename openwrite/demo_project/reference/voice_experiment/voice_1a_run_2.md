# THE IMMEDIATE RECKONING — Run 2

The first thing I remember is the smell. Damp earth and cordite from a practice round someone had fired the night before, and beneath that, the sour reek of men wearing the same clothes for four days. I am not remembering this from a distance. I am remembering it the way the body remembers — all at once, without sequence, the smell arriving before the image, the image arriving before the sound. The body keeps its own chronology.

I can feel the trench floor against my back right now. The ridges of packed earth. The six points of pressure — skull, shoulder blades, small of the back, hips. I can feel the beret, damp, the wool pressing against my forehead. I can feel the rifle beside me, its weight a fact against my hip. These are not things I am reconstructing. These are things that are still here.

We reached the position on the evening of July twenty-third. The day after the Magdalene. I had said her prayer on the road — the sinner who became a saint — and the prayer seemed appropriate and the appropriateness was a comfort I would not be able to sustain.

The trench was a ditch. Shallow. Crumbling. The parapet stones and sandbags, the sandbags rotted in places and restuffed with dirt. A corrugated sheet of tin where the bags had collapsed. The whole thing smelled of damp earth and old tobacco and something else — the smell of men who have stopped believing they will leave a place.

Erice showed me where to lie. Compact man. Mustache. The quiet authority of someone who has stopped explaining. "Keep your head down after dark. They fire at muzzle flashes and sounds." He pointed to a tin can. "Piss in that. Anything else, wait for daylight and use the sap."

The sap was a shallow trench behind the main position to a latrine pit screened by dirt and canvas. The canvas stolen from a supply truck. The supply truck stolen from the Republic. The Republic had received it from the French, who sold it to both sides. The war was full of these circuits.

I slept badly. The ground. The cold. Tomás beside me, sleeping well — beret over face, hands in armpits — and I envied him and resented the envy in equal measure.

Before dawn, Erice. "Your turn. Watch the ridge."

I climbed up. Crouched. Looked.

The valley was green. After three days of dust — the white road, the yellow earth, the brown hills — the green hit me like water. Fields running down to a river. The far slope rising to a ridge dark with trees. Buildings on the ridge. A church tower. A cross at its peak, catching the first light.

I am writing this in the back room of the hardware store. The same table where my father kept his ledgers. The same inkwell. The same chair with the broken arm. And as I write I am not here — I am there, behind the parapet, with the light on my face and the cross on the tower and the future still intact. The pen moves and the boy moves with it. The distance collapses. I am twenty-two again and the beret is on my head and the rifle is in my hands and I believe.

Somewhere behind those buildings, men were waking. Men who believed they were right. As right as I was. With the same certainty, the same prayers, the same conviction that God was on their side. I have to say this. I have to say it now, while the saying costs something, before the years of retelling have sanded the edges smooth.

The shot came at full light. A crack — sharp, immediate. The bullet passed my head. Not a whistle. A snap. Flat, vicious. A chip of the parapet hit my cheek. Blood.

I dropped. My legs folded. I was in the dirt — back against the wall, rifle across my knees, breath in short hard gasps. My hands shaking. I put them flat on the dirt. Cool. Damp. I pressed.

Tomás sat beside me. Cigarette. Smoke at the sky. "The first one is the worst."

"That was the first one."

"I know." He offered the cigarette. I took it. My hands shook and the cigarette trembled and the smoke was hot and I coughed, and Tomás said, "You get used to it."

I am writing this and my hands are steady. The hardware store is quiet. The inkwell is full. The chair is uncomfortable, the way my father said a chair should be. And I want to tell the boy behind the parapet: Tomás was right. You do get used to it. The shaking stops. The body learns. And that is the worst of it — not that the fear stays, but that it goes. Because when the fear goes, the numbness comes, and the numbness is the thing that lets a man do what the war will ask him to do.

I did not know that then. I knew the rifle. I knew the beret. I knew the farmhouse across the valley where someone who believed in a different Spain was looking through a sight at the place where I stood.

I put my eye to the parapet again.

The boy is there. He is looking at the cross on the tower. He is thinking about the king and the fueros and the crusade. He is thinking about his father's hand on his shoulder and the words: "You will wear the beret." He does not know that the rightness is the thing that will break. He does not know that the king will not come. He does not know that in three years he will be sitting at a table in a hardware store trying to write this down, and that the writing will be harder than the fighting, because the fighting required only the body and the writing requires the thing the body cannot provide: the willingness to look at what happened and call it what it was.

I am looking. I am calling it.

The king did not come.
