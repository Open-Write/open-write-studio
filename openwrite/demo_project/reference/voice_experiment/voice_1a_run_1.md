# THE IMMEDIATE RECKONING — Run 1

I did not sleep. I am trying to be honest about why, and the honesty requires me to say: it was not the ground and it was not the cold. It was the dark itself — the specific darkness of a trench at the front, which is not like the dark of a bedroom or a church or a road at night. This dark had weight. This dark had the weight of the ridge above us and the valley below us and the men on the far slope who were, at that moment, also not sleeping, and also listening, and also waiting for the morning that would make us visible to each other.

I can feel it now — the packed earth against my back, the ridges pressing into the base of my skull and my shoulder blades and the small of my back. I can feel the beret on my head, the wool damp with sweat that had not yet cooled. I can feel the rifle beside me, its barrel pointing away from the sleeping men, the way the training manual said. I can feel all of this as I sit here writing, and the feeling is not memory. The feeling is presence. The body does not forget. The body keeps the trench.

We arrived on the evening of July twenty-third. I know the date because it was the day after the feast of the Magdalene, and I had said the Magdalene's prayer on the road, walking beside Tomás, who was humming a march. The prayer seemed right. The Magdalene was a sinner who became a saint. We were sinners going to war to restore the faith. The parallel was clean. I held onto it.

The trench was a ditch. I use the word "trench" because there is no better word, but what I mean is a shallow gouge in the hillside — no revetments, no fire steps, no traverses. Men had dug it with shovels and lined the forward edge with stones and filled sandbags with the excavated dirt, and the result was something that would stop a bullet if the bullet was not moving very fast and did not hit the part of the parapet where the sandbags had rotted and been replaced by a sheet of corrugated tin from a barn.

Sergeant Erice showed me my position. A compact man with a mustache that needed trimming. "Keep your head down after dark," he said. "They fire at muzzle flashes and sounds." He pointed to a tin can at the end of the trench. "If you need to piss, use the can. If you need to do more, wait for daylight."

I lay down. The ground was hard. The night was cold — the Castilian meseta loses its heat fast. Tomás slept beside me with his beret over his face and his hands tucked into his armpits. He slept well. I envied him. I resented the envy.

Before dawn, Erice shook my shoulder. "Your turn. Watch the ridge."

I climbed up. The parapet was waist-high. I crouched behind it with my rifle across the top and my chin on my forearms, and I looked.

The valley was beautiful. I know that is not what a man is supposed to say about the place where he will try to kill other men, but it was — the light the color of pale honey, lying across the fields and the river and the far slope like a cloth. The ridge dark with trees. The buildings the same pale stone as the buildings around Pamplona. And the church tower — yes, it was a church tower — rising above the trees with a cross at its peak that caught the first light and held it.

Somewhere behind those buildings, men were waking up. Eating the same hard bread. Drinking the same bitter coffee. Checking the same rifles. Men who believed — and I have to say this now, because I am saying it while I still feel the weight of it, while the feeling has not yet been smoothed by the years of telling — men who believed they were right. Not evil. Not deluded. Right. As right as I was.

The shot came at full light. The sun was over the ridge and the valley was bright and I had been watching for an hour. The sound was a crack — sharp, immediate — and I heard the bullet pass my head. Not the Hollywood whistle. A flat, vicious snap. A piece of the parapet jumped. A chip of stone hit my cheek.

I dropped. My legs folded. I was sitting in the dirt with my back against the wall and my rifle across my knees and my breath coming in short, hard gasps I could not control. My hands shook. I could see them shaking. I put them flat on the dirt floor and the dirt was cool and damp and I pressed my palms into it.

Tomás sat down beside me. He took out a cigarette. Lit it. Blew the smoke at the sky. Then: "The first one is the worst."

"That was the first one," I said.

"I know." He offered me the cigarette. I took it. My hands shook and the cigarette trembled between my fingers and the smoke was hot in my throat and I coughed, and Tomás said, "You get used to it."

I did not believe him. I did not believe that a man could get used to the sound of a bullet passing his head. I did not believe that the body could learn to accept the proximity of its own death as a condition of daily life.

I am sitting here now and I can tell you: it stopped. Not that morning. That morning I sat in the trench for a long time with my hands pressed into the dirt and my heart in my throat and the beret hot on my head and the thought, repeating, that someone had tried to kill me and that I was alive, and the aliveness was sharp in a way that nothing before it had been. But by the next day the shaking was less. By the day after, it was only my hands. By the end of the week, nothing.

The farmhouse across the valley. The church tower with the cross. The bullet that missed. The dirt in my palms. The cigarette that trembled.

I look at the boy behind the parapet and I want to reach through the years and put my hand on his shoulder and say: the rightness you feel — the certainty, the completeness of it — that is the thing that will break. Not the bullets. Not the cold. Not the hunger. The rightness. The faith that has not yet been asked to account for itself.

But I cannot reach him. He would not listen. The beret is on his head and the rifle is in his hands and the cause is in his blood and he is not built for doubt. He is built for exactly this — the crouch behind the parapet and the eye on the ridge and the certainty that God is watching and the king will come.

The king did not come.
