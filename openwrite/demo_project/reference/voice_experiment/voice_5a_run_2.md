# THE WARMER SPARSE — Run 2

The can was full. He carried it down the sap — a shallow trench, barely knee-deep, screened by a mound of dirt and a piece of canvas. Behind the canvas was a pit. He poured the can into the pit and covered it with dirt. The shovel was caked. He wiped his hands on his trousers.

The war smelled like this. Not cordite. Not blood. This.

He walked back to the trench. The sun was up. The heat was building. The sandbags were damp. The stones were warm. Twelve men in thirty meters of trench, doing what men do in the morning — cleaning rifles, eating bread, staring at nothing.

Tomás was cleaning the rifle. Tomás always cleaned the rifle. The rag had been white once. The oil was in a small tin bottle. The smell was sharp and he would never smell it again without thinking of this trench, this morning, the way Tomás's hands moved over the metal with care.

"Sleep?" Tomás asked.

"No."

"Nobody does. Not the first night."

He sat down. He took the beret off and wiped the sweatband and put it back on. The wool was damp.

Tomás looked at him. Not a long look. A glance — the kind a man gives when he is deciding whether to say something or let it pass. Tomás let it pass. He went back to the rifle. The rag moved over the bolt. The metal shone.

Erice came. The sergeant stopped. Looked down.

"Parapet."

He picked up the Mauser. Checked the chamber. Loaded. Checked the safety. On. Moved to the firing step. Climbed up. The parapet was at his waist. He put his elbows on the stones.

The valley.

Green fields. A river at the bottom. Thin. Bright. The far slope rising to a ridge. A thousand meters. The air was clear and the clear air made the ridge look close.

On the ridge: a farmhouse. Stone. No roof. The rafters bare. The walls blackened at the top where fire had been.

A wall. Low. Running along the ridge. A gap where stones had fallen.

A church tower. Pale. Slender. A cross at the top.

He rested the rifle on the parapet. Looked through the sights at the cross. The cross was small at this distance. A dark shape against the sky.

He thought: the men behind that wall look at that cross every day.

He thought: the cross is the same cross that hangs above my father's table.

He thought: the cross does not know which side its tower is on.

He watched. The sun moved. The shadows shortened. Sweat ran under the beret. Down the temples. Along the jaw. Dripped from the chin onto the stones.

Nothing moved on the ridge. The farmhouse was still. The wall was still. The tower was still.

A lizard ran along the parapet. It stopped. It lifted its head. It looked at him. It did not know about the bullet. It knew the stones and the sun and the insects and that was enough.

He thought about Tomás. The way Tomás had handed him bread on the road without looking. The way Tomás slept with his beret over his face and his hands in his armpits, curled small, as if making himself less of a target even in sleep. The way Tomás cleaned the rifle the way a woman irons a shirt — with patience, with repetition, with the understanding that the act itself was the point.

An hour. The sun was above the ridge. The light was hard. White. The valley shimmered.

The shot.

A crack. Flat. Dry. The bullet passed his head. He heard it — a snap, a tearing. The parapet jumped. Stone dust. A chip hit his cheek. The sting. Blood.

He dropped.

The body went first. The mind followed. He was on his back in the trench. The rifle on his chest. The sky above. Blue. Far. Indifferent.

His hands. He held them up. They shook. The fingers trembled. He could see the fine, rapid movement of the muscles. He put his hands on the dirt. The dirt was cool. He pressed. The shaking slowed.

Tomás was there. Tomás crouched. Tomás's hand on his arm. The grip firm. The hand warm.

"First time?"

"Yes."

"It passes."

He did not believe it. He sat up. He looked at his hands. The shaking was less. He put his hands on the dirt floor and the dirt was cool and the coolness was a thing to hold onto and he held onto it.

He looked at Tomás. Tomás was sitting against the wall with the rag in his hand and the cigarette between his lips.

"What happens after the first time?" Martín asked.

Tomás smoked. He blew the smoke at the sky. "You watch. You wait. You drop. You get up. You watch again."

"And then?"

"Then they shoot again."

Tomás paused. He looked at Martín — not a glance this time, but a look. The look lasted two seconds. Three. In it was something that was not comfort and was not advice. It was the look of a man saying: I will be here. I will be here when you get up. That is all I can do.

He moved to the firing step. Climbed up.

The ridge. The farmhouse. The wall. The tower. The cross.

He watched.

The distance was a thousand meters. The distance was what kept him alive. The distance was what would, eventually, close. That is what wars do. They close distances. They eat the space between men.

He thought: the bullet crossed a thousand meters in less than a second.

He thought: the next one might not miss.

He thought: I will watch anyway.

He thought about his father. The morning he left. The hand on the shoulder. "You will wear the beret." The words were a way of saying what his father could not say. The beret was the sentence his father's mouth could not form. Wear this. Remember me. Come back.

The beret was hot. The rifle was warm. The cross held the light.

He did not take the beret off.
