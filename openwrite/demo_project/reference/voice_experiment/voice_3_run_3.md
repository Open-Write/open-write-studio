# THE CHRONICLER — Run 3

The war that would destroy the Spanish Republic and create the Franco dictatorship began on July 17, 1936, with a military rising in Spanish Morocco. Within hours it had spread to the mainland. In Pamplona, the capital of Navarre and the spiritual home of the Carlist movement, the rising was received not as a coup but as a crusade. The Carlists had been waiting for this moment for a century — since the First Carlist War of 1833, since the Second of 1846, since the Third of 1872, since the failed risings of 1900 and 1909 and 1919. The red beret, the symbol of the requeté, was taken from its hooks and its boxes and its cedar closets and placed on the heads of men who had been taught from birth that this was what the beret was for.

Martín Javier Echeverría was one of those men. He was twenty-two, the second son of Don Eusebio Echeverría, a Carlist elder and former requeté who ran a hardware store on the Calle Mayor in Pamplona and who had been preparing his sons for this war since they were old enough to understand the stories of their grandfather, who had fought in the Third Carlist War and whose wooden crucifix hung above the dining table as a relic and a promise. Martín had studied at the seminary for two years before discovering that he was not called to the priesthood, and had worked in his father's store since, selling nails and hinges and rope to the men of Pamplona and playing pelota on Sundays and attending Mass every morning and living the life of a young Carlist in a Carlist town — a life shaped by faith and tradition and the certainty that the cause was just and the king would come.

On July 18, the day after the rising, Martín enlisted. He walked into the recruitment office on the Calle de la Merced, gave his name and his father's name and the name of his parish, and was issued a red beret and a Mauser 98 rifle and a place in the tercio of Navarre. He did not say goodbye to his father in any formal sense — there was no scene at the door, no embrace, no tears. His father's hand came to rest on his shoulder and stayed there, and his father said, "You will wear the beret," and Martín said, "Yes," and that was the whole of it. The Carlist catechism does not include a chapter on farewell.

The requeté marched south on July 19. They reached the railhead at Logroño and from there marched on foot toward the front lines that were forming in the valleys and ridges of central Castile. The march took three days. The road was white with dust and the sun was the feast of Santiago and the men sang the requeté hymn in the first hour and fell silent in the third and did not sing again. By the second day the berets were the color of the road and by the third day the men were the color of the road and the only red left was the flash of the beret's lining when the wind caught it.

They reached the position on the evening of July 23. The position was a trench — a shallow, poorly constructed excavation on the forward slope of a hill overlooking a valley. The parapet was built of loose stones and sandbags and corrugated tin and stolen canvas. The floor was dirt. There was a tin can for a latrine and a sap trench that led to a pit and a shovel that had been left by the previous unit. The trench faced west across the valley toward a ridge approximately one thousand meters distant.

On the ridge was the enemy. The Republic had established a defensive position among the buildings of an abandoned farmhouse and a low stone wall and a church tower. The farmhouse had been damaged by fire. The wall had been breached. The church tower was intact, its cross catching the evening light when the requeté arrived.

Martín did not sleep. The ground was hard and the night was cold and the sounds of the trench — the coughing, the shifting, the occasional shot from a sentry — kept him on the surface of rest. He lay with his beret on his head and his rifle beside him and his blanket pulled to his chin and he listened to the war, and the war was quiet.

At dawn, Sergeant Erice — a compact man from Estella with a mustache and the bearing of a career soldier — assigned Martín to the morning watch. Martín climbed the firing step and put his elbows on the parapet and looked out at the valley.

The valley was green. The fields were terraced, divided by low stone walls, planted with olives and grain. A river ran along the bottom, thin and bright, catching the early light. The far ridge rose steeply, dark with trees, and on the ridge the buildings of the enemy position were visible in the strengthening light: the farmhouse, the wall, the church tower, the cross.

He watched. The sun rose. The heat built. Sweat formed under the beret and ran down his temples. He watched the buildings and the trees and the terraced fields and the river, and nothing moved, and the stillness was the second lesson: the war is mostly waiting.

At seven hundred hours — the time is approximate, as no one in the trench had a reliable watch — a shot was fired from the enemy position. The bullet crossed the valley in approximately one second and passed Martín's head at a distance he estimated at less than half a meter. It struck the parapet to his left and dislodged a chip of stone that struck his left cheek and drew blood.

He dropped into the trench. The fall was not voluntary — the body's response to the proximity of a bullet is older than training, older than discipline, older than the catechism. He landed on his back with the rifle across his chest and his hands shaking and his heart rate elevated to a degree that he felt in his temples and his wrists and the hollow of his throat.

Tomás — who will be referred to throughout by his Christian name, as was the custom of the requeté — crouched beside him and waited and then offered a cigarette. Martín took it. His hands shook. The cigarette trembled. The smoke was harsh and he coughed and Tomás said: "The first one is the worst."

It was not the worst. Martín would learn this in the months and years to come — at Guadalajara, where the Republic's Russian tanks broke the Carlist lines; at the northern campaign, where he fought Basque Catholics who prayed to the same God and carried the same crucifixes and believed they were right; at Teruel, where the cold killed men in their sleep; at the Ebro, where the water ran red and Tomás died with his hand on Martín's arm and his beret over his face. The first shot was not the worst. The worst was the accumulation — the thousand small erosions of faith and certainty and the body's willingness to go on — that would follow.

But on the morning of July 24, sitting in a trench with the cigarette trembling in his hand and the blood drying on his cheek and the beret hot on his head, Martín did not know this. He knew the rifle. He knew the beret. He knew the cross on the church tower across the valley, and he knew the prayer that he said under his breath — "Lord, who hast called us to this crusade" — and he knew that the shaking would stop, because Tomás had said it would, and Tomás was a man who had been here for two weeks, and two weeks was, at that moment, an eternity.

The shaking stopped. He stood up. He moved to the firing step. He climbed up. He put his elbows on the parapet.

The ridge was there. The farmhouse was there. The wall was there. The church tower was there. The cross was there.

He watched. The morning passed. The sun climbed. The heat built. The valley filled with light and the light was hard and white and the shadows shortened to nothing and the buildings on the ridge shimmered in the heat and the cross on the tower was a dark shape against the sky.

He would watch this valley for three weeks before the order came to advance. He would cross it at night, in a column of men who did not speak, carrying the rifle and the beret and the crucifix that his father had given him the morning he left, and he would take the ridge and the farmhouse and the church tower, and the cross would still be there, and the men who had defended it would be dead or captured or fled, and the requeté would hold the position for three days before the Republic counterattacked and they fell back to the trench and the whole process began again.

This was the war. This was the crusade. This was the morning it became real — not in the hymns and the blessings and the three days of marching, but in the crack of a bullet and the chip of stone and the blood on the cheek and the shaking of the hands and the return, the deliberate and conscious return, to the parapet.

Martín watched. The cross held the light.
