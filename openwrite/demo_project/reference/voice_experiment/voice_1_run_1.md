# THE RECKONING — Run 1

I did not sleep. I tell myself now that I did not sleep because the ground was hard and the night was cold and the men around me were snoring, but that is a cleaner reason than the truth. The truth is that I lay on my back with my beret on my head and my blanket pulled to my chin and I listened to the dark for the sound of the war, and the war was quiet, and the quiet was worse than any sound I had imagined.

We had marched three days from the railhead at Logroño. The road south was white with dust and the sun was the feast of Santiago and the men sang the requeté hymn in the first hour and fell silent in the third. By the second day the dust had turned our berets from red to the color of a dead man's skin, and by the third day I could not tell my sweat from the canteen water I poured over my wrists. Tomás walked beside me and did not complain, and I was grateful for that, and I was ashamed of my gratitude, because a requeté should not need the silence of another man to keep his back straight.

We arrived at the line in the last light. The position was a shallow trench cut into the side of a hill that faced west across a valley — I did not know the name of the valley then, and I will not name it now, because the name is not the point. The point is that the trench was not deep enough to stand in and the parapet was built from loose stones and sandbags that had been opened by rain and restuffed with dirt, and the whole thing smelled of damp earth and old tobacco and something else that I would learn, in the weeks to come, was the smell of men who have stopped believing they will leave a place.

The sergeant — his name was Erice, a man from Estella with a face like a boot — showed me my position and told me to keep my head below the parapet until morning and not to fire at anything unless he gave the order. I asked him where the enemy was. He pointed across the valley with his chin and said, "Over there. You will see them in the morning." He said it the way my father said "the store is on the next street" — with the tone of a man stating something so obvious it did not warrant emphasis.

I lay down. I put my rifle beside me with the barrel pointing away from the sleeping men, the way the training manual said. I put my beret over my face to keep the insects off. The wool smelled of dust and sweat and, faintly, of the cedar closet in the hallway of my father's house in Pamplona where it had hung for two years waiting for this.

I did not sleep.

In the morning — the light came early, the way it does in Castile, sudden and without the soft gradations of the Navarrese hills — I sat up and looked at the trench in daylight for the first time. It was worse than I had thought. The walls were crumbling. The floor was packed earth that had turned to mud in the recent rain and dried into ridges that pressed into my back through the blanket. There were three other men in my section of the trench: Tomás, who was already awake and cleaning his rifle with a rag that had once been a shirt; a boy from Tudela whose name I would learn was Ignacio, who was still sleeping with his mouth open; and an older man whose name I never learned, because he was transferred out two days later and replaced by someone whose name I also did not learn. The war would do this — it would fill the spaces around you with men whose names you did not know, and you would stop asking.

I put on my beret. I checked the rifle. I climbed to the parapet.

The valley below was green — greener than I had expected, after three days of dust. There was a river at the bottom, thin as a vein, and on the far slope the land rose toward a ridge that was maybe a thousand meters away. I could see structures on the ridge: a farmhouse, a wall, what might have been a church tower, though it was hard to tell at that distance. The structures had been damaged. The farmhouse was missing its roof. The wall had a gap in it that was too regular for collapse.

That was the enemy position.

I stared at it. I had expected — I do not know what I had expected. Something more dramatic. Something that would look like the enemy. Instead it looked like a farm. It looked like the farms I had seen from the road my whole life, the stone buildings that dotted the hills around Pamplona, the places where men milked goats and stored grain and hung hams from the rafters. The only difference was the gap in the wall and the missing roof and the fact that somewhere behind those structures there were men who wanted to kill me.

I thought about the king. I thought about Don Jaime, who was in exile, and about the promise that when the war was won the king would return and the fueros would be restored and Spain would be what it had been before the liberals and the masons and the atheists had torn it down. I thought about my father's face when I told him I had enlisted — the way he did not smile, because this was not a thing to smile about, but the way his hand came to rest on my shoulder and stayed there, heavy and warm, and the way he said, "You will wear the beret." Not "you will fight well" or "you will make me proud" or any of the things a father might say. You will wear the beret. As if the beret were the whole of it. As if the wool and the tradition it carried were enough.

I was still looking at the enemy position when the shot came.

The sound was not what I expected. I had heard gunfire before — hunting in the hills above Pamplona, the feast of San Fermín when the men fired blanks into the air to start the encierro. But this was different. This was a single crack, sharp and flat, that seemed to come from the air itself rather than from the ridge, and it was followed by a sound I had never heard: the hiss of a bullet passing close enough to feel the displacement of air against my cheek.

I dropped. My body dropped before my mind told it to. I was on the floor of the trench with my hands over my head and my heart hammering against my ribs and the taste of dirt in my mouth, and I did not remember falling.

Tomás was beside me. He had his hand on my shoulder — the same hand, the same shoulder, the same weight as my father's, and I hated myself for the comparison.

"Are you hit?" he asked.

"No."

"Then get up. They shoot at everyone the first time. It means nothing."

I got up. My hands were shaking. I put them on the rifle to steady them. I looked at the ridge again and the farmhouse was still there and the gap in the wall was still there and nothing had changed except that now there was a man behind that wall or that ridge who had aimed a rifle at the place where my head had been and pulled the trigger, and the bullet had missed, and the missing was not skill or protection or divine providence. It was distance and wind and the fact that a thousand meters is a long way to shoot straight.

I adjusted my beret. The wool was hot from the sun. I could feel the sweat on my forehead where the band had been.

This was the front. This was the crusade. This was the place where the hymns and the blessings and the three days of marching had been leading, and it looked like a farm and it sounded like a crack in the air and it felt like dirt in my mouth and my own hands refusing to be still.

I did not know, then, what I know now. I did not know that the farmhouse on the ridge would be taken and retaken three times in the next month, or that the boy from Tudela — Ignacio, who had been sleeping with his mouth open — would be dead in nine days, or that the red beret on my head would have to bear a symbol that was not the cross of Burgundy by the following February. I did not know that the king would not come. I did not know that the fueros would be abolished. I did not know that the Church would bless a man who had no use for the things the Church was supposed to stand for.

I knew the rifle. I knew the beret. I knew the farmhouse across the valley where someone who believed in a different Spain than mine was looking through a sight at the place where I stood.

I put my eye to the parapet again. This time I kept it there.
