# THE PRESENT TENSE — Run 2

Dust on the rifle. Dust on the bread. Dust on the wool of the beret where it rests on the parapet beside his head. Martín lies on his back in the trench and looks at the sky and the sky is the color of a washed shirt — pale, clean, without cloud. It is the twenty-fourth of July and the war is six days old and the trench smells of dirt and sweat and the tin of the latrine can and the gun oil on Tomás's rag.

He did not sleep. The ground was hard and the night was cold and the sounds of the position — the coughing, the shifting, the occasional crack of a rifle from somewhere down the line where a man on watch fired at a shadow or a noise or nothing at all — kept him on the surface of sleep without letting him sink into it. He lay with his beret on his head because his father had told him that a requeté sleeps in his beret, and his father had worn his beret for two decades, and the beret was the cause and the cause was the beret and the two could not be separated.

Light comes. It comes the way it does on the meseta — not gradually, not with the soft pinks and grays of the Navarrese hills, but all at once, as if someone has lifted a curtain. The trench walls go from black to brown to the pale dry color of old paper. The stones of the parapet emerge. The sandbags, rotted and restuffed, take shape. The men in the trench become visible: Tomás, sitting up, already cleaning the rifle; Ignacio, the boy from Tudela, still asleep with his mouth open; an older man whose name Martín does not know, sitting against the wall with his eyes closed and his rosary in his hands.

Martín sits up. His spine pops. His neck is stiff. He rolls his head and the joints crack and the sound is loud in the quiet. He reaches for the canteen and drinks. The water is warm and metallic and he drinks again and wipes his mouth with the back of his hand and the hand comes away dirty.

Erice is moving through the trench. The sergeant walks with a slight crouch, his head below the parapel line, his boots finding the same spots on the same stones with the unconscious precision of a man who has walked this trench every morning for weeks. He stops at Martín's section.

"You. Parapet. Watch the ridge."

Martín picks up the Mauser. The rifle is long and heavy and the barrel is cool in the early air. He moves to the firing step and climbs up and puts his elbows on the parapet and looks.

Green. After three days of white road and yellow dust, the valley is green. The fields on the far slope are terraced — low stone walls dividing the hillside into strips of cultivation, olives on the upper slopes, something lower that might be wheat or might be fallow. A river runs at the bottom of the valley, thin and bright, catching the new light. The far ridge is a thousand meters away, maybe twelve hundred, and on the ridge there are buildings.

A farmhouse. Square, stone, without a roof — the rafters are visible, dark lines against the sky, and the walls are blackened at the top where fire has been. A wall running along the ridge, low, built of the same stone as the farmhouse. A gap in the wall where the stones have fallen or been pushed. And behind the wall, rising above it, a church tower — slender, pale, with a cross at its peak that holds the first sunlight like a cup holds water.

He stares at the church tower. He thinks about the church in Pamplona — the Iglesia de San Nicolás, where he was baptized, where he served Mass as a boy, where his father sat in the third pew from the front every Sunday and holy day for forty years. The tower across the valley is not the same tower. The cross is not the same cross. But the shape is the same, and the purpose is the same, and the thought comes to him — unbidden, unwelcome, quickly pushed away — that the men behind that wall worship the same God he does, and the cross on that tower means the same thing to them that it means to him.

He watches. Minutes pass. The sun clears the ridge and the valley fills with light and the shadows shorten and the heat begins. Sweat forms on his forehead under the beret. He can feel it — a slow welling at the hairline, a trickle down the temple, the dampness where the wool band meets the skin. He does not take the beret off. A requeté does not remove his beret on watch.

Movement. He tenses. His hand tightens on the rifle. His eyes fix on a point on the far ridge where something has changed — a shadow, a shape, something that was not there before. He stares. The shape resolves: a man, standing behind the wall, looking in his direction. The man is too far away to see clearly — a shape, a silhouette, a dark figure against the pale stone. The man raises something to his face. Binoculars. A telescope. A rifle scope.

Martín does not move. He does not breathe. He watches the man on the ridge and the man on the ridge watches him and the distance between them is a thousand meters of green valley and bright river and the air itself, which is thick with heat and light and the weight of the thing that is about to happen.

The shot comes.

The sound is a crack — not a boom, not a thunder, but a crack, sharp and dry, like a stick broken over a knee. He hears the bullet before he understands it: a snap, a zip, a sound like the air being torn, and then the crack of the rifle echoing off the valley walls, and then silence. The parapet jumps. A chip of stone flies from the wall six inches from his face. He feels the chip hit his cheek — a sting, a wetness, a drop of blood that runs down toward his jaw.

He drops. The body folds — knees, waist, shoulders — and he is in the trench on his back and the sky is above him and the sky is blue and empty and the rifle is across his chest and his hands are shaking and his heart is a fist beating against the inside of his ribs.

He tastes dirt. He smells earth. He feels the beret on his head — still there, still on, pulled down by the fall but not off. He reaches up and adjusts it. The wool is hot.

Tomás crouches beside him. Tomás's face is calm. Tomás has been here for two weeks and the calm is the calm of experience, not the calm of indifference — Martín can see it in the eyes, which are watchful and steady, and in the hands, which are relaxed on the rifle.

"First time?"

"Yes."

"It passes."

"What passes?"

"The shaking."

Martín looks at his hands. The shaking is real — a tremor in the fingers, a vibration in the wrists, the fine oscillation of muscles that have been told by the body that they should run and have been told by the mind that they should stay. He presses his hands against the dirt floor. The dirt is cool. The coolness is steadying.

He sits up. He looks at the rifle. He checks the chamber. Loaded. He checks the safety. On. He moves to the firing step. He climbs up. He puts his elbows on the parapet.

The farmhouse is there. The wall is there. The church tower is there. The cross is there. The man with the binoculars — or the scope — is gone, or has moved, or is behind the wall where Martín cannot see him.

The valley is green and bright and the river is silver and the distance is a thousand meters and the distance is what is keeping him alive and the distance is what will, eventually, be crossed, because that is what wars do — they close distances, they eat the space between men, they bring the rifle and the target together until the bullet does not have to fly so far.

He watches. The sun climbs. The heat builds. Sweat runs down his back and his chest and his legs and the wool of the beret is damp against his forehead. He watches the ridge and the ridge watches him and neither of them moves and the war, which is six days old, settles into the rhythm it will keep for months: the watch, the wait, the shot, the drop, the return to the parapet.

He does not take the beret off.
