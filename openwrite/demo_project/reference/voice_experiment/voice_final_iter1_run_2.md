# THE IMMEDIATE RECKONING — Round 3, Iteration 1, Run 2

The inkwell is full. I filled it this morning before I sat down, the way my father taught me — the funnel, the slow pour, the wipe of the rim with the rag. The rag is the same rag. The funnel is the same funnel. The ink is different — I buy it from the shop on the Calle Estafeta now, not from the Frenchman my father used — but the ritual is the same and the ritual is the point. The ritual says: you are here, at this table, with this pen, and the thing you are about to do has a cost.

I am writing about the trench. I am writing about the first day. And the inkwell is full and the pen is ready and my hands are steady and I want to say: my hands were not steady then.

We reached the position on the evening of July twenty-third. The day after the Magdalene. On the road I had said her prayer — the sinner who became a saint — and Tomás was humming a march beside me and the dust was white on our boots and the sun was on our necks and the prayer seemed like a thing I could hold. A coin in the pocket. Something to reach for.

The trench was a ditch. I keep calling it a trench because that is the word the manuals use, but what I mean is a gouge in the hillside — no revetments, no fire steps, no traverses. Sandbags along the forward edge, rotted in places, restuffed with dirt. A sheet of corrugated tin where the bags had collapsed. The tin rattled when the wind came from the valley. At night the rattling sounded like someone trying to get in.

Erice showed me where to lie. Compact man. Mustache. The kind of authority that does not need to raise its voice. "Keep your head down after dark. They fire at muzzle flashes and sounds." He pointed to a tin can. "Piss in that. Anything else, wait for daylight and use the sap."

The sap was a shallow trench behind the main position to a latrine pit screened by dirt and canvas. The canvas stolen from a supply truck. The supply truck stolen from the Republic. The Republic had received it from the French, who sold it to both sides. The war ran on these circuits — theft and resale and theft again, the same matériel changing hands across the same lines, the same canvas covering the same holes in the same dirt.

Tomás slept well. Beret over face, hands in armpits. The body of a man who had decided that sleep was a thing he was owed and would collect. I lay beside him and listened to the dark and the dark was not silent — it was full of small sounds, the tin rattling, someone coughing further down the line, the insects in the grass above the parapet. The dark had layers. I learned that later. The dark of the trench was not the dark of the road was not the dark of the church. Each dark had its own weight and its own sounds and its own particular way of pressing on the chest.

Before dawn, Erice. "Your turn. Watch the ridge."

I climbed up. The parapet was waist-high. I crouched behind it with my rifle across the top and my chin on my forearms and I looked.

The valley was green. After three days of dust — the white road, the yellow earth, the brown hills — the green was the first honest thing. Fields running down to a river. The far slope rising to a ridge dark with trees. Buildings on the ridge. A church tower. A cross at its peak, catching the first light.

I am at the table now. The pen is in my hand. The inkwell is full. And I am not here — I am there, behind the parapet, with the light on my face and the cross on the tower and the rifle under my chin and the beret on my head and the future still intact. The pen moves and the boy moves with it. I can feel his hands on the stone. I can feel the cold of the parapet through his palms. I can feel the weight of the beret — not heavy, but present, the wool pressing against the forehead the way a hand presses against a wound. The distance collapses. Not because I am remembering. Because the body never left.

Somewhere behind those buildings, men were waking. Eating the same hard bread. Drinking the same bitter coffee. Checking the same rifles. Men who believed they were right. Not evil. Not deluded. Right. As right as I was. I have to say this while the saying costs something — before the years of retelling turn them into targets and make the war into a clean line between right and wrong.

The shot came at full light. A crack — sharp, immediate. The bullet passed my head. Not a whistle. A snap. Flat, vicious. A chip of the parapet hit my cheek. Blood.

I dropped. My legs folded. I was sitting in the dirt with my back against the wall and my rifle across my knees and my breath coming in short hard gasps I could not control. My hands shook. I pressed them flat on the dirt floor. The dirt was cool and damp and I pressed my palms into it and the dirt took the shaking the way the water takes a stone — not by stopping it but by absorbing it, by letting it sink.

Tomás sat down beside me. Cigarette. Smoke at the sky. "The first one is the worst."

"That was the first one."

"I know." He offered the cigarette. I took it. My hands shook and the cigarette trembled between my fingers and the smoke was hot in my throat and I coughed, and Tomás said, "You get used to it."

I did not believe him. I want to be precise about this — I did not believe him, and the not-believing was its own kind of innocence. Because I thought the shaking was the truth. I thought the body's refusal — the trembling hands, the short breath, the heart in the throat — was the honest response to what had happened. I did not understand that the body's refusal would end. That the shaking would stop. That the hands would steady. That the breath would slow. That the heart would return to its place. And that the return was the wound. The numbness came not as relief but as surrender — the body's admission that this was the world now, and the world included bullets, and bullets were not exceptional, and a man could live in the same space as his own death and eat his bread and smoke his cigarette and sleep his sleep.

I am writing this at the table. The inkwell is full. The pen is steady. The chair is uncomfortable, the way my father said a chair should be — a chair that is too comfortable is a chair that does not respect the work. And the work is this: to look at the boy behind the parapet and say what he does not know. That the rightness is the thing that will break. That the king will not come. That the numbness will come instead, and the numbness will carry him through the war and out the other side, and what comes out the other side will be alive and intact and not the man who went in.

The boy is there. He is looking at the cross on the tower. He does not know. He cannot know. The knowing requires the distance, and the distance requires the years, and the years have not yet happened.

The writing will be harder than the fighting, because the fighting required only the body and the writing requires the thing the body cannot provide: the willingness to look at what happened and call it what it was.

I am looking. I am calling it.

The king did not come.
