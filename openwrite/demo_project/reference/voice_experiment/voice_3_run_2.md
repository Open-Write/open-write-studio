# THE CHRONICLER — Run 2

On the morning of July 24, 1936, Martín Javier Echeverría saw the enemy for the first time. He was twenty-two years old. He had been at the front for less than twelve hours. He did not know the name of the valley, and he did not know the name of the village on the far ridge, and he did not know that the farmhouse whose roof had been destroyed by fire belonged to a family named Aldecoa who had fled to Bilbao three weeks earlier and would not return until 1941, by which time the house would be rebuilt and the wall repaired and the church tower straightened, and the Aldecoa family would find their home occupied by a Falangist official who would refuse to leave for two years. None of this was known to Martín. What Martín knew was the rifle in his hands, the beret on his head, and the thousand meters of green valley that separated him from men who were trying to kill him.

The requeté had arrived at the position the previous evening, after a three-day march from the railhead at Logroño. The march had been uneventful — dust, heat, the hymns of the Carlist cause, the gradual exhaustion of men carrying full packs in the Castilian summer. The company was part of the tercio of Navarre, one of several requeté formations that had mobilized within hours of the rising on July 17 and were now being pushed south toward the front lines that were forming around Madrid. The requeté were volunteers — not conscripts, not regulars, but men who had chosen to fight for God, king, and the fueros, and who carried that choice with the seriousness of men who believe that history is watching.

Martín was one of them. His father, Don Eusebio, was a Carlist elder who had worn the red beret in the 1920s campaigns and who had hung his own beret on a hook in the hallway of the house on the Calle Mayor in Pamplona, where it had waited for twenty years for the next call. The call came on July 18. Martín walked into the recruitment office on the Calle de la Merced, gave his name and his father's name and the name of his parish, and was issued a new beret — stiff, red, the wool still carrying the lanolin of the factory — and a Mauser rifle and a place in the column that would march south the following morning.

The position was a trench — or rather, a ditch. It had been dug by the men of a requeté company that had arrived a week earlier and had been improved, in the manner of field fortifications, by the successive addition of stones, sandbags, corrugated tin, and stolen canvas. It ran along the forward slope of a hill that overlooked a valley, and from the parapet a man could see, across approximately one thousand meters of green fields and a thin river, the ridge where the Republic had established its line.

The ridge was fortified. There was a farmhouse — stone, old, its roof destroyed, its walls blackened at the top where fire had been applied, deliberately or incidentally, during the fighting of the previous week. There was a low stone wall running along the ridge line, breached in two places by what appeared to be artillery fire, though the requeté had no artillery of their own and the Republic's guns were, at this stage of the war, directed at the larger cities. There was a church tower, rising above the wall, pale stone, with a cross at its peak.

It is worth pausing on the cross. The cross on the church tower across the valley was the same cross that hung in every church in Spain, that was carried in every procession, that was worn on the lapel of every requeté. The men who had burned the farmhouse and breached the wall and, presumably, killed or driven off the priests who had served that church — those men lived, or had lived, in the shadow of that cross. The cross was still there. It had not been torn down. It had not been replaced by the red flag of the Republic. It stood on the tower in the morning light, catching the sun, and it was the same cross that Martín's grandfather had fought under in the Third Carlist War, and it was the same cross that Martín's father prayed under every morning in the Iglesia de San Nicolás in Pamplona, and it was the cross of the crusade that Martín had enlisted to fight.

The morning was clear. The sun rose over the ridge at approximately six-thirty and the light spread across the valley with the speed that is characteristic of the Castilian meseta, where the horizon is flat and the air is dry and the sun does not creep but arrives. Martín was on the parapet by six, assigned to the morning watch by Sergeant Erice, and he had been crouching behind the stones for thirty minutes before the light was strong enough to see the far ridge.

He watched. The rifle was across the parapet, the barrel pointing toward the enemy position, the stock pressed into his shoulder. His hands were on the rifle — the right hand on the grip, the left hand on the forend — and his chin was resting on his forearms and his eyes were on the ridge. He watched the farmhouse and the wall and the church tower and the cross and the trees — olives, he thought, or holm oaks — and the terraced fields and the thin line of the river, and he waited for something to happen.

Nothing happened. The morning was still. A bird called from somewhere below the parapet — a lark, perhaps, or a stone-curlew, though Martín could not identify birds by sound and did not try. A lizard ran along the top of the wall and stopped and lifted its head and ran again. The river glinted. The trees were dark and motionless. The buildings on the ridge were silent.

He watched for an hour. The sun climbed. The heat built. Sweat formed on his forehead under the beret and ran down his temples and dripped from his jaw onto the stones. He could feel the wool of the beret growing damp, the band absorbing the sweat, the weight of the beret increasing as the moisture accumulated. He did not take it off. A requeté does not remove his beret on watch.

The shot came at approximately seven hundred hours.

The sound was a single crack — sharp, flat, immediate. It came from the direction of the ridge, though at a thousand meters the sound of a rifle shot does not arrive with directional precision; it seems to come from the air itself, from the space between the shooter and the target, from the bullet's path rather than from the rifle that fired it. The bullet passed Martín's head at a distance that he estimated, later, at less than half a meter. It struck the parapet to his left and dislodged a chip of stone approximately two centimeters in diameter. The chip struck his left cheek below the eye and drew blood.

He dropped. The motion was not voluntary. The body moved before the mind authorized the movement — the knees buckled, the waist folded, the shoulders collapsed, and he was in the trench on his back with the rifle clutched to his chest and the sky above him and his heart rate at a level that he could feel in his temples and his wrists and the hollow of his throat and the backs of his knees.

He lay there. The sky was blue. The sun was on the parapet above him, warming the stones, and the warmth radiated down into the trench and onto his face. He could hear his own breathing — short, fast, shallow, the breathing of a body in alarm. He could hear his heart. He could hear, from somewhere down the trench, the sound of someone else dropping — another man, another shot, another parapet.

Tomás appeared. Tomás crouched beside him and looked at him and did not speak for a period that Martín later estimated at thirty seconds but that felt, at the time, much longer. Then Tomás took out a cigarette and lit it and inhaled and exhaled and said: "The first one is the worst."

Martín took the cigarette. His hands were shaking — a fine, rapid tremor in the fingers and the wrists that he could see and feel but could not control. The cigarette trembled between his fingers. The smoke was harsh in his throat and he coughed and the coughing was a relief because it was a physical thing to do, a motion of the body that was not the motion of fear.

He sat up. He looked at the parapet above him — the stones, the sandbags, the corrugated tin, the canvas. The bullet had struck a stone to the left of where his head had been. The chip was gone — it was on his cheek, or on the floor of the trench, or somewhere he would never find it. The stone had a fresh mark on it — a pale scar where the bullet had bitten into the surface.

He stood. He moved to the firing step. He climbed up. He put his elbows on the parapet. He looked at the ridge.

The farmhouse was there. The wall was there. The church tower was there. The cross was there.

He watched. The sun climbed higher. The heat intensified. The valley filled with light and the light was hard and white and it bleached the color from the fields and the river and the ridge, and the buildings on the ridge shimmered faintly in the heat, and the cross on the tower was a dark shape against the bright sky.

He would watch this ridge for the next three weeks. He would watch it during the day and listen to it at night and learn its rhythms — the morning quiet, the midday stillness, the late-afternoon shots, the evening silence. He would learn that the enemy fired at movement and at sound and at the glint of light on metal, and that the enemy did not fire at night because the enemy, like the requeté, was conserving ammunition. He would learn that the ridge could be taken — that a company of men could cross the valley in forty minutes under cover of darkness and reach the wall and the farmhouse and the church tower — and that the ridge could be held, for a time, before the Republic counterattacked and the requeté fell back to the trench and the whole process began again.

None of this was known to him on the morning of July 24. What was known was the rifle, the beret, the cross on the tower, and the bullet that had missed. He watched. He did not move.
