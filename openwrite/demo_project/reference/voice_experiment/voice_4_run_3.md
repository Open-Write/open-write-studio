# THE BODY — Run 3

The blanket is rough. The wool is not the wool of the beret — it is coarser, thicker, the wool of a military blanket that has been issued and used and folded and unfolded and slept on by men whose names are not written on it. The fibers are stiff with dried sweat and dirt and the oil of skin and the faint residue of gun solvent where someone, at some point, cleaned a rifle on this blanket. He lies on it and the blanket lies on the ground and the ground is hard and the hardness comes through the blanket and into his back and his back is a map of the hardness — six points of pressure, six ridges of packed earth, six places where the body meets the ground and the ground refuses to yield.

His fingers are dirty. He holds them up in the gray light before dawn and the dirt is under every nail, packed into the creases of the knuckles, ground into the whorls of the fingerprints. The dirt is the color of the trench walls — pale, dry, the color of old paper. His hands are not large. They are the hands of a man who has worked in a hardware store — calloused at the base of the fingers, thick at the palm, the nails trimmed short because long nails catch on rope and wire and the handles of tools. The hands are the hands of his father, or his father's hands are the version of these hands that has been in the world for twenty more years. The same width. The same calluses. The same way of holding a rifle — right hand on the grip, left hand on the forend, index finger along the trigger guard.

Light. It comes through the eyelids — the red of blood, the orange of fire, the slow shift to yellow to white to the pale bright nothing of the Castilian morning. He opens his eyes. The parapet is above him. The stones are gray. The sandbags are dark with moisture. The sky is the color of clean water.

He sits up. The spine pops — three places, three sounds, the release of pressure that has been building all night. The neck is stiff. He turns the head and the neck resists and the resistance is in the tendons and the tendons are in the front of the throat and the back of the skull and the turning hurts until it doesn't, until the tendons give and the head moves freely and the relief goes down through the shoulders and into the arms and the arms are heavy with sleep and the sleep was not sleep but the shallow, surface rest of a body that has not let go of the waking world.

The rifle. He reaches for it without looking. The hand finds the stock — the wood, the grain, the smooth place where his grip has been. The hand wraps around the stock and the fingers find their positions and the positions are the same positions they found yesterday and the day before and the day before that, because the rifle has been in his hands for four days now and the hands know it the way they know the handle of the hammer and the grip of the pelota and the shape of the crucifix that hangs above the dining table.

He checks the chamber. The bolt pulls back. The round is there. Brass. Copper. The bullet tip, dull gold. He pushes the bolt forward. The round enters the chamber. The bolt locks. The bolt handle turns down. The rifle is loaded.

Erice. The boots on the dirt. The sound is a soft thud — leather on packed earth, the heel striking first, then the ball of the foot, then the toe. The rhythm is steady. The sergeant does not hurry. The sergeant does not slow. The sergeant walks through the trench the way a man walks through his own house — knowing every step, every stone, every place where the wall is low and the head must duck.

"Parapet."

Martín stands. His legs are stiff. The knees protest. The ankles crack. He moves to the firing step and steps up and the step is eighteen inches and his boots fit on it and the parapet is at his waist and he puts his elbows on the stones and the stones are rough and the roughness is in his elbows and his elbows are on the parapet and his hands are on the rifle and the rifle is on the parapet and he looks.

The valley.

The green hits him first. After three days of white road and yellow dust and brown earth, the green is a physical thing — it enters through the eyes and goes into the chest and the chest expands and the breath comes easier and the body, which has been braced against the dryness and the heat and the hardness of the ground, relaxes, slightly, in the presence of the green.

The fields are terraced. Low stone walls divide the hillside into strips — some planted with grain that is gold and ready for harvest, some with olives that are dark and twisted and old, some fallow, the grass growing wild between the stones. The terraces run down the slope to the river and the river is thin and bright and the sun is on it and the sun turns the water to silver and the silver is a line that runs the length of the valley.

The far slope. He lifts his eyes. The slope rises to a ridge. The ridge is a thousand meters away. The air is clear and the clear air makes the ridge look close — close enough to see the stones of the wall, the shape of the farmhouse, the outline of the church tower. But the distance is real. The distance is a thousand meters of open ground and green fields and the thin bright river and the air itself, which is warm and still and carries the sound of a bird — a lark, high above the valley, singing.

The buildings. The farmhouse is stone — honey-colored, the same stone as every farmhouse in this part of Castile. The roof is gone. The rafters are bare — dark lines against the sky, the ends blackened where fire has been. The fire was recent. The walls are standing but the top course is blackened and the blackening is the color of charcoal and the charcoal smell, if there was one, has been carried away by the wind.

The wall. Low, running along the ridge. Built of the same stone. A gap in the wall — a place where the stones have fallen or been pushed or been hit. The gap is the width of a man.

The tower. The church tower rises behind the wall. It is pale — lighter than the farmhouse, lighter than the wall, as if the stone is different or the light is different or the tower has been kept clean while the other buildings have been allowed to weather. The tower is slender. The tower is old. The tower has a cross at its peak.

The cross. Iron or wood. Dark against the sky. The sun is behind it and the sun turns the cross to a silhouette and the silhouette is sharp and clear and the shape is the shape that has been the shape of the faith for two thousand years — the vertical beam, the horizontal beam, the place where they meet.

He watches. His hands are on the rifle. The right hand on the grip. The left hand on the forend. The index finger along the trigger guard. The thumb on the safety. The rifle is an extension of the body. The body is an extension of the rifle. The two are one thing — a man with a gun, looking at a cross.

Sweat. The hairline first. A dampness. A welling. A trickle that runs down the temple and along the jaw and drips from the chin. Then the back of the neck. The collar of the shirt is damp. The band of the beret is damp. The skin is slick. Then the small of the back. The shirt clings. The belt is wet.

He watches for an hour. The sun moves. The shadows shorten. The heat builds. The valley fills with light and the light is hard and white and the buildings on the ridge shimmer faintly and the shimmer is the heat made visible and the heat is the thing that will kill more men than the bullets — not today, not this week, but in the months to come, at Teruel, at the Ebro, in the trenches where the heat and the cold and the wet and the dry take the body apart piece by piece while the bullets take it apart all at once.

The shot.

The body drops. The body drops before the mind understands. The knees buckle and the waist folds and the shoulders collapse and the rifle comes with him and he is in the trench and the trench is the place where the body goes when the body is told by the sound of the bullet that the body is in danger.

On his back. The rifle across the chest. The stock against the right shoulder. The barrel pointing at the sky. The sky is blue. The sun is above the parapet. The warmth of the sun is on his face.

He lies there. The heart is in his chest and the heart is fast and the beats are not the beats of a resting heart but the beats of a heart that has been startled and the startling is in the rhythm — the unevenness, the acceleration, the stumbling of a muscle that has been told to run.

He puts his hands on the dirt. The dirt is cool. The coolness is in the dirt and the dirt is in his palms and the palms are pressed flat and the flatness is steady and the steadiness is the opposite of the shaking and the shaking is in his fingers and his wrists and his forearms and the shaking is the body's response to the proximity of the bullet and the bullet is in the stone of the parapet six inches from where his head was.

Tomás. The hand on the arm. The face above him. The eyes.

"Are you hit?"

He runs his hands over his body. Head. Face. Chest. Arms. Legs. No blood. No wound. No hole.

"No."

"Then breathe."

He breathes. The air goes in through the nose and out through the mouth and the breathing slows and the heart slows and the hands slow and the shaking fades and the body returns to the body and the body is alive and the aliveness is sharp and vivid and the sharpness is in every nerve and every muscle and every bone and the body knows, now, what it did not know an hour ago: that a bullet can cross a thousand meters in less than a second and that the second is not long and that the distance is not safe and that the body is fragile and the fragile body is the only thing he has.

He sits up. He picks up the rifle. He checks the chamber. He checks the safety. He moves to the firing step. He climbs up.

The ridge. The farmhouse. The wall. The tower. The cross.

He watches. The beret is on his head. The rifle is in his hands. The cross is on the tower. The sun is in the sky.

He watches.
