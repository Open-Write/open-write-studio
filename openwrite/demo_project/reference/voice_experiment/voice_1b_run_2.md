# THE LAYERED RECKONING — Run 2

The first thing I remember is the smell. Not the sound of the shot, not the sight of the ridge, not the weight of the rifle — the smell. Damp earth and cordite and the sour reek of men who had been wearing the same clothes for four days. The smell was a physical thing. It coated the inside of the nostrils. It stayed for hours. It mixed with the gun oil and the bread and the tobacco and became the smell of the war itself — not the war of the newspapers and the speeches and the hymns, but the war of the body, the war of the trench, the war of men living in the ground.

I have read that smell is the sense most closely tied to memory. I believe it. Even now, years later, if I bend down to pull a weed in the garden and my hands break the surface of the dirt, the smell rises and I am back. The body does not care about chronology. The body does not know that the war ended. The body knows only what it smelled, and the smelling is permanent.

We reached the position on the evening of July twenty-third. I know the date because it was the day after the feast of the Magdalene, and I had said the Magdalene's prayer on the road, walking beside Tomás, who was humming a march. The road was white with dust. The dust was in my teeth. I could feel it gritting between my molars when I closed my mouth. The sun was the feast of Santiago and the heat pressed down on the back of the neck like a hand.

The trench was a ditch. Shallow. Crumbling. The parapet stones and sandbags, the sandbags rotted in places, restuffed with dirt. A corrugated sheet of tin where the bags had collapsed. The tin had been taken from a barn and the barn had been burned and the tin still smelled of the fire — faint, acrid, beneath the earth and the sweat and the gun oil. I noticed this. I noticed everything that first night. The senses were wide open, the way they are when the body knows it is in danger and does not yet know what form the danger will take.

Erice showed me my position. "Keep your head down after dark. They fire at muzzle flashes and sounds." He pointed to a tin can. "If you need to piss, use the can." The can had a dent. The dent caught the light of his lamp. I remember the dent with a clarity I cannot bring to the faces of men I knew for months.

I slept badly. The ground was hard — the ridges of packed earth pressing into my spine through the blanket. The cold came fast, the way it does on the meseta once the sun goes down. Tomás slept beside me with his beret over his face and his hands tucked into his armpits. The sound of his breathing. Steady. Slow. The sound of a man who could sleep anywhere, a skill I envied and resented in equal measure.

Before dawn, Erice. His hand on my shoulder — rough, callused. I could feel each finger separately. "Your turn. Watch the ridge."

I climbed up. The parapet was waist-high. I crouched behind it with my rifle across the top and my chin on my forearms, and I looked out at the valley for the first time in the light.

It was beautiful. I have said this before and I will say it again because the beauty was the first betrayal — the valley should not have been beautiful, not here, not at the front, not at the place where men would die. But the light was the color of pale honey and it lay across the fields and the river like a cloth, and the ridge on the far side was dark with trees, and the church tower rose above the trees with a cross at its peak that caught the first light and held it.

Somewhere behind those buildings, men were waking up. Eating the same hard bread. Drinking the same bitter coffee. Checking the same rifles. Men who believed — and I have to say this, because it is true, and because the forgetting of it is the thing I most regret — men who believed they were right. Not evil. Not deluded. Right. As right as I was. With the same certainty and the same prayers and the same conviction that God was on their side.

I could smell the coffee they were drinking. Not really — a thousand meters of valley separated us. But I could imagine it, and the imagining was a kind of recognition, and the recognition was the first crack in the catechism, though I did not know it then.

The shot came at full light. A crack. Sharp. Immediate. I heard the bullet pass my head — a flat, vicious snap, like a branch breaking close to the ear. A piece of the parapet jumped. A chip of stone hit my cheek. I felt the wetness before I felt the sting.

I dropped. My legs folded. I was in the dirt — back against the wall, rifle across my knees, breath in short hard gasps. My hands shaking. I could see the tremor — the fine, rapid oscillation of the muscles, the tendons like cords under the skin. I put them flat on the dirt floor. The dirt was cool. Damp. Gritty. I pressed my palms into it. I could feel individual grains — sharp, distinct, each one a fact against the skin.

Tomás sat beside me. He took out a cigarette. Lit it. The match flared and in the flare I saw his face — calm, the eyes steady, the mouth set. He blew the smoke at the sky. "The first one is the worst."

"That was the first one."

"I know." He offered me the cigarette. I took it. My hands shook and the cigarette trembled and the smoke was hot and bitter in my throat and I coughed, and Tomás said, "You get used to it."

I did not believe him. I could feel the bullet in the air — not the bullet itself, but the displacement it had left, the column of disturbed air that had passed my face like a finger drawn across the cheek. I could feel the chip of stone on my skin — a small, sharp pressure, the blood running warm from it toward my jaw. I could feel the dirt under my palms and the beret on my head and the rifle across my knees and all of these things were real and specific and present, and they did not feel like things a man could get used to.

It stopped. Not that morning. But it stopped.

I think about that morning now and what I see is the boy I was. Twenty-two. Beret. Rifle. Faith complete and undamaged. The boy is crouching behind a parapet looking at a cross on a church tower and thinking about the king and the fueros and the crusade and the rightness of what he is doing, and he does not know that the rightness is the thing that will break.

The smell of the dirt. The weight of the beret. The chip of stone. The cigarette that trembled. The cross on the tower that held the light.

I would like to say I wish someone had told him. But no one could have told him. And if they had, he would not have listened, because the beret was on his head and the rifle was in his hands and the cause was in his blood and the boy was not built for doubt.

The king did not come.
