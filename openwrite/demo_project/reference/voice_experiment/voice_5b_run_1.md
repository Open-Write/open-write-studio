# THE STRUCTURED SPARSE — Run 1

He did not sleep, and the not-sleeping was its own kind of darkness.

The ground was hard beneath him — packed earth, the ridges pressing into his spine at six points he could name without looking: the base of the skull, both shoulder blades, the small of the back, both hips. The body maps the ground when the ground will not yield. The night was cold. The men around him snored and shifted and coughed, and the coughing echoed in the trench, and the trench was shallow, and the parapet was low, and the stars above it were very far away.

He lay on his back. The beret on his head. The blanket at his chin. The rifle beside him, barrel pointing away from the sleeping men.

The dark was quiet. The quiet was the war, waiting.

Tomás slept nearby — on his back, the beret over his face, hands tucked into his armpits. A man making himself small. Beside him, Ignacio, the boy from Tudela, slept with his mouth open, and beyond them both an older man whose name he had not caught slept with his rosary in his hands, the beads catching a thread of starlight.

Three days of marching. The road south from Logroño white with dust. The sun the feast of Santiago. The men sang the requeté hymn in the first hour and fell silent in the third — not gradually, but all at once, as if a door had closed. By the second day the berets were the color of the road. By the third day the men were the color of the road. The only red left was the flash of the lining when the wind caught it.

The rifle on his shoulder grew heavier each day. The pack dug into his hips. The boots were new and stiff and raised blisters that burst and bled and hardened into calluses — calluses that would outlast the war, if he did.

He closed his eyes. The dark was the same.

In the morning the light came fast — no warning, no gradation. Black to gray to the pale dry color of the trench walls. The stones emerged. The sandbags. The corrugated tin. The canvas that flapped.

He sat up. His spine cracked. His neck was stiff. He rolled his shoulders and the joints popped and the sound was loud in the quiet.

Tomás was awake, cleaning his rifle with a rag that had been a shirt once. The oil smell was sharp — gun solvent, metal, the sourness of use.

"Morning," Tomás said.

He did not answer. He reached for the canteen and drank. The water was warm, metallic. He drank again. He clipped the canteen to his belt.

His hands were dirty. The dirt under his nails, in the creases of his knuckles. He picked at it with his thumbnail and the dirt came away in small dark crescents that fell onto the blanket. Castilian dirt — pale, dry, nothing like the dark wet soil of Navarre.

Erice came. The sergeant moved through the trench with his head below the parapet. His boots found the same spots on the same stones. He stopped at Martín's section.

"Parapet. Watch."

Two words. Martín picked up the Mauser — long, heavy, the stock scarred with use. He checked the chamber. Loaded. He checked the safety. On. He moved to the firing step. Climbed. Put his elbows on the stones.

The valley.

Green. After three days of dust, green — the fields running down to a thin bright river, the far slope rising to a ridge dark with trees. Buildings among the trees. A farmhouse, stone, no roof, the rafters dark against the sky. A wall, low, running along the ridge, a gap in it where stones had fallen or been pushed or hit. And above the wall a church tower — pale, slender, older than the farmhouse, older than the wall.

A cross at the top. Iron or wood. At this distance he could not tell.

He watched the cross. The sun had just cleared the ridge. The cross caught the light and held it the way a cup holds water.

He thought about his father. The hook in the hallway. The beret hanging there. The dining table below the crucifix. The name carved into the base — Martín, his grandfather's name, his name. The morning he left, his father's hand on his shoulder, the weight of it, and his father's words: "You will wear the beret."

Not "be careful." Not "come home."

He watched the ridge. Nothing moved. The farmhouse still. The wall still. The tower still. The trees dark, motionless. The terraced fields empty. The river glinting.

A bird called. A lizard ran along the parapet — stopped, lifted its head, ran again. The sun climbed. Heat built. Sweat formed under the beret: a dampness at the hairline, a trickle down the temple, the wool of the band growing wet.

An hour passed. The shadows shortened. The valley filled with hard white light and the buildings on the ridge shimmered faintly.

From somewhere down the line: a man singing. A hymn. Faint. The words indistinct. The melody one he recognized from the marches. Then silence.

The shot came.

A crack. Sharp. Flat. The bullet passed his head — a snap, a tearing, the sound of air being broken. A chip of stone flew from the parapet. The chip hit his cheek. Blood. A thin line running toward the jaw.

He dropped.

The body folded. Knees. Waist. Shoulders. He was in the trench on his back. The rifle across his chest. The sky above — blue, empty, far away.

His hands shook. He looked at them. Shaking. He gripped the rifle. The shaking did not stop. He put his hands on the dirt. Cool dirt. He pressed his palms into it. The shaking slowed.

Tomás crouched beside him. Tomás's hand on his arm. Tomás's face — calm, steady, the eyes of a man who had been here before.

"Are you hit?"

He touched his cheek. His fingers came away with blood. The chip. The stone.

"No."

"Breathe."

He breathed. In. Out. The heart slowed. The hands steadied.

He sat up. He looked at his hands. Still. The dirt on his palms — cool, dry, the dirt of Castile. He wiped it on his trousers. He picked up the rifle. Checked the chamber. Loaded. Checked the safety. On.

He looked at Tomás. Tomás was sitting against the wall with his cigarette and his calm eyes and the rag still in his hand from the morning cleaning. Tomás had been here for two weeks. Two weeks was, at that moment, an eternity.

"Does it get easier?" Martín asked.

Tomás smoked. He blew the smoke at the sky. "The shooting does. The rest does not."

He did not ask what "the rest" meant. He would learn.

He moved to the firing step. Climbed up. Put his elbows on the parapet.

The ridge was there. The farmhouse. The wall. The tower. The cross.

A chip in the stone. Six inches from where his head had been. Six inches. The width of his hand. The distance between the bullet being in the stone and the bullet being in his skull.

He watched. The sun climbed higher. The heat built. Sweat ran down his face and dripped from his chin onto the stones of the parapet. The stones absorbed it. Dark circles where the drops landed.

He thought: the bullet crossed a thousand meters in less than a second.

He thought: six inches is the width of my hand.

He thought: the next one might not miss.

He watched. The beret on his head. The rifle in his hands. The cross on the tower. The morning passed. The afternoon came. The war continued.

He watched.
