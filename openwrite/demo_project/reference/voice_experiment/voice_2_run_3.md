# THE PRESENT TENSE — Run 3

The can is almost full. Martín stands at the end of the trench with his back to the parapet and his hand on the tin can and the smell rising from it, and he thinks: this is the latrine. This is what the war's latrine smells like. He has been here for twelve hours and this is the first thing the war has shown him that he did not expect — not the trench, not the rifle, not the enemy, but the can, and the smell, and the fact that forty men share it and that it is his turn to empty it into the sap trench behind the position.

He carries the can down the sap. The sap is a shallow cut in the hillside, barely knee-deep, screened from the valley by a mound of dirt and a piece of canvas that flaps in the morning breeze. Behind the canvas is a pit. He pours the can into the pit and covers it with dirt using a shovel that has been left there for this purpose and the shovel is caked with the same dirt and the same purpose and he thinks: this is the war too. Not the rifle and the hymn and the beret. The can and the pit and the shovel.

He walks back to the trench. The sun is up now — fully up, not the thin light of dawn but the hard, white light of Castile in late July, and the heat is already building in the trench like water filling a bowl. The walls radiate warmth. The sandbags are hot to the touch. The stones of the parapet shimmer faintly, though that might be the dust motes in the air, not the stone itself.

Tomás is in the same position he was in when Martín left — sitting against the wall with the rifle across his knees and the rag in his hand and the bottle of gun oil beside him. Tomás has been cleaning the rifle since before dawn. He cleans it the way a priest cleans the chalice — with care, with attention, with the knowledge that the object in his hands is the thing that stands between him and the alternative.

"Finished?" Tomás asks.

"The can is empty."

"For now."

Martín sits down. He takes the beret off — the first time since yesterday evening — and wipes the sweatband with his hand and puts it back on. The wool is damp. It will be damp all day. It will be damp every day until October, when the rains come and the damp becomes wet and the wet becomes cold and the cold becomes the thing that kills more men than the bullets.

Erice appears. He moves along the trench with his head ducked below the parapet line and his eyes moving — always moving, checking the sandbags, checking the men, checking the firing positions. He stops at Martín's section.

"Parapet. Now."

Martín picks up the Mauser. He moves to the firing step. He climbs up.

The valley opens in front of him like a book.

Green fields. A river — thin, bright, running along the bottom of the valley like a thread of silver. The far slope rising to a ridge. Trees on the ridge — olives, he can see now, their trunks twisted and their leaves a gray-green that catches the light. Buildings on the ridge. A farmhouse, stone, without a roof — the rafters are bare, dark against the sky, and the top of the walls are blackened. A wall, low, running along the ridge line. A gap in the wall. And a church tower, rising behind the wall, pale stone, a cross at the top.

He rests the rifle on the parapet. He looks through the iron sights at the church tower. The tower is far — a thousand meters, maybe more. At this distance the cross is a small dark shape against the sky. He cannot see the details. He cannot see whether the cross is wooden or iron, whether it is old or new, whether it is straight or leaning. He can see only the shape, and the shape is the same shape that hangs above the dining table in his father's house in Pamplona, the same shape that hangs in every church in Navarre, the same shape that the requeté wear on their lapels and their flags and their hearts.

He thinks: the men behind that wall look at that cross every day.

He thinks: the men behind that wall believe the cross is theirs.

He thinks: I believe the cross is mine.

He does not have time to finish the thought. The shot comes.

It comes the way all first shots come — without announcement, without warning, without the courtesy of a sound that says "this is a bullet and it is aimed at you." The sound is a crack, flat and dry, and the bullet passes his head with a snap that is louder than the crack, a vicious tearing sound that is not a whistle and not a hum and not any of the sounds he has imagined a bullet makes. It is the sound of air being broken. It is the sound of something moving faster than the ear can track, passing close enough to feel the pressure change against the skin of his face.

He drops. The body goes first — the knees buckle, the waist folds, the shoulders collapse — and he is in the trench on his back with the rifle clutched to his chest and the sky above him and the sky is blue and very far away and completely indifferent to the fact that a bullet just passed through the space where his head was.

His hands shake. He looks at them. They are shaking the way a leaf shakes in wind — a rapid, fine, uncontrollable vibration that starts in the fingers and moves up through the wrists to the forearms. He grips the rifle tighter. The shaking does not stop. He presses his hands against the stock and the stock is wood and the wood is solid and the solidity helps but does not stop it.

He breathes. The air goes in and the air goes out and the air tastes of dust and the dust is the same dust that has been in his mouth since the march south and the dust tastes of the road and the sun and the three days of walking and the hymns and the blessings and the father's hand on the shoulder and the beret — always the beret, on his head, on his head, on his head.

Tomás is beside him. Tomás crouches and looks at him and does not say anything for a moment, and then Tomás says: "Are you bleeding?"

Martín touches his cheek. His fingers come away with a thin line of blood — the chip of stone from the parapet, the fragment that the bullet knocked loose when it struck the wall. The blood is bright in the morning light. He looks at it on his fingers and he thinks: this is the first blood of the war. Not the blood of a wound. The blood of a stone chip. The blood of proximity.

"I'm fine."

"Good." Tomás sits down. He takes out a cigarette. He puts it between his lips and lights it and inhales and exhales and the smoke rises out of the trench like a signal, like a flag, like a thing that says to the men across the valley: here we are, we are still here, we are smoking cigarettes in the morning sun and we are not afraid.

But Martín is afraid. He is afraid in a way that is physical and specific and located in his hands and his chest and the dryness of his mouth. He is not afraid of dying — he has not thought about dying, not yet, not in the way that will come later, when the dying is closer and more frequent and has faces and names. He is afraid of the sound. He is afraid of the crack and the snap and the pressure on his skin and the chip of stone and the blood on his fingers and the knowledge that a thousand meters is not far enough, that the distance between the trench and the ridge is the distance between being alive and being dead, and that the distance can be closed in the time it takes a bullet to fly.

He sits up. He looks at his hands. The shaking is less. He puts his hands on the dirt floor and the dirt is cool and the coolness is a thing to hold onto and he holds onto it.

He picks up the rifle. He moves to the firing step. He climbs up. He puts his elbows on the parapet. He looks at the ridge.

The farmhouse. The wall. The church tower. The cross.

The cross is small at this distance. It is a dark shape against the bright sky. It is the same cross that his grandfather fought under and his father prayed under and that he has carried in his heart since before he knew what carrying meant.

He watches. The sun rises higher. The heat builds. The sweat runs down his face and mixes with the blood from the chip and he wipes it away with his sleeve and the sleeve is dusty and the dust mixes with the blood and the sweat and he watches the ridge and the ridge watches him and the war settles into its first morning and the morning is long and the light is hard and the distance is everything.

He does not take the beret off.
