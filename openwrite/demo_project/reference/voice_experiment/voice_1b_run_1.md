# THE LAYERED RECKONING — Run 1

The first thing I remember is the smell. Damp earth. Cordite from a practice round someone had fired the night before. And beneath that, the sour reek of men who had been wearing the same clothes for four days — a smell that was not quite sweat and not quite dirt but something in between, something human and animal at the same time, something that attached itself to the wool of the beret and the canvas of the pack and the creases of the skin and would not wash out for weeks.

Even now — even after everything — if I catch the smell of wet dirt I am back in that trench with the beret on my head and the rifle beside me and the future still intact. The nose has no sense of irony. The nose does not know how the story ends. The nose knows only what it smelled, and what it smelled was the beginning.

We reached the position on the evening of July twenty-third. The day after the feast of the Magdalene. I know the date because I had said the Magdalene's prayer that morning on the road, walking beside Tomás, who was humming a march I did not recognize. The prayer seemed appropriate. The Magdalene was a sinner who became a saint. We were sinners going to war to restore the faith.

The trench was a shallow gouge in the hillside. I use the word "trench" because there is no better word, but it was not a trench in the way that the engineers build them. No revetments. No fire steps. No traverses. It was a ditch — men had dug it with shovels and lined the forward edge with stones and filled sandbags with the excavated dirt, and the result was something that would stop a bullet if the bullet was not moving very fast and did not hit the part of the parapet where the sandbags had rotted and collapsed and been replaced by a corrugated sheet of tin that someone had taken from a barn.

Sergeant Erice showed me where to lie. Compact man. Mustache that needed trimming. The quiet authority of someone who had been doing this long enough to stop explaining. "Keep your head down after dark," he said. "They fire at muzzle flashes and sounds. If you need to piss, use the can." He pointed to a tin can at the end of the trench. The can had a dent in its side. The dent caught the light. I remember the dent.

I did not sleep. The ground was hard — packed earth, the ridges pressing into my back through the blanket. The Castilian meseta loses its heat fast once the sun goes down. Tomás slept beside me with his beret pulled over his face and his hands tucked into his armpits. He slept well. Tomás could sleep anywhere — a skill I envied and resented in equal measure. The sound of his breathing was steady and slow and I counted the breaths the way a man counts the ticks of a clock, not because the counting helps but because the alternative is to lie in the dark and listen to the war.

The war was quiet. The quiet was the worst part. The quiet had texture — the scratch of a boot on stone, the clink of a canteen against a buckle, the soft thud of a man shifting in his sleep. The quiet was not silence. The quiet was the sound of men waiting.

Before dawn, Erice shook my shoulder. His hand was rough. I could feel the calluses through the cloth of my shirt. "Your turn on the parapet. Stay low. Watch the ridge. Report anything that moves."

I climbed up. The parapet was waist-high and I crouched behind it with my rifle across the top and my chin resting on my forearms, and the stone was rough under my elbows and the sandbags were damp and the corrugated tin was warm where the sun had begun to touch it, and I looked out at the valley for the first time in the light.

It was beautiful. I know that is not what a man is supposed to say about the place where he will try to kill other men, but the valley was beautiful. The light was the color of pale honey and it lay across the fields and the river and the far slope like a cloth, and the ridge on the far side was dark with trees — olives, I thought, or maybe holm oaks — and the buildings on the ridge were the same pale stone as the buildings around Pamplona, and the church tower rose above the trees with a cross at its peak that caught the first light and held it.

The cross was iron or wood. At that distance I could not tell. But the light was on it and the light made it bright and the brightness was the first thing I saw and the last thing I would see, every morning, for the next three months.

Somewhere behind those buildings, men were waking up. Men who believed — and I have to say this, because it is true, and because the forgetting of it is the thing I most regret — men who believed they were right. Not evil. Not deluded. Right. As right as I was. With the same certainty and the same prayers and the same conviction that God was on their side. I could smell the coffee they were drinking. Not really — a thousand meters of valley and air separated us. But I could imagine it, and the imagining was a kind of recognition, and the recognition was a thing I was not supposed to feel.

The shot came at full light. The sun was over the ridge by then and the valley was bright and I had been watching for an hour and seen nothing. The sound was a crack, sharp and immediate, and I heard the bullet pass my head — not the Hollywood whistle, but a flat, vicious snap, like a branch breaking close to the ear. A piece of the parapet jumped. A chip of stone flew up and hit my cheek. I did not feel the sting until later. I felt the wetness immediately.

I dropped into the trench. My legs folded under me and I was sitting in the dirt with my back against the wall and my rifle across my knees and my breath coming in short, hard gasps. My hands were shaking. I could see them — the fingers trembling, the knuckles white, the tendons standing up under the skin like cords. I put them flat on the dirt floor. The dirt was cool and damp and gritty and I pressed my palms into it and waited.

Tomás sat down beside me. He did not say anything at first. He took out a cigarette and lit it and blew the smoke at the sky, and the smoke was thin and blue and it rose out of the trench and into the morning air and dissolved. Then he said, "The first one is the worst."

"That was the first one," I said.

"I know." He offered me the cigarette. I took it. My hands were still shaking and the cigarette trembled between my fingers and the smoke was hot in my throat and I coughed, and Tomás said, "You get used to it."

I did not believe him. I did not believe that a man could get used to the sound of a bullet passing his head. I did not believe that the body could learn to accept the proximity of its own death as a condition of daily life. The taste of the cigarette was bitter and the smoke was in my eyes and the dirt was under my nails and the blood was on my cheek and the beret was hot on my head and all of these things were real and present and they did not feel like things a man could get used to.

It stopped. Not that morning. That morning I sat in the trench for a long time with my hands pressed into the dirt and my heart beating in my throat and the beret hot on my head and the thought, repeating, that someone had tried to kill me and that I was alive, and the aliveness was sharp and vivid in a way that nothing before it had ever been. But by the next day the shaking was less, and by the day after that it was only my hands, and by the end of the week it was nothing.

I think about that morning now and what I see is the boy I was — twenty-two years old, with a beret his father had worn and a rifle he had been given by a sergeant who would be dead before October and a faith that was complete and undamaged. The boy is crouching behind a parapet and looking at a church tower across a valley and thinking about the king and the fueros and the crusade, and he does not know that the rightness is the thing that will break.

I would like to say I wish someone had told him. But no one could have told him. And if they had, he would not have listened, because the beret was on his head and the rifle was in his hands and the cause was in his blood and the boy was not built for doubt.

The king did not come.
