# THE SPARSE — Run 3

Dirt in his nails. Dirt in his hair. Dirt in the folds of the beret.

He lay in the trench and the trench was dark and the dark was quiet and the quiet was the war, not yet a war, still a silence that had not been broken.

The ground was hard. The blanket was thin. The beret was on his head. The beret was always on his head. His father had said to sleep in it and he slept in it and the wool was damp from the sweat of the night and the dampness was against his forehead and the forehead was hot.

The men around him were sleeping. Tomás — on his back, the beret over his face, the hands tucked into the armpits. Ignacio — the boy from Tudela, on his side, the mouth open, the breath slow. The older man — against the wall, the rosary in the hands, the eyes closed.

He did not sleep. He listened. The dark had sounds. The coughing. The shifting. The occasional shot from somewhere down the line — a sentry, nervous, firing at a shadow. The shot was distant and flat and it echoed off the walls of the valley and then it was gone and the quiet came back.

Three days of marching. The road from Logroño was white with dust and the dust got into everything — the rifle, the bread, the folds of the beret, the creases of the hands. The men sang in the first hour and fell silent in the third. The sun was the feast of Santiago and the heat was the heat of Castile in July and the heat was a weight on the shoulders and the back and the head. Tomás walked beside him and did not complain and the silence was a gift and the gift was the only one Tomás had to give.

He thought about the morning. The morning would bring the light and the light would bring the ridge and the ridge would bring the enemy and the enemy would bring the thing he had been marching toward for three days. The thing the hymns were about. The thing the beret was for.

Light came. It came all at once — the way it does on the meseta, no warning, no gradation. The trench walls went from black to brown to the color of old paper. The parapet appeared. The stones. The sandbags. The sky.

He sat up. His spine cracked. His neck was stiff. He put on the beret — it had slipped in the night, tilted to one side — and adjusted it, pulling the right side down the way his father did. The way his grandfather did before that.

Tomás was awake. Tomás was always awake first. Tomás was sitting against the wall with the rifle across his knees and the rag in his hand and the tin of oil beside him.

"Sleep?" Tomás asked.

"No."

"Nobody does. Not the first night."

He drank from the canteen. The water was warm. Metallic. He drank again. He clipped the canteen to his belt. He picked up the rifle. Checked the chamber. Loaded. Checked the safety. On.

Erice came. The sergeant's boots on the dirt. The soft thud. The scrape. The rhythm of a man who knew every step. He stopped.

"Parapet."

One word. Martín moved to the firing step. The step was a ledge of packed earth, eighteen inches high. He stepped up. The parapet was at his waist. He put his elbows on the stones and the stones were rough and the sandbags were damp and the corrugated tin was warm. He climbed up.

The valley opened.

Green. The fields were green. After three days of white dust and yellow road and brown earth, the green was a thing that went through the eyes and into the chest. The river was silver. The far slope rose to a ridge and the ridge was dark with trees and the trees were olives and the olives were old.

On the ridge: buildings. A farmhouse. Stone. No roof. The rafters bare against the sky. The walls blackened at the top. A wall, low, running along the ridge. A gap in the wall. A church tower. Pale. Slender. A cross at the top.

He watched the cross. The cross was small at this distance. A dark shape. It caught the light and held it. The light was new — the sun had just cleared the ridge. The cross was the first thing the light touched.

He thought about the king. Don Jaime. In exile. In Austria or in France — he did not know which. An old man waiting for a call that had not come. The call would come. The war would be won. The king would return. The fueros would be restored. The catechism was clear on this.

He thought about the fueros. The ancient rights of Navarre and the Basque provinces. The rights that the Republic had been eroding. The rights that the crusade would restore. The rights that his grandfather had fought for in the Third Carlist War and his father had spoken about at the dining table beneath the crucifix and that he, Martín, had absorbed into his blood the way a child absorbs the language of his parents — not by learning but by living.

He thought about his grandfather. The name — Martín. The same name. The crucifix above the table. The name carved into the base. The man who had worn the red beret in the Third Carlist War and who had died before Martín was born and who was, in the family's telling, a saint and a soldier and a man who had never wavered.

He watched the ridge. Nothing moved. The farmhouse was still. The wall was still. The tower was still. The trees were dark and motionless. The terraced fields were empty. The river glinted.

A bird called. A lizard ran along the parapet and stopped and lifted its head and ran again. The sun climbed. The heat built. Sweat formed under the beret. He felt it — a dampness at the hairline, a trickle down the temple, the wool of the band growing wet.

He could hear the trench behind him — the coughing, the clink of a canteen against a buckle, the scrape of a boot on stone. He could smell it — the earth, the sweat, the gun oil, the faint sweetness of rotting burlap. He could feel the heat on the back of his neck, building, pressing, the beret absorbing it and holding it against his skull.

An hour passed.

The shot came.

A crack. Flat. Dry. The bullet passed his head. He felt the air move — a pressure, a displacement, the skin of his cheek tightening. The parapet jumped. A chip of stone. The chip hit his cheek. Blood. A thin line. Running toward the jaw.

He dropped.

The body folded. Knees. Waist. Shoulders. He was on his back. The rifle across his chest. The sky above. Blue. Far. Indifferent.

His hands shook. He looked at them. They shook. He could not stop them. He gripped the rifle. The shaking transferred to the stock. He let go. He put his hands on the dirt. The dirt was cool. He pressed. The shaking slowed.

Tomás crouched beside him. Tomás's hand on his arm. The grip firm. The hand warm.

"Are you hit?"

He touched his cheek. Blood on his fingers. Bright. Thin. The chip. The stone.

"No."

"Then breathe."

He breathed. In. Out. The air went in and the air came out and the heart slowed and the hands steadied and the shaking stopped.

He sat up. He looked at the parapet above him. A chip in the stone. Fresh. The color of the inside of bread. Six inches from where his head had been.

Six inches.

He thought: the bullet crossed a thousand meters in less than a second.

He thought: six inches is the width of my hand.

He thought: the next one might not miss.

He stood. He moved to the firing step. He climbed up. He put his elbows on the parapet. The stones were rough under his elbows. The sandbags were damp. The corrugated tin was warm.

The ridge was there. The farmhouse was there. The wall was there. The tower was there. The cross was there.

He watched. The sun was higher. The valley was bright. The distance was a thousand meters of green fields and silver river and the air itself, which was warm and still and carried the sound of the bird, still singing, high above the valley, thin and clear.

He thought: I will watch anyway.

He thought: this is what the beret is for.

He thought: the king will come. The fueros will be restored. The crusade will succeed.

He watched. The beret was on his head. The rifle was in his hands. The cross was on the tower. The sun was in the sky.

The morning passed. The afternoon came. The war continued.

He watched.
