# THE WARMER SPARSE — Run 1

He did not sleep.

The ground was hard. The night was cold. The men around him snored and shifted and the sounds were small and human and he listened to them the way a man listens to the breathing of his children through a wall — not for meaning, but for proof that they are there.

Tomás slept on his back with the beret over his face. One hand hung at his side, the fingers curled, the knuckles raw. The hand of a man who worked before he fought. Martín looked at the hand and looked away. It was not his to look at for long.

He lay on his back. The beret was on his head. The blanket was at his chin. The rifle was beside him with the barrel pointing away from the sleeping men.

The dark was quiet. The quiet was the war, waiting.

Three days of marching. The road south from Logroño was white with dust. The sun was the feast of Santiago. The men sang in the first hour and fell silent in the third. By the second day the berets were the color of the road. By the third day the men were the color of the road. The only red left was the flash of the lining when the wind caught it.

He walked beside Tomás. Tomás did not complain. Once, on the second day, Tomás handed him a piece of bread without being asked — just held it out, not looking, eyes on the road — and Martín took it and ate it and neither of them spoke about it. The bread was dry. It was the best thing he had ever eaten.

In the morning the light came fast. The sky went from black to gray to the pale dry color of the trench walls. The stones of the parapet emerged. The sandbags. The corrugated tin.

He sat up. His spine cracked. His neck was stiff.

Tomás was awake. Tomás was cleaning his rifle with a rag. The oil smell was sharp.

"Morning," Tomás said.

He reached for the canteen and drank. The water was warm and tasted of metal. He offered the canteen to Tomás. Tomás took it and drank and handed it back. The canteen was lighter now. That was all that had changed between them, and it was enough.

Erice came. The sergeant moved through the trench with his head below the parapet.

"Parapet. Watch."

Martín picked up the rifle. Checked the chamber. Loaded. Checked the safety. On. Moved to the firing step. Climbed up. Put his elbows on the stones.

The valley.

Green. After three days of dust, the valley was green. Fields running down to a river. The river thin and bright. The far slope rising to a ridge. Trees on the ridge. Buildings among the trees.

A church tower. Pale. Slender. A cross at the top. The cross caught the early light and held it.

He watched the cross. He thought about the morning he left. His father's hand on his shoulder. The weight of it. "You will wear the beret." Not "be careful." Not "come home." You will wear the beret.

He understood, now, what his father meant. The beret was not a command. It was a way of saying: I cannot protect you. I can only give you this. The wool. The tradition. The name inside the band. Take it. Wear it. Come back if you can.

The sun climbed. The heat built. Sweat formed under the beret. A trickle down the temple. The wool of the band growing wet.

A bird called. A lizard ran along the parapet and stopped and lifted its head and ran again. Somewhere down the line, a man was singing. A hymn. Faint. The melody one he recognized from the marches. The singing stopped. The quiet came back.

The shot came.

A crack. Sharp. Flat. The bullet passed his head and he heard it — a snap, a tearing. A chip of stone flew from the parapet. The chip hit his cheek. Blood.

He dropped.

The body folded. Knees. Waist. Shoulders. He was in the trench on his back. The rifle across his chest. The sky above him. Blue. Far away.

His hands shook. He looked at them. They shook. He gripped the rifle. The shaking did not stop. He put his hands on the dirt. The dirt was cool. He pressed his palms into it. The shaking slowed.

Tomás crouched beside him. Tomás's hand on his arm. The grip firm. The hand warm. Tomás did not let go right away. He held on for a breath longer than he needed to, and the holding was not reassurance — it was recognition. I was here. I am here. The shaking is normal.

"Are you hit?"

He touched his cheek. Blood on his fingers.

"No."

"Breathe."

He breathed. The air went in. The air came out. The heart slowed. The hands steadied.

He sat up. He looked at the rifle. He looked at his hands. The hands were still. He wiped the dirt on his trousers.

"Does it get easier?" Martín asked.

Tomás sat back. He took out a cigarette and lit it and blew the smoke at the sky. For a moment his face was behind the smoke and the smoke was thin and the face was calm.

"The shooting does. The rest does not."

He did not ask what "the rest" meant. He would learn.

He moved to the firing step. Climbed up. Put his elbows on the parapet.

The ridge was there. The farmhouse was there. The wall was there. The tower was there. The cross was there.

A chip in the stone. Six inches from where his head had been. He looked at the chip. Six inches. The width of his hand.

He thought: the bullet crossed a thousand meters in less than a second.

He thought: six inches is the width of my hand.

He thought: the next one might not miss.

He watched. The sun climbed higher. The heat built. The beret was on his head. The rifle was in his hands. The cross was on the tower.

The morning passed. The afternoon came. The war continued.

He watched.
