# THE PRESENT TENSE — Run 1

The light comes fast. One moment the trench is dark and the men in it are shapes — a shoulder here, a boot there, the dull shine of a rifle barrel catching starlight. The next moment the sky is pale and the walls of the trench are the color of old bone and the parapet is a line of stones against the brightening east, and Martín is awake, or has been awake, or has not slept at all, and the distinction does not matter because the day has started and the day does not care whether he is ready.

He sits up. His back is stiff from the ground. The dirt has pressed ridges into his spine through the blanket, and he rolls his shoulders to work them out, and the movement makes a sound — the creak of joints, the rustle of wool — that seems enormous in the quiet of the trench. Beside him, Tomás is already awake, sitting with his back against the wall, cleaning his rifle with a rag. The rag was a shirt once. Now it is an oily gray cloth that smells of solvent and metal and the specific sourness of gun oil that Martín will never be able to smell, for the rest of his life, without thinking of this trench.

"Morning," Tomás says. He does not look up from the rifle.

Martín nods. His mouth is dry. He reaches for the canteen and drinks, and the water is warm and tastes of the metal container, and it is the best thing he has ever tasted. He drinks again. He screws the cap back on and clips the canteen to his belt and looks at his hands. They are dirty. The dirt is packed under his nails and into the creases of his knuckles and it is the color of the trench walls — pale, dry, the dirt of the Castilian meseta, nothing like the dark, wet soil of Navarre.

He puts on the beret. The wool is stiff and new and it sits on his head with a weight that is not physical. He adjusts it — pulling the right side down, the way his father does, the way his grandfather did before that. The beret is red. It is the color of the Carlist cause, the color of the requeté, the color of the blood that the cause has shed across three wars and a century of waiting. In the early light it looks almost black. In full sun it will blaze.

Erice appears. The sergeant moves through the trench the way a man moves through his own house — knowing every step, every loose stone, every place where the wall is low enough to require ducking. He stops at Martín's position and looks down at him.

"Your turn on the parapet. Stay low. Watch the ridge. Report anything."

Martín picks up his rifle. The Mauser is long and heavy and the stock is scarred with use, and it fits into his hands with the familiarity of a tool he has been carrying for weeks rather than days. He checks the chamber. Loaded. He checks the safety. On. He moves to the forward wall of the trench and finds the firing step — a ledge of packed earth that raises him high enough to see over the parapet without exposing more than his head and shoulders.

He climbs up. He puts his elbows on the parapet. He looks out.

The valley is green. This is the first thing he sees — green, after three days of white dust on the road south. The valley is a fold in the land, wide and shallow, with a river at the bottom that catches the early light and throws it back in a thin line of silver. The far slope rises to a ridge that is maybe a thousand meters away. The ridge is dark with trees. The trees are olive trees, or maybe holm oaks — at this distance he cannot tell. On the ridge, above the trees, there are buildings. A farmhouse, low and square, its stone the color of honey. A wall, running along the ridge line. A church tower, rising above the wall, with a cross at its peak that catches the first sun and holds it.

He stares. The buildings look like the buildings around Pamplona. The farmhouse could be any farmhouse in Navarre — the same stone, the same proportions, the same way of sitting in the land as if it had grown there. The church tower could be the tower of any village church. The cross is the same cross that hangs above the dining table in his father's house.

Behind those buildings, men are waking up. Men are eating bread and drinking coffee and checking rifles. Men who wear different uniforms and carry different flags and believe in a different Spain. Men who would kill him if they could see him, if they could aim at him, if the distance were shorter or the light were better or the wind were still.

He watches. The light strengthens. The shadows shorten. The valley fills with color — the green of the fields, the silver of the river, the gold of the stone, the dark of the trees. A bird calls from somewhere below the parapet. A lizard runs along the top of the wall and stops and lifts its head and runs again. He can smell the trench — the earth, the sweat, the gun oil, the faint sweetness of rotting sandbag burlap. He can feel the heat building on the back of his neck where the beret's band presses against the skin. He can hear, from somewhere down the line, the sound of a man singing — a hymn, faint, the words indistinct, the melody one he recognizes from the marches. The singing stops. The silence returns.

The shot comes without warning.

The sound is a crack — sharp, flat, immediate. He hears the bullet before he understands what it is: a snap, like a branch breaking close to the ear, and then the crack of the rifle, and then the silence, and in the silence the realization that the bullet passed close to his head. A piece of the parapet jumps. Stone dust rises. A chip of rock hits his cheek — he feels it now, a sting, a wetness, blood.

He drops. His body folds. He is in the trench on his back with the rifle across his chest and the sky above him — blue, empty, indifferent — and his heart is hammering against his ribs and his breath is coming in short hard bursts and his hands are gripping the rifle stock so tightly that his knuckles ache.

The taste of dirt. The smell of earth. The weight of the beret on his head.

Tomás is there. Tomás is crouching beside him with his hand on Martín's arm, and his face is calm — not the calm of a man who does not feel fear, but the calm of a man who has felt it and learned to carry it.

"Are you hit?"

"No."

"Then breathe."

Martín breathes. The air goes in and comes out. The heart slows. The hands loosen on the rifle. The sky is still blue. The parapet is still stones. The bird is still calling from below the wall.

He sits up. He looks at the rifle. He looks at his hands. The shaking is visible — a fine tremor in the fingers, a vibration in the wrists. He presses his hands flat on the dirt floor of the trench and waits.

"The first one," Tomás says. He takes out a cigarette. He lights it. He blows smoke at the sky. "You get used to it."

Martín does not believe him. He does not believe that the body can learn to accept this — the crack, the snap, the chip of stone, the blood on the cheek. He does not believe that a man can sit in a trench and eat bread and drink water and clean his rifle and sleep and wake and do it all again while somewhere across a valley a man with a rifle is trying to kill him.

But he picks up the rifle. He checks the chamber. He checks the safety. He moves back to the firing step. He climbs up. He puts his elbows on the parapet. He looks at the ridge.

The farmhouse is there. The wall is there. The church tower is there. The cross is there.

He watches. The sun is higher now. The valley is bright. The distance between the trench and the ridge is a thousand meters of open ground and green fields and a thin line of river, and across that distance someone is looking back at him through a sight, and the distance is the only thing that separates them, and the distance is not enough and it is everything.

The beret is hot on his head. He does not take it off.
