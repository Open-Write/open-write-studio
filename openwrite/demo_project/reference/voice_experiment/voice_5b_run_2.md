# THE STRUCTURED SPARSE — Run 2

The can was full and the can was foul and the can was the war's first honest thing.

He carried it down the sap — a shallow trench, barely knee-deep, screened by a mound of dirt and a piece of canvas that flapped in the morning breeze. Behind the canvas, a pit. He poured. He covered. He wiped his hands on his trousers. The shovel was caked with the same dirt and the same purpose.

Not cordite. Not blood. This was the smell of the war.

He walked back. The sun was up. Heat building. Sandbags damp, stones warm, corrugated tin shimmering faintly. The trench was thirty meters long and in those thirty meters twelve men were doing what men in trenches do in the morning — cleaning rifles, eating bread, drinking water, smoking cigarettes, sleeping, staring at nothing.

Tomás was cleaning the rifle. Tomás always cleaned the rifle. The rag was gray — white once, or at least lighter. The oil in a small tin bottle. The smell sharp, and he would never smell gun oil again without thinking of this trench, this morning, the way Tomás's hands moved over the metal with the care of a man polishing a chalice.

"Sleep?" Tomás asked.

"No."

"Nobody does. Not the first night."

He sat. He took the beret off — the first time since yesterday evening — and wiped the sweatband with his hand and put it back on. The wool was damp. It would be damp all day. It would be damp every day until the rains came in October, and then the damp would become wet and the wet would become cold and the cold would become the thing that killed more men than the bullets.

Erice came. The sergeant stopped. Looked down.

"Parapet."

He picked up the Mauser. Checked the chamber — loaded. Checked the safety — on. Moved to the firing step, a ledge of packed earth eighteen inches high, wide enough for his boots. Stepped up. The parapet at his waist. Elbows on the stones: rough. Sandbags: damp. Corrugated tin: warm where the sun touched it.

The valley.

Green fields running down to a river — thin, bright, the color of tin where the sun hit it. The far slope rising to a ridge. A thousand meters. Maybe more. The air was clear and the clear air made the ridge look close, close enough to see the stones of the wall and the shape of the farmhouse and the outline of the church tower. But the distance was real. The distance was a thousand meters of open ground and green fields and the thin bright river and the air itself, which was warm and still and carried no sound.

On the ridge: a farmhouse. Stone. Honey-colored. No roof. The rafters bare. The walls blackened at the top — the charring still black, not yet gray, not yet weathered. The fire was recent.

A wall. Low. A gap where stones had fallen. Wide enough for a man to walk through.

A church tower. Pale. Slender. The stone lighter than the farmhouse, as if the tower had been kept clean while everything else had been allowed to weather.

A cross at the top.

He rested the rifle on the parapet. Looked through the sights at the cross. Small at this distance. A dark shape against the sky, the sun behind it, turning it to a silhouette — sharp, clear.

He thought: the men behind that wall look at that cross every day.

He thought: the cross is the same cross that hangs above my father's table.

He thought: the cross does not know which side its tower is on.

He watched. The sun moved. Shadows shortened. Sweat ran under the beret — temples, jaw, chin, stones. The stones absorbed it. Dark circles.

Nothing moved on the ridge. Farmhouse still. Wall still. Tower still. Trees dark, motionless. Terraced fields empty. The river glinting at the bottom of the valley, thin as a thread.

A bird. High above. Singing. The song thin, clear, far away. He did not know what kind. He did not try to find out.

He could smell the trench from here — earth, sweat, gun oil, the faint sweetness of rotting burlap. He could feel the heat on the back of his neck where the beret's band pressed into the skin. He could hear, from somewhere down the line, a man coughing. The cough echoed. Then silence.

An hour. The sun above the ridge. Light hard, white. The valley shimmering.

The shot.

A crack. Flat. Dry. The bullet passed his head — a snap, a tearing. The parapet jumped. Stone dust rose. A chip hit his cheek. The sting. The wetness. Blood.

He dropped.

The body went first. The mind followed. He was on his back in the trench, the rifle on his chest, the sky above — blue, far, indifferent.

His hands. He held them up. They shook. The fingers trembled — the fine, rapid movement of muscles he could see but could not command. He put his hands on the dirt. Cool. He pressed. The dirt held. The shaking slowed.

Tomás was there. Crouching. Hand on arm. Grip firm. Hand warm.

"First time?"

"Yes."

"It passes."

He did not believe it. But he sat up. He picked up the rifle. Checked the chamber — loaded. Checked the safety — on. He looked at his hands. Less shaking now. He put his hands on the dirt floor and the dirt was cool and the coolness was a thing to hold onto.

He looked at Tomás. Sitting against the wall, rag in hand, tin of oil beside him, cigarette between his lips. Tomás had been here for two weeks. Two weeks of mornings and parapets and shots and drops and the return to the parapet.

"What happens after the first time?" Martín asked.

Tomás smoked. Blew the smoke at the sky. "You watch. You wait. You drop. You get up. You watch again."

"And then?"

"Then they shoot again."

He moved to the firing step. Climbed up.

The ridge. The farmhouse. The wall. The tower. The cross.

The distance was a thousand meters. The distance was what kept him alive. Wars close distances. They eat the space between men. They bring the rifle and the target together until the bullet does not have to fly so far.

A lizard ran along the parapet. Stopped. Lifted its head. Ran again. It did not know about the bullet. It knew the stones and the sun and the heat and the insects and that was enough.

He envied the lizard.

He thought: the bullet crossed a thousand meters in less than a second.

He thought: the next one might not miss.

He thought: I will watch anyway.

He thought about his father. The morning he left. The hand on the shoulder. "You will wear the beret." Not a command — a description. A fact. The way a man says the sun rises in the east. You will wear the beret because that is what the beret is for. Because that is what you are for.

The beret was hot. The rifle was warm. The cross held the light.

He did not take the beret off.
