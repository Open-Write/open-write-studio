# THE IMMEDIATE RECKONING — Round 3, Iteration 1, Run 3

The first thing is the smell. It is always the smell. Damp earth and cordite and the wool of the beret — the wool had been wet for days and it smelled of sweat and smoke and the particular animal smell of lanolin breaking down. I am not reconstructing this. I am sitting at my father's table and the smell is here, in the room, as real as the ink. The body does not file its memories in order. The body dumps them all on the floor and says: sort it out.

I will try.

July twenty-third. The day after the Magdalene. We reached the position in the evening — the last hour of light, the shadows long in the valley, the dust settling on our boots and our rifles and the red berets. I had said the Magdalene's prayer on the road. The sinner who became a saint. I held the prayer in my chest the way a man holds a coin in his pocket — not spending it, just knowing it was there.

The trench was a ditch. Shallow. The parapet stones and sandbags, the sandbags rotted and restuffed with dirt. A corrugated sheet of tin where the bags had collapsed. Someone had scratched a name into the tin — I could not read it in the dark, but I ran my thumb over the letters and the letters were deep and the hand that made them had pressed hard, the way a man presses when he wants the record to last.

Erice showed me where to lie. Compact man. Mustache. The quiet authority of someone who has stopped explaining. "Keep your head down after dark. They fire at muzzle flashes and sounds." He pointed to a tin can. "Piss in that. Anything else, wait for daylight and use the sap."

Tomás slept well. Beret over face, hands in armpits. The body of a man who had made a decision about sleep and was not going to revisit it. I lay beside him and listened to the dark. The dark had sounds — the tin rattling in the wind, someone coughing, the insects in the grass above the parapet. The dark had weight. The dark was the ridge above us and the valley below us and the men on the far slope who were, at that moment, also not sleeping, and also listening, and also waiting for the morning that would make us visible to each other.

I slept. I must have slept because I woke. Erice's hand on my shoulder. "Your turn. Watch the ridge."

I climbed up. Crouched. Looked.

The valley was green. After three days of dust — the white road, the yellow earth, the brown hills — the green hit me like water. Fields running down to a river. The far slope rising to a ridge dark with trees. Buildings on the ridge. A church tower. A cross at its peak, catching the first light.

My hand was on the parapet. The stone was cold. I can feel the cold now — not as memory but as fact, the way I feel the table under my forearms, the way I feel the pen between my fingers. The hand on the parapet. The hand on the table. The same hand, separated by thirty years and a war and a defeat that was also a victory and a victory that was also a defeat.

Somewhere behind those buildings, men were waking. Men who believed they were right. As right as I was. I have to say this now, while the saying costs something, before the retelling has sanded the edges and turned them into a category — the enemy, the Reds, the godless — when they were men. Catholic men, some of them. Basque men, some of them. Men who had the same crucifix in their churches and the same saints on their calendar and the same God listening to the same prayers. I did not know that then. I know it now. The knowing is the cost.

The shot came at full light.

A crack. Sharp. Immediate. The bullet passed my head. Not a whistle — a snap. Flat. Vicious. A chip of the parapet jumped and hit my cheek and I felt the blood before I understood what had happened. The body knew before the mind. The body was already dropping — legs folding, back against the wall, rifle across the knees — while the mind was still standing at the parapet, looking at the cross on the tower, thinking about the king.

I am at the table. My hands are flat on the wood. The inkwell is full. The crack runs through the left side where my mother set a pot too hot. My hands are steady.

They were not steady then. They shook. The rifle shook on my knees. My breath came in short hard gasps I could not control. I put my hands flat on the dirt floor and the dirt was cool and damp and I pressed my palms into it and the pressing was a kind of prayer — not to God, not to the king, not to the Magdalene, but to the dirt itself, to the physical fact of the ground beneath me, the ground that was holding me up, the ground that did not care about the crusade or the fueros or the cross on the tower or the bullet that had passed my head.

Tomás sat beside me. Cigarette. Smoke at the sky. "The first one is the worst."

"That was the first one."

"I know." He offered the cigarette. I took it. My hands shook and the cigarette trembled and the smoke was hot and I coughed, and Tomás said, "You get used to it."

The writing stops here. The pen is on the table. My hand is on the table. I am looking at the wall of the hardware store and the wall is plaster and the plaster is cracked and behind the plaster is the stone and behind the stone is Pamplona and behind Pamplona is the valley and the ridge and the cross on the tower and the boy behind the parapet who is, at this moment, thirty years ago, holding a cigarette that trembles and listening to a man tell him that the shaking will stop.

Tomás was right. The shaking stopped. Not that morning. That morning I sat in the dirt for a long time with my palms pressed into the earth and my heart in my throat and the beret hot on my head and the blood drying on my cheek. But by the next day the shaking was less. By the day after, it was only my hands. By the end of the week, nothing.

And the nothing was worse than the shaking. Because the shaking was the body telling the truth. The body saying: this is not normal. A bullet passed your head. A man tried to kill you. You are alive by an accident of wind and elevation and the quality of the enemy's sight. The shaking was honest. The nothing was the lie. The nothing was the body accepting that this was the world now — that bullets were not exceptional, that death was not extraordinary, that a man could live in the same space as his own extinction and eat his bread and smoke his cigarette and sleep his sleep.

The numbness. That is the word. Not the absence of feeling. The replacement of feeling with function. The body learned to operate in the space where fear had been, and the operation was smooth, and the smoothness was the wound.

I am at the table. The pen is in my hand again. The inkwell is full. The chair is uncomfortable. The crack in the table runs left to right, the way a river runs, the way a bullet runs, the way a line of text runs from margin to margin.

The boy is there. He is looking at the cross on the tower. He is thinking about the king and the fueros and the crusade. He is thinking about his father's hand on his shoulder and the words: "You will wear the beret." He does not know that the rightness is the thing that will break. He does not know that the king will not come. He does not know that in three years he will be sitting at a table in a hardware store trying to write this down, and that the writing will be harder than the fighting, because the fighting required only the body and the writing requires the thing the body cannot provide: the willingness to look at what happened and call it what it was.

I am looking. I am calling it.

The king did not come.
