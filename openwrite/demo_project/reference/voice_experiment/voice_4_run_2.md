# THE BODY — Run 2

The wool is against his forehead. The band of the beret — a strip of cotton sewn inside the wool, stiff with starch, the edge rough where the thread has been trimmed — presses into the skin above his eyebrows and the pressure is constant, the same whether he is standing or sitting or lying on his back in the dirt with the blanket pulled to his chin. He has been wearing the beret for sixteen hours. His father wore his for twenty years. The difference is that his father's beret had softened — the wool stretched, the band relaxed, the starch dissolved in sweat and rain and the oils of the skin — and this one has not. This one is new. This one is still a helmet.

He lies in the trench and looks at the sky. The sky is dark — not the dark of true night, which is black and total, but the dark of the hour before dawn, which is gray, which is the color of a ceiling seen through half-closed eyes. The stars are gone. The moon is gone. The sky is a flat, even gray that has no depth and no distance and no feature, and it is the ceiling of the trench and the ceiling is very far away and very close.

His body is mapped by the ground. The ridges of the packed earth — formed by rain, hardened by sun, scored by boots — are pressed into his back at six distinct points: the base of the skull, the left shoulder blade, the right shoulder blade, the small of the back, the left hip, the right hip. Each ridge has its own pressure, its own angle, its own insistence. He has tried shifting. The ridges find new positions. He has tried lying on his side. His shoulder takes all the weight and the shoulder goes numb and the numbness is worse than the ridges. He lies on his back and accepts the ground.

Light comes. It comes through the skin of his eyelids — a brightening, a shift from gray to pink to the red of blood held up to the sun. He opens his eyes. The trench walls are there. Pale, dry, the color of bone. The parapet is there. A line of stones against the brightening sky. The sandbags, soft and rotting, their burlap split in places, the filling spilling out in small dark heaps that attract ants.

He sits up. The spine cracks — three times, three places, the sound of knuckles being popped. The neck is stiff. He rolls the head and the neck resists and then gives and the giving is a relief that goes down through the shoulders and into the arms. He puts his hands on the ground and pushes. The ground is cool. The coolness is in the dirt and the dirt is in his palms and the palms are calloused from the hardware store — from the rope and the nails and the handles of the shovels and the axes and the hammers that he sold to the men of Pamplona for four years.

The rifle. He reaches for it. The stock is cool — the wood has lost the warmth of his hands during the night and has absorbed the coolness of the ground. The barrel is cool. The metal of the bolt is cool. The trigger guard is cool. He runs his hand along the stock and the wood is smooth where his grip has been and rough where it has not and the roughness is the grain of the beech, raised by the moisture of his sweat, and the smoothness is the wear of his hand.

He checks the chamber. The bolt pulls back with a sound — metal on metal, the spring compressing, the extractor claw gripping the rim of the case. The round is there. Brass, copper, the bullet tip dull gold. He pushes the bolt forward and the round slides into the chamber and the bolt locks and the bolt handle turns down and the rifle is loaded and the loadedness is a weight in his hands that is different from the weight of the unloaded rifle, though the difference is grams, though the difference is nothing, though the difference is everything.

Erice. The sergeant's boots on the dirt — the soft thud, the scrape, the rhythm. Erice stops. Erice looks down. Erice's face is a map of Morocco — the lines of the sun, the scar on the chin from a Riffian blade, the mustache that needs trimming.

"Parapet."

One word. Martín stands. He moves to the firing step. The step is a ledge of packed earth, eighteen inches, the width of his boot. He steps up. The parapet is at his waist. He puts his elbows on the parapet and the stones are rough and the sandbags are damp and the corrugated tin is warm where the new sun touches it.

He looks.

Green. The valley is green. Not the green of Navarre — not the deep, wet green of the Basque hills, where the grass grows to the waist and the rain falls every third day and the rivers run brown with peat. This is the green of Castile — dry, pale, the green of crops that grow in dust and die in heat. The fields are terraced. The walls between the terraces are low stone, the same stone as the farmhouse, the same stone as the church tower. The olives are dark against the green — their trunks twisted, their leaves gray-green, their roots gripping the terraced hillside with the tenacity of things that have been growing in the same place for a hundred years.

The river is at the bottom. Thin. Bright. The sun is on it and the sun turns the water to silver and the silver is a line that runs the length of the valley, from the left edge of his vision to the right, unbroken, continuous, the only straight line in a landscape of curves and terraces and slopes.

The ridge. He lifts his eyes. The ridge is a thousand meters away — twelve hundred, maybe, the distance is hard to judge because the air is clear and the clear air makes things look closer than they are. On the ridge: the farmhouse. Stone. Honey-colored. The walls are standing but the roof is gone. He can see the rafters — dark lines against the sky, the ends charred where the fire took them. The fire was recent — the charring is still black, not yet gray, not yet weathered. Below the farmhouse: the wall. Low, running along the ridge, built of the same stone. The wall has a gap in it — a place where the stones have fallen or been pushed or been hit by something heavy. The gap is wide enough for a man to walk through.

Above the wall: the tower. The church tower is slender, pale, older than the farmhouse, older than the wall. It rises above the wall by the height of two men and at its peak is the cross. The cross is iron or wood — at this distance he cannot tell. The cross is dark against the sky. The sun is behind it and the sun turns the cross to a silhouette and the silhouette is sharp and clear and the shape is the shape that has hung above every altar and every grave and every door of every church in Spain for five hundred years.

He watches. His hands are on the rifle. The right hand on the grip — the fingers curled, the index finger along the trigger guard, the thumb on the safety. The left hand on the forend — the palm flat, the fingers wrapped, the wood warm now from the heat of his skin. The rifle is an extension of his arms. The rifle is a thing his body knows.

Sweat. The hairline first — a dampness, a welling, a slow accumulation that breaks into a trickle and the trickle runs down the temple and along the jaw and drips from the chin. Then the back of the neck — the collar of the shirt is damp, the wool of the beret's band is damp, the skin is slick. Then the small of the back — the shirt clings, the belt is wet, the waistband of the trousers is a line of moisture.

An hour. The sun moves. The shadows shorten. The heat builds. The valley shimmers faintly — not visibly, not with the eye, but with the sense that the air itself is moving, that the distance between the trench and the ridge is not fixed but fluid, that the thousand meters is expanding and contracting with the heat.

The shot.

The body knows before the mind. The body hears the crack and the snap and the body drops — knees, waist, shoulders, the sequence of collapse that is written in the muscles and the bones and the nerves, that does not require thought, that does not require permission. He is in the trench. On his back. The rifle across his chest. The stock against his right shoulder. The barrel pointing at the sky.

The sky is blue. The sun is above the parapet. The warmth of the sun is on his face.

His hands. He looks at them. They are shaking. The fingers are trembling — not a vibration, not a tremor, but a shaking, a rapid back-and-forth that he can see and feel and cannot stop. He grips the rifle. The shaking transfers. He lets go. He puts his hands on the dirt. The dirt is cool. He presses. The dirt holds.

His heart. In the chest. In the throat. In the temples. In the wrists. In the backs of the knees. The heart is fast — faster than he has counted, faster than he has felt, and the beats are not regular but arrhythmic, the heart stumbling over itself in its urgency to pump the blood to the muscles that need it.

His mouth. Dry. The tongue thick. The throat clicking when he swallows. He reaches for the canteen and the hand shakes as he unclips it and he drinks and the water is warm and metallic and the warmth goes down into his stomach and the stomach accepts it.

Tomás. Tomás's hand on his arm. Tomás's face above him. Tomás's eyes — calm, steady, the eyes of a man who has been here before.

"Are you hit?"

He checks. He runs his hands over his head, his face, his chest, his arms. No blood. No wound. No pain that was not there before.

"No."

"Then breathe."

He breathes. The air goes in. The air goes out. The body calms. The hands slow. The heart finds its rhythm. The shaking fades — from shaking to trembling to nothing.

He sits up. He picks up the rifle. He checks the chamber. He checks the safety. He moves to the firing step. He climbs up.

The ridge. The farmhouse. The wall. The tower. The cross.

The parapet has a mark on it — a fresh chip in the stone, the size of a fingernail, the color of the inside of bread. The chip is six inches from where his head was. He looks at the chip. He looks at the place where his head was. The six inches is the width of his hand. The width of his hand is the distance between the bullet being in the stone and the bullet being in his head.

He watches. He does not move. The beret is on his head. The rifle is in his hands. The cross is on the tower. The sun is in the sky.

He watches.
