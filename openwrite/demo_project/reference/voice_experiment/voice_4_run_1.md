# THE BODY — Run 1

The dirt is in his nails. It has been there since yesterday — packed under the left thumbnail where he gripped the shovel during the march, ground into the creases of the right hand where he held the rifle. He picks at it with the thumbnail of the other hand and the dirt comes away in small dark crescents that fall onto the blanket and do not move.

His spine hurts. The ground is hard — packed earth, dried into ridges by the rain that came before the drought that came before the war — and the ridges press into the small of his back and between his shoulder blades and the base of his skull. He has been lying on this ground for six hours and his body has mapped every ridge and every stone and every place where the dirt is harder than the dirt beside it. He shifts. The ridges find new places. He shifts again. The blanket, thin wool, the color of nothing, slides against the collar of his shirt and the shirt is stiff with sweat and dust and the wool of the beret, which he has not taken off since yesterday evening, which his father told him to sleep in, which the requeté tradition demands he wear at all times, even in the dark, even on his back, even in the dirt.

The beret is heavy. Not the wool — the wool is light, three hundred grams at most, the weight of a handful of flour. The weight is the rest of it: the tradition, the century of wars, the grandfather who wore it, the father who wore it, the hook in the hallway where it hung waiting. The weight sits on his skull like a hand. He can feel it — the band pressing against his forehead, the crown resting on the top of his head, the rim touching his ears. The wool is damp already, from the sweat of the night, from the heat of his head, from the moisture of the ground that has crept into everything.

Light comes. He sees it through his eyelids — the red of blood, the orange of the sun, the slow brightening that turns the dark to gray to the pale dry color of the trench walls. He opens his eyes. The walls are close — arm's length, less. The parapet is above him, a line of stones and sandbags against the brightening sky. The sky is the color of a washed shirt.

He sits up. His spine pops — three distinct cracks, at the base, in the middle, at the top. His neck is stiff. He rolls his head and the joints in his neck crack and the sound is loud in the quiet of the trench. He puts his hands on the ground and pushes himself up and the ground is cool and the coolness goes through his palms and into his wrists and the wrists are sore from the weight of the rifle he carried for three days.

The rifle is beside him. He picks it up. The stock is wood — beech, the armorer said, though Martín cannot tell one wood from another — and the wood is smooth where his hands have held it and rough where they have not. The barrel is steel and the steel is cool in the morning air and he runs his hand along it and feels the smoothness and the faint ridges where the rifling is cut and the muzzle, which is open and dark and the diameter of a finger. The rifle weighs four kilograms. He knows this because the armorer told him. He knows it in his hands because his hands have carried it for three days and the weight has settled into the muscles of his shoulders and his forearms and the small of his back.

He checks the chamber. The bolt slides back and the chamber is clean and the round is seated — brass, copper, the tip of the bullet visible, dull gold. He slides the bolt forward and the round locks into place and the bolt handle turns down and the rifle is ready. He checks the safety. The safety is a small lever on the right side of the receiver and he pushes it forward with his thumb and it clicks and the rifle is safe.

Erice is moving through the trench. The sergeant's boots are on the dirt — Martín can hear them, the soft thud of leather on packed earth, the scrape of the sole on a loose stone, the rhythm of a man who knows where every step goes. Erice stops at Martín's position. Martín looks up. Erice's face is above him — the mustache, the jaw, the eyes that do not move.

"Parapet. Watch. Report."

Three words. Martín nods. He picks up the rifle and moves to the firing step. The firing step is a ledge of packed earth, eighteen inches high, wide enough for his boots. He steps up. The parapet is at his waist. He puts his elbows on the parapet and the stones are rough under his elbows and the sandbags are soft and damp and the corrugated tin is warm where the sun has touched it.

He looks out.

The valley is below him. Green — the green of fields, the green of trees, the green of grass along the river. The river is thin, a line of silver at the bottom of the valley, and it catches the light and throws it back. The far slope rises — terraced, divided by low stone walls, planted with olives that are dark and twisted and old. The ridge is above the trees. On the ridge, buildings. Stone. A farmhouse — he can see the walls, the color of honey, and the place where the roof should be and is not. A wall, low, running along the ridge. A gap in the wall. A church tower, rising behind the wall, pale, slender, with a cross at the top.

The cross catches the light. The light is new — the sun has just cleared the ridge and the light is the color of water and it lies across the valley like a cloth and the cross is the first thing it touches.

He watches. His hands are on the rifle. The right hand on the grip, the fingers curled around the stock, the index finger along the trigger guard. The left hand on the forend, the palm flat against the wood, the fingers wrapped around the barrel. The rifle is warm now — his hands have warmed it. The stock is warm. The barrel is warm. The metal of the trigger guard is cool against the side of his index finger.

Sweat. He feels it start — the hairline, the temples, the hollow at the base of the skull where the beret's band presses against the skin. The sweat forms and runs and he feels it — a trickle, a line of wetness that follows the path of least resistance down the side of his face and along the jaw and drips from the chin onto the stones of the parapet. The stones absorb it. Dark circles appear where the drops land.

He watches the ridge. The buildings are still. The wall is still. The tower is still. The cross is still. Nothing moves. The trees are dark and motionless. The terraced fields are empty. The river glints. The air is warm and still and the smell is dust and earth and the gun oil on the rifle and the sweat on his own body and the faint, sweet rot of the sandbags where the burlap has split and the filling is spilling out.

An hour. He knows it is an hour because the sun has moved — from the edge of the ridge to above it, from the color of water to the color of white fire, from a thing he can look at to a thing he cannot. The shadows have shortened. The valley is brighter. The heat is building and he can feel it on his face and his hands and the back of his neck and the wool of the beret, which is now fully damp, which is now a weight that he can feel not just on his skull but in his skull, pressing down, pressing in.

The shot.

The sound arrives first — a crack, flat and dry, the sound of a branch breaking, the sound of a whip, the sound of the air itself being split. He hears it in his right ear — the ear that is turned toward the ridge, the ear that is closer to the source. The sound is followed by a second sound — a snap, a hiss, a tearing — that is the bullet passing his head. The bullet is close. He feels it. Not with his hands — with his face. The air moves. The displacement of air, the pressure wave of a object moving faster than sound, touches the skin of his left cheek and the skin moves — not much, not visibly, but he feels it, the way you feel a breath on the back of your neck.

He drops. The body folds — knees first, then waist, then shoulders. The rifle comes with him. He is in the trench on his back and the rifle is across his chest and the stock is against his right shoulder and the barrel is pointing at the sky. The sky is blue. The sun is above the parapet, warming the stones, and the warmth radiates down and onto his face.

His hands. He looks at them. They are shaking. The fingers are trembling — a rapid, fine oscillation that he can see but cannot stop. He grips the rifle tighter. The shaking transfers to the rifle. He lets go. He puts his hands flat on the dirt floor. The dirt is cool. The coolness is a thing to hold. He presses his palms into the dirt and the dirt gives slightly and the giving is steady and the steadiness helps.

His heart. He can feel it — in his chest, in his throat, in his temples, in the backs of his knees. The heart is beating fast, faster than he has ever felt it, and the beats are not the regular rhythm of a resting heart but the uneven, accelerating rhythm of a heart that has been told by the body that the body is in danger.

His mouth. It is dry. The tongue is thick. He swallows and there is nothing to swallow and the throat clicks and the click is loud. He reaches for the canteen. His hand shakes as he unclips it. He drinks. The water is warm and metallic and it goes down his throat and into his stomach and the stomach accepts it and the dryness lessens but does not leave.

Tomás is there. Tomás crouches beside him. Tomás's hand is on his arm — the left arm, the one closest, the one that is not holding the rifle. The hand is warm and dry and the grip is firm and the firmness is steady.

"Are you hit?"

"No."

"Then breathe."

He breathes. The air goes in through the nose and out through the mouth and the breathing slows and the heart slows and the hands stop shaking. Not all at once — the shaking lessens, decreases, fades, until it is only a tremor in the fingertips, until it is nothing.

He sits up. The rifle is still across his chest. He picks it up. He checks the chamber. Still loaded. He checks the safety. Still on. He moves to the firing step. He steps up. He puts his elbows on the parapet.

The ridge is there. The farmhouse is there. The wall is there. The church tower is there. The cross is there.

The parapet has a new mark on it — a chip in the stone, six inches from where his head was. The chip is fresh. The stone beneath it is lighter than the surface. He looks at the chip and he looks at the place where his head was and the distance between the chip and the place is six inches and the six inches is the difference between the chip being in the stone and the chip being in his skull.

He watches. The sun climbs. The heat builds. The sweat runs. The beret is heavy. The rifle is warm. The cross on the tower holds the light.

He does not move.
