# THE IMMEDIATE RECKONING — Round 3, Iteration 1, Run 1

The smell came first. It always does. Damp earth and cordite and the sour wool of the beret against my forehead — the wool had been wet for three days and it smelled of sweat and smoke and something else, something I would not have a word for until later, when I was back in Pamplona and standing in the church and the incense hit me and I was in the trench again, on my back, with the ridges of packed earth pressing into the base of my skull.

I can feel those ridges now. Here. At this table. The wood is smooth under my forearms — my father's table, the one he kept his ledgers on, the inkwell in the same corner, the same crack running through the left side where my mother set a pot too hot once and the wood split and my father said nothing and my mother said nothing and the crack stayed. The table is smooth and the trench was not and the body remembers both.

We reached the position on the evening of July twenty-third. The day after the Magdalene. I had said her prayer on the road — the sinner who became a saint — and the prayer sat in my chest like a coin. Something I could reach for if I needed it.

The trench was a ditch. Shallow. Crumbling. The parapet stones and sandbags, the sandbags rotted in places and restuffed with dirt. A corrugated sheet of tin where the bags had collapsed. The whole thing smelled of damp earth and old tobacco and the particular sourness of men who have been wearing the same clothes since Pamplona.

Erice showed me where to lie. Compact man. Mustache. The quiet authority of someone who has stopped explaining. "Keep your head down after dark. They fire at muzzle flashes and sounds." He pointed to a tin can. "Piss in that. Anything else, wait for daylight and use the sap."

The sap was a shallow trench behind the main position to a latrine pit screened by dirt and canvas. The canvas stolen from a supply truck. The supply truck stolen from the Republic. The Republic had received it from the French, who sold it to both sides. The war was full of these circuits.

I lay down. Tomás beside me, sleeping well — beret over face, hands in armpits. I watched the dark and the dark watched me. At some point I slept. I know I slept because I woke, and waking meant I had been asleep, though it did not feel like sleep. It felt like the dark had rearranged itself and I was still inside it.

Before dawn, Erice. "Your turn. Watch the ridge."

I climbed up. Crouched. Looked.

The valley was green. After three days of dust — the white road, the yellow earth, the brown hills — the green was a kind of violence. Fields running down to a river. The far slope rising to a ridge dark with trees. Buildings on the ridge. A church tower. A cross at its peak, catching the first light.

My hand was on the parapet. The stone was cold. I remember the cold — not because it mattered, but because my hand was there and the cold was there and the two things met and the meeting is still in my palm, thirty years later, at this table, in this chair, with the inkwell open and the pen waiting. The hand on the parapet. The hand on the table. The same hand.

Somewhere behind those buildings, men were waking. Men who believed they were right. As right as I was. With the same certainty, the same prayers, the same conviction that God was on their side. I am saying this because I have to say it now, while the saying costs something, before the years of retelling have sanded the edges smooth and made the enemy into a abstraction and the war into a story.

The shot came at full light. A crack — sharp, immediate. The bullet passed my head. Not a whistle. A snap. Flat, vicious. A chip of the parapet hit my cheek. Blood.

I dropped. My legs folded. I was in the dirt — back against the wall, rifle across my knees, breath in short hard gasps. My hands shaking. I put them flat on the dirt. Cool. Damp. I pressed.

Tomás sat beside me. Cigarette. Smoke at the sky. "The first one is the worst."

"That was the first one."

"I know." He offered the cigarette. I took it. My hands shook and the cigarette trembled and the smoke was hot and I coughed, and Tomás said, "You get used to it."

He was right. That is the thing I need to say. He was right, and the rightness of it is worse than the fear was. Because the fear was honest. The fear was the body telling the truth — that a bullet had passed my head, that a man had tried to kill me, that I was alive by an accident of wind and elevation and the quality of the enemy's sight. The fear was the body's refusal to accept what was happening. And then the body accepted it. The shaking stopped. The hands steadied. The cigarette stayed still between the fingers. And the acceptance was the wound. Not the bullet. The acceptance.

I did not know that then. I knew the rifle. I knew the beret. I knew the farmhouse across the valley where someone who believed in a different Spain was looking through a sight at the place where I stood.

I put my eye to the parapet again.

The boy is there. He is looking at the cross on the tower. He is thinking about the king and the fueros and the crusade. He is thinking about his father's hand on his shoulder and the words: "You will wear the beret." He does not know that the rightness is the thing that will break. He does not know that the king will not come. He does not know that in three years he will be sitting at a table in a hardware store trying to write this down, and that the writing will be harder than the fighting, because the fighting required only the body and the writing requires the thing the body cannot provide: the willingness to look at what happened and call it what it was.

I am looking. I am calling it.

The king did not come.
