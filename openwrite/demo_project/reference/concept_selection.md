# Concept Selection — Editorial Panel Evaluation

*Three concepts for a Spanish Civil War novel, evaluated against eight dimensions. No hedges. A decision is made.*

---

## CONCEPT 1: THE RED BERET

**Dramatic Power: 9/10** — The premise generates inevitable conflict from a single structural fact: the Carlists provide the blood, the faith, and the infantry for the Nationalist victory, and Franco repays them by absorbing their movement and burying their king. The stakes are personal (Martín's faith, his father's legacy, his red beret) and historical (the fueros, the Carlist monarchy, the Church's alignment with a dictator). The conflict does not require contrivance — it is built into the historical architecture. The unification decree is not a plot twist; it is a historical fact that functions as a knife.

**Tragic Coherence: 9/10** — The tragic arc is structurally flawless. Martín's downfall comes from his own nature: he is a traditionalist who helps destroy tradition. He fights for a king Franco will never crown. He is Catholic in a movement that will be subordinated to a secular dictatorship. His virtues — faith, loyalty, willingness to sacrifice — are the instruments of his subordination. The tragedy is not that he fails; it is that he succeeds, and success is the mechanism of loss. This is the rarest form of tragic structure: the victory that is also a defeat.

**Moral Complexity: 8/10** — The concept achieves genuine moral complexity. Comandante Salazar (the Falangist) is not a villain — he is himself being subordinated by Franco while believing he serves José Antonio's radical vision. Padre Joaquin's theological struggle (is blessing a bullet the same as blessing a soul?) is real and unresolved. Don Eusebio's grief is the grief of a man who sent his son to fight for something that will never arrive. Franco remains offstage as a force, not a character — correct for this story. The Carlist cause is presented with full conviction: these men believe in something specific and beautiful, and they are brave. The only limitation is that the exclusively Nationalist vantage means the Republic appears only as the enemy across the line, not as a world with its own moral architecture.

**Historical Grounding: 9/10** — The concept is built from specific, documented events: the unification decree (February 17, 1937), the northern campaign (Carlist units fighting Basque Nationalists — Catholic men fighting Catholic men for the same fueros from opposite sides), Teruel, the Ebro, the fall of Madrid. The historical texture is not decorative; it is load-bearing. The Carlist-Franco tension is a real historical fact, not an invention. The Northern Campaign sequence — where requeté soldiers enter Basque towns their ancestors would have recognized, now enemy territory — is the kind of scene that could only exist in this war, in this place, with these people. The story would collapse without the historical setting.

**Novelistic Scope: 8/10** — Five acts mapped to the war's arc provide clear structure. Martín's single POV enforces focus and inevitability — the reader moves forward with him toward a victory that is also a defeat. The internal journey (faith → doubt → hollowed conviction) sustains 80,000–100,000 words. The risk: the exclusively military vantage point may limit the range of scenes. Ministries, ports, and offices are unavailable; this is trenches, battalion camps, Pamplona streets, and the inside of Martín's head. The Carlist calendar device (each act opens with a feast day connecting to the 19th-century Carlist wars) adds historical depth without flashback. The scope is sufficient but requires disciplined scene construction to avoid monotony of setting.

**No-Villain Rule: 9/10** — This concept handles the no-villain rule better than the other two, precisely because it takes the perspective most readers will instinctively resist. The Carlists are not sympathetic default — they are ultra-Catholic monarchists who fought for a theocratic vision. The concept forces the reader to take their cause seriously, to understand its beauty and specificity, and then to watch Franco destroy it. Salazar is the most sympathetically rendered antagonist across all three concepts — a man who enforces the unification while being consumed by it. The chaplain's doubt is genuine theological struggle, not authorial judgment. Franco is a historical force, not a cartoon. The risk — and it is real — is that readers may resist spending 300 pages inside a Carlist's head. But the concept is designed to make that resistance the point: if the reader does not believe in what Martín believes in, the betrayal has no weight.

**Structural Misdirection: 9/10** — The misdirection is the strongest of the three concepts. The reader, for the first two acts, believes this is a war story — a young man's journey through the crusade, the camaraderie of the Requeté, the moral clarity of fighting for faith and king. The Carlist cause is presented with full conviction. The unification decree (Act III) is the turn: the reader realizes the story is not about the war at all. It is about what happens after the crusade succeeds — how a movement's victory becomes its erasure. The reader has been watching a man march toward his own subordination and calling it heroism. This is genuine misdirection — it recontextualizes everything without overturning it. The reader's belief in the crusade is not betrayed; it is revealed as the mechanism of the protagonist's destruction.

**Catharsis: 9/10** — The catharsis is the rarest and most devastating kind: the catharsis of victory hollowed out. Martín survives. He goes home. He hangs up his red beret. The crucifix remains on the wall, but the God it represents has been claimed by the regime. This is not the catharsis of defeat (which is sad) or martyrdom (which is ennobling). It is the recognition that winning was the mechanism of loss. The ending image — Don Eusebio hanging the wooden crucifix on the wall and saying nothing — is earned and devastating. It carries the weight of a century of Carlist wars, a generation of blood, and the silence of a man who sent his son to fight for a king who will never come.

**COMPOSITE: 8.8/10**

**STRENGTHS:**
1. The tragic structure is genuinely novel — victory-as-defeat is rarer and more devastating than defeat-as-defeat, and the historical architecture supports it without contrivance.
2. The Northern Campaign sequence (Carlists fighting Basque Nationalists) is a scene that could only exist in this war — Catholic men killing Catholic men for the same fueros from opposite sides — and it is the moral center of the novel.
3. The unification decree functions as a structural hinge that recontextualizes everything the reader has believed, and it is a historical fact, not an invention.

**WEAKNESSES:**
1. The exclusively Nationalist POV may alienate readers who resist spending a full novel inside a Carlist's head — the concept requires the reader to take a theocratic monarchist vision seriously, which is a harder sell than the other two concepts.
2. The single military POV limits the range of available scenes — no ministries, no embassies, no civilian world beyond Pamplona letters. The novel must sustain itself on trenches, camps, and internal reflection.
3. The ending, while devastating, is quiet — Martín hangs up his beret, Don Eusebio says nothing. The risk is that the final note is a whisper rather than a chord.

**RISK:** The reader's resistance to the Carlist perspective. If the reader cannot take Martín's cause seriously — if they dismiss Carlism as mere reactionary Catholicism — the betrayal has no weight. The novel lives or dies on whether the reader believes in what Martín believes in.

## CONCEPT 2: THE TELEPHONE EXCHANGE

**Dramatic Power: 8/10** — The premise generates strong conflict: the revolution's enemy was never Franco — it was the Republic's own communists. The May Days are a genuine flashpoint, and the Telephone Exchange as the physical location where the revolution is built and then destroyed is a powerful central image. The dual POV (Montserrat in Barcelona, Pau on the Aragon front) provides range. However, the dramatic power is concentrated in the May Days (Act IV), and the subsequent acts risk feeling like denouement. The war continues for two more years after the revolution is killed, and the concept must sustain narrative momentum through a long decline.

**Tragic Coherence: 7/10** — The tragic arc is sound but less structurally tight than Concept 1. Montserrat's idealism leads her to build something real (the collectivized Telephone Exchange), and the revolution is destroyed by her own allies. But her downfall comes more from historical forces than from her own nature — she is not the architect of her destruction in the way Martín is. She is a witness to a murder, not the instrument of it. The tragedy is real, but it is the tragedy of the revolution, not specifically of Montserrat. Pau's disappearance is the emotional center, but it happens offstage — he is arrested, he disappears, the body is never recovered. This is historically accurate but narratively unsatisfying.

**Moral Complexity: 8/10** — Jordi (the childhood friend turned communist functionary) is a strong moral counterweight — he is not wrong that the war needs centralization, and his betrayal of Montserrat is motivated by genuine conviction, not malice. The anarchist vision is presented with full conviction: these people built something real. Volkov (the Soviet advisor) is the closest to a villain across all three concepts, but he is rendered as a functionary, not a monster — he uses the same methods he fled in Stalin's Russia, and he cannot see it. The dual-POV structure means the Aragon front provides a vantage on the war itself, not just the revolution. However, the Nationalist side is entirely absent — Franco's forces appear only as an external threat, not as a world with its own moral architecture.

**Historical Grounding: 7/10** — The concept draws on real events: the May Days, the POUM suppression, Durruti's death, the Retirada. The Telephone Exchange as a physical location is historically grounded. However, this is the concept most shadowed by prior literary treatment — George Orwell's *Homage to Catalonia* covers the same ground (the May Days, the POUM, the Aragon front) from a similar vantage. The novel would need to distinguish itself from Orwell's account, which is a real constraint. The anarchist revolution in Barcelona has also been extensively treated in fiction (C.J. Sansom's *Winter in Madrid*,民检察院 Bolaño's work, etc.). The concept is historically grounded but not historically unique.

**Novelistic Scope: 7/10** — The dual POV provides range, but the concept faces a structural challenge: the revolution is killed in May 1937, and the war continues until March 1939. Acts IV and V must sustain narrative momentum through a long decline — Montserrat working in the corpse of what she built, Barcelona bombed, the Retirada, the French concentration camps. This is the territory of epilogue, not of rising action. The risk is that the novel's second half feels like a coda. The Telephone Exchange as a central symbol (same room, same switchboard, different masters) is powerful but may not sustain 30,000 words of post-revolution narrative.

**No-Villain Rule: 8/10** — Jordi is the strongest "antagonist" in the structural sense — a childhood friend who genuinely believes centralization will win the war. His position is not wrong. Volkov is the weakest point: he is a Soviet functionary who uses fascist methods while wearing the revolution's uniform. The concept tries to render him as a functionary, not a monster, but the NKVD's role in Nin's murder makes true neutrality difficult. The anarchist side is presented with conviction, but the concept does not give the Nationalist side its most articulate version — Franco's forces are an external threat, not a moral counterweight.

**Structural Misdirection: 7/10** — The misdirection (a revolution story → a counter-revolution story) is real but less surprising than Concept 1's. Readers with any knowledge of the Spanish Civil War will know the May Days are coming. The concept relies on the reader believing the story is about whether the revolution can survive Franco, when it is actually about how the war devours the revolution from within. This is genuine misdirection, but it is more predictable — the May Days are the war's most famous internal crisis, and educated readers will be waiting for them.

**Catharsis: 7/10** — The catharsis is the quieter, more terrible catharsis of survival — of going on after the thing you lived for has been murdered by your own side. Montserrat in the French concentration camp, alongside the anarchists and communists who destroyed each other, sharing the same sand — this is a powerful image. But it is the catharsis of exhaustion, not of recognition. The reader does not learn something new in the final pages; they confirm what they already feared. The ending risks feeling like a long sigh rather than a blow.

**COMPOSITE: 7.6/10**

**STRENGTHS:**
1. The May Days are the war's most dramatic internal crisis, and placing the reader inside the Telephone Exchange when the shooting starts is viscerally powerful.
2. The Telephone Exchange as a central symbol — same room, same switchboard, different masters — is the kind of concrete, physical metaphor that anchors a novel.
3. Jordi as a childhood friend turned antagonist gives the political betrayal a personal dimension that pure factional conflict cannot achieve.

**WEAKNESSES:**
1. The novel's second half (after the revolution is killed in May 1937) must sustain 30,000+ words through a long decline — the Retirada, the camps, the fall of Barcelona. This is structurally difficult.
2. The Orwell shadow is real — *Homage to Catalonia* covers the same events from a similar vantage, and the novel must distinguish itself from a canonical work.
3. The Nationalist side is absent as a moral counterweight — Franco's forces appear only as an external threat, which reduces the concept's moral complexity relative to the other two.

**RISK:** The post-May-Days narrative. The revolution is killed in Act IV (of V). The novel must sustain itself for 25% of its length after its emotional center has been destroyed. If the Retirada and exile sections feel like epilogue, the novel collapses.

## CONCEPT 3: THE GOLD

**Dramatic Power: 7/10** — The premise generates conflict, but it is systemic rather than personal. The gold shipment is a genuinely dramatic historical event — 510 tons of national wealth shipped in the dark — and the transformation from salvation to subjugation is a strong arc. But Antonio is a treasury official. He manages logistics, not combat or revolution. His agency is limited to approving transactions and watching consequences unfold. The dramatic power comes from the situation, not from the protagonist's choices. He made one choice (the gold shipment) and then watches it play out for 800 pages. The other two protagonists have ongoing agency; Antonio has a single decision and its aftermath.

**Tragic Coherence: 7/10** — The tragic arc is intellectually coherent but structurally different from the other two. Antonio's pragmatism leads him to make the deal that saves the Republic while also selling it. But his downfall is not driven by his own nature — it is driven by the impossibility of his situation. He had no good options. Non-Intervention sealed the Western border. The Soviet Union was the only seller. The tragedy is the tragedy of the deal itself, not of the man. This makes it a systemic tragedy rather than a personal one — closer to a political novel than a Shakespearean tragedy. The other two protagonists are the architects of their own destruction; Antonio is the victim of circumstances.

**Moral Complexity: 8/10** — The concept handles moral complexity well. Elena (the Soviet handler) is both savior and colonizer — she facilitates the Republic's defense and its subjugation simultaneously. Fernando (the military officer) is a patriot who serves a foreign power's interests. Azaña authorizes what he hates because there is no alternative. The concept captures the Republic's impossible position: Non-Intervention was a death sentence, and Soviet aid came with Soviet control. Every character is the protagonist of their own justified story. However, the concept is more intellectually complex than emotionally complex — the moral dilemmas are real but rendered through policy, not through bodies.

**Historical Grounding: 9/10** — The gold shipment is one of the war's most documented events. The concept draws on specific, verifiable facts: 510 tons, 72.9% of reserves, four Soviet ships, Cartagena. The involvement of Azaña (a real historical figure) in key scenes adds authenticity. The financial vantage point — the cost of a Soviet tank in gold pesetas, the exchange rate of the peseta, the ledger as moral accounting — is historically grounded and novel. This concept uses history more precisely than the other two: the gold shipment is a single, datable event with documented consequences. The story would collapse without the historical setting.

**Novelistic Scope: 6/10** — This is the concept's greatest weakness. Antonio is not at the front — he is in ministries, ports, embassies, offices. The war arrives at his desk in the form of ledgers, requisitions, casualty reports, and diplomatic cables. Over 80,000–100,000 words, this vantage point risks becoming claustrophobic. The other two protagonists have physical journeys (Martín marches through Spain; Montserrat moves from Barcelona to France). Antonio's journey is bureaucratic — from Madrid to Cartagena to Madrid. The ledger as a central symbol is clever but may not sustain visceral prose. The concept is the most intellectually ambitious of the three, but it is also the hardest to render as a novel rather than a political treatise.

**No-Villain Rule: 8/10** — The concept handles the no-villain rule well. Elena is not a villain — she is a functionary operating under orders, and she genuinely facilitates the Republic's defense. Fernando is a patriot who believes Soviet discipline saved Madrid. Even Azaña, who authorizes the deal, is rendered with sympathy — he hates what the gold costs, but there is no alternative. The Nationalist side is entirely absent, however. Franco appears only as the threat that necessitates the deal. The concept's moral complexity is internal to the Republic — it does not engage with the Nationalist side's moral architecture.

**Structural Misdirection: 7/10** — The misdirection (a survival story → a sovereignty story) is real but more predictable than Concept 1's. The gold-as-mortgage metaphor is the concept's structural turn, and it is genuine. But the pattern — a desperate deal that saves you and enslaves you — is a familiar narrative shape (Faustian bargains, devil's deals). The reader may see the turn coming earlier than the concept intends. The May Days, again, serve as the pivot point — but in this concept, the May Days are filtered through Antonio's bureaucratic vantage, which reduces their visceral impact.

**Catharsis: 7/10** — The ending — Antonio burning the treasury records in a courtyard in Madrid, the gold in Moscow, the weapons rusted or destroyed — is symbolically strong. The ledger as moral accounting (gold out, weapons in, and the subtraction that consumed a nation) is a powerful final image. But the catharsis is intellectual rather than emotional. Antonio is not destroyed by the ending; he is diminished. He burns paper. The reader recognizes the tragedy but may not feel it in the body. The other two endings — Martín's silent crucifix, Montserrat in the concentration camp — are more viscerally devastating.

**COMPOSITE: 7.5/10**

**STRENGTHS:**
1. The gold shipment as a single, datable, documented event gives the concept a precision the other two lack — the reader can look up the facts and find them confirmed.
2. The financial vantage point is genuinely novel — no major Spanish Civil War novel has been told from the treasury. The ledger as moral accounting (gold pesetas per tank, per day of bombardment) is the kind of concrete detail that grounds abstraction.
3. The concept captures the Republic's impossible position more precisely than the other two — Non-Intervention, Soviet leverage, the Casado coup — and renders it through a single human decision.

**WEAKNESSES:**
1. Antonio is an observer, not an agent. He made one decision (the gold shipment) and then watches consequences unfold. Over 80,000 words, the bureaucratic vantage risks becoming claustrophobic.
2. The concept is more intellectually complex than emotionally complex. The moral dilemmas are real but rendered through policy, not through bodies. The novel risks reading as a political treatise.
3. The Nationalist side is entirely absent — Franco appears only as the threat that necessitates the deal. The concept does not engage with the moral architecture of the other side.

**RISK:** The vantage point. A treasury official in a ministry is not a natural protagonist for a 90,000-word novel. The concept must find a way to make the bureaucratic world viscerally real — to render ledgers and diplomatic cables with the same intensity the other two concepts bring to trenches and barricades. If the prose cannot bridge the gap between the intellectual and the visceral, the novel becomes a policy argument.

## SELECTION

**Winner: Concept 1 — THE RED BERET**

**Why it won:** The Red BerET's tragic structure is the most novel and the most devastating of the three: the catharsis of victory hollowed out, not of defeat. Martín's personal fate is inseparable from the historical arc — his traditionalism is the instrument of his subordination, and the unification decree is a historical fact that functions as a structural hinge without requiring invention. The Northern Campaign sequence, where Carlist requeté fight Basque Nationalists — Catholic men killing Catholic men for the same fueros from opposite sides — is a scene that could only exist in this war, in this place, with these people, and it is the moral center of a novel. The single-POV structure enforces inevitability: the reader moves forward with Martín toward a victory that is also a defeat, and cannot escape into another perspective. The ending — Don Eusebio hanging the crucifix on the wall and saying nothing — carries the weight of a century of Carlist wars and earns its silence.

**Why the others lost:**

*Concept 2 — THE TELEPHONE EXCHANGE:* The concept's dramatic power is concentrated in the May Days (Act IV), and the novel must sustain 25% of its length after its emotional center has been destroyed. The post-revolution narrative — the Retirada, the French camps, the fall of Barcelona — risks feeling like epilogue rather than rising action. The Orwell shadow (*Homage to Catalonia*) is a real constraint: the novel must distinguish itself from a canonical work that covers the same ground from a similar vantage. The Nationalist side is absent as a moral counterweight, which reduces the concept's moral ambition relative to the other two.

*Concept 3 — THE GOLD:* Antonio is an observer, not an agent. He made one decision and watched consequences unfold. The treasury vantage point is intellectually novel but viscerally limited — ledgers, diplomatic cables, and casualty reports are not the stuff of 90,000 words of sustained prose. The concept is more political treatise than Shakespearean tragedy. The ending, while symbolically strong (burning the records, the gold in Moscow), is intellectual rather than emotional — the reader recognizes the tragedy but may not feel it in the body.

**The risk to manage:** The reader's resistance to the Carlist perspective. If the reader cannot take Martín's cause seriously — if they dismiss Carlism as mere reactionary Catholicism — the betrayal has no weight. The novel lives or dies on whether the reader believes in what Martín believes in. **Mitigation:** The prose must render the Carlist vision with the same conviction Martín feels. The reader must experience the unification decree as Martín does — as a betrayal of something beautiful — not from an outside moral vantage. The novel's power comes from the reader's investment in the crusade; if that investment is withheld, the hollowing-out has nothing to hollow. The format rules and voice spec must enforce this: no authorial irony, no winking at the reader, no suggestion that Martín's beliefs are quaint. He believes in something specific and beautiful, and he is brave, and the regime he helped build takes it from him. That is the story.
