# TV Voice Guidance

*How to maintain multi-episode voice consistency across a TV season. Adapted from the dual-voice guidance for the multi-writer reality of television production.*

---

## The Principle

In a real TV writers' room, multiple writers write different episodes. The showrunner's job is to ensure the show sounds like one voice — one authorial sensibility across 600+ pages and 10 episodes. In this AI-assisted system, the same challenge exists: voice drift across episodes is a real risk.

**The goal:** Every episode should feel like it was written by the same hand. The viewer should not be able to tell which episode was "written" first or which scene was the hardest. The show has a voice. Every scene speaks it.

---

## The "One Writer" Test

Write 3 consecutive scenes from different episodes — Episode 2 Scene 5, Episode 5 Scene 3, Episode 8 Scene 7 — and have Lara read them cold. The question:

> "Does this feel like one writer or three?"

If Lara can tell the episodes were written at different times or with different framing, the voices haven't been unified yet.

### What Makes Episodes Feel Like One Author

- **Consistent sentence-level discipline** — not the same sentences, but the same standards
- **Shared thematic preoccupations** — every episode is asking the same questions
- **Compatible restraint** — no episode is more verbose than another
- **Matching specificity** — every episode earns its details with equal precision
- **Body-anchor consistency** — the same physical grounding vocabulary across episodes
- **Dialogue density consistency** — similar ratios of dialogue to action across episodes

## Character Voice Cards for TV

Every series regular needs a voice card that travels across episodes. The voice card is loaded for every scene that includes that character.

### Voice Card Structure

```markdown
# Character Voice Card: [NAME]

## Voice Registers
- **[Part 1 Name]:** [Description of how this part sounds]
- **[Part 2 Name]:** [Description of how this part sounds]
- **[Part 3 Name]:** [Description of how this part sounds]

## Verbal Habits
- [Specific speech pattern 1]
- [Specific speech pattern 2]
- [Specific speech pattern 3]

## Vocabulary
- Words this character uses: [list]
- Words this character never uses: [list]
- Sentence length tendency: [short/medium/long/mixed]

## Dialogue Examples (from locked episodes)
- "[Quote 1]" — Episode X, Scene Y
- "[Quote 2]" — Episode X, Scene Y
- "[Quote 3]" — Episode X, Scene Y

## Anti-Examples (what this character would NOT say)
- "[Bad example 1]" — Why: [explanation]
- "[Bad example 2]" — Why: [explanation]

## Physical Voice
- How this character moves: [description]
- Default body anchor: [hands/eyes/breath/spine/jaw]
- Gesture vocabulary: [specific gestures this character uses]

## Evolution Across the Season
- Episode 1-3: [How the character sounds early]
- Episode 4-6: [How the character sounds mid-season]
- Episode 7-10: [How the character sounds late season]
```

### Voice Card Rules

1. **Load the voice card before every scene** that includes that character.
2. **Update the voice card after every episode lock** — add new dialogue examples, note any evolution.
3. **The voice card is the authority** — if a line of dialogue contradicts the voice card, the dialogue is wrong.
4. **Evolution is allowed** — characters change across the season. But the evolution must be tracked in the voice card, not improvised in the moment.

## Show Bible Voice Reference

The show bible establishes the voice of the show itself — not just individual characters, but the show's tonal register, dialogue style, and action line discipline.

### Show Voice Elements

| Element | Description | Example |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| **Tonal register** | The show's overall tone | Dark comedy, prestige drama, procedural, thriller |
| **Dialogue style** | How characters speak | Naturalistic, heightened, poetic, staccato |
| **Action line discipline** | How much detail in action lines | Sparse (minimal), moderate (standard), detailed (literary) |
| **Silence ratio** | How much silence vs. dialogue | High, moderate, low |
| **Subtext density** | How much is said vs. implied | High, moderate, low |

### The Show Voice Lock

Before production begins, lock the show's voice:
1. Write 3 test scenes in the show's voice
2. Have Lara read them cold
3. Ask: "What show is this? What's the tone?"
4. If Lara's description matches the intended tone, the voice is locked
5. If not, adjust and re-test

### Per-Episode Voice Verification

After writing each episode, verify:
1. **Does the episode sound like the show?** Compare to the locked voice spec.
2. **Do the characters sound like themselves?** Compare to their voice cards.
3. **Is the dialogue density consistent?** Compare page counts and dialogue ratios to prior episodes.
4. **Is the action line discipline consistent?** Compare detail levels to prior episodes.

## Multi-Writer Voice Consistency

### The Challenge

In a real TV writers' room, different writers have different strengths:
- One writer is great at dialogue but weak on action lines
- Another is great at atmosphere but weak on plot advancement
- The showrunner's job is to sand off these differences so the show sounds uniform

In this AI-assisted system, the same challenge exists:
- Different episodes may be written in different sessions
- Different context windows may produce different writing
- The system must ensure consistency despite these variations

### Prevention Strategies

1. **Reload everything.** Before every scene, reload:
   - `bible/06_format_rules.md` (the discipline document)
   - The character voice cards for characters in the scene
   - The last 1-2 pages of the prior scene
   - The convention ledger for pattern awareness

2. **Use the convention ledger.** Track dialogue patterns, body anchors, sentence rhythms, and scene structures. If a pattern is emerging, the ledger will flag it.

3. **Run the voice critic on every scene.** One call per character. Check both intra-episode consistency and cross-episode consistency.

4. **The "cover the names" test.** After writing a scene, cover the character names. Can you tell who is speaking from the dialogue alone? If not, the voices aren't distinct enough.

5. **The "read aloud" test.** Read the scene aloud (or have the system read it). Does it sound like the same show as the last episode? If not, identify what's different and fix it.

### Voice Drift Detection

Voice drift happens gradually. By the time it's obvious, it may have contaminated several episodes. The voice critic must check:

1. **Vocabulary drift** — Is the character using words they wouldn't use?
2. **Sentence length drift** — Are the character's sentences getting longer or shorter?
3. **Verbal tic drift** — Are the character's speech patterns changing?
4. **Subtext density drift** — Is the character becoming more or less direct?
5. **Voice register drift** — Is the character's dominant register shifting without narrative justification?

### Voice Drift Correction

If voice drift is detected:
1. **Identify the source episode** — when did the drift begin?
2. **Compare to the voice card** — what changed?
3. **Rewrite the drifted scenes** — restore the original voice
4. **Update the voice card** — if the drift was actually evolution (narratively justified), update the card
5. **Re-run the voice critic** — verify the fix

## The Body-Anchor Technique

The body-anchor is the bridge between scenes and episodes. Every scene uses physical grounding — hands, eyes, breath, spine, jaw, feet. The reader's attention follows bodies across transitions.

### How to Use Body-Anchors Across Episodes

1. **At episode transitions:** End one episode with a body action. Begin the next episode with a body action. The viewer's attention follows the bodies across the week.
2. **At emotional peaks:** When emotion runs high, anchor it in the body. Hands gripping a table edge. Eyes that don't meet. Breath held too long.
3. **At callbacks:** When a callback pays off, use the same body anchor that was used in the seed. The viewer feels the connection before understanding it.

### Body-Anchor Variety

The convention ledger tracks body-anchor usage. If hands are becoming the default, switch to eyes or breath or spine. The goal is variety — not because any single body anchor is wrong, but because repetition of any anchor makes the show feel manufactured.

## Voice Consistency Across Episodes and Writers

### The Showrunner's Voice

In a real writers' room, the showrunner's voice IS the show's voice. Every writer filters their work through the showrunner's sensibility. In this AI-assisted system, the show bible and format rules serve as the showrunner's voice.

### The Episode Writer's Discipline

The episode writer must:
1. Obey the format rules absolutely (they are the showrunner's voice)
2. Use the character voice cards (they are the characters' voices)
3. Consult the convention ledger (it prevents drift)
4. If critics flag extraneous material, the cutter will address it
5. Check against prior episodes (it ensures consistency)

### The Voice Critic's Job

The voice critic must:
1. Check each character's voice against their voice card
2. Check each character's voice against their voice in prior episodes
3. Check the episode's overall voice against the show's voice
4. Flag any drift — vocabulary, sentence structure, subtext density, voice registers
5. Praise where register bleed-through creates dramatic richness

## Voice Experiment Protocol (for New Shows)

Before production begins on a new show, lock the show's voice through empirical evaluation:

1. **Write 3 test scenes** in the show's voice
2. **Have Lara read them cold** — ask "What show is this? What's the tone?"
3. **If the description matches:** Voice is locked. Proceed to production.
4. **If the description doesn't match:** Adjust the voice rules and re-test.
5. **Lock the voice spec** — a document describing the show's voice with examples and anti-examples.

### Voice Spec Structure

```markdown
# Locked Voice Spec: [SHOW TITLE]

## Tonal Register
[Description of the show's tone]

## Dialogue Style
[Description of how characters speak]

## Action Line Discipline
[Description of detail level]

## Silence Ratio
[How much silence vs. dialogue]

## Examples (from test scenes)
- "[Quote 1]" — This is the show's voice because [explanation]
- "[Quote 2]" — This is the show's voice because [explanation]

## Anti-Examples (what the show does NOT sound like)
- "[Bad example]" — Why: [explanation]

## Character Voice Summaries
- [Character 1]: [Brief voice description]
- [Character 2]: [Brief voice description]
```

## Key Insight

> "A TV show's voice is not the voice of any single episode. It is the voice that emerges across all episodes — the cumulative effect of 600 pages of consistent discipline. The viewer doesn't remember individual scenes. They remember how the show *felt*. That feeling is the voice."
