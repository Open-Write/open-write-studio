# TV Craft — What You Need to Know

*Distilled craft knowledge for episodic television production. Adapted from screenplay and novel craft guidance. Transferable to any TV project.*

---

## The Discipline Document

The single most important file in any TV project is `bible/06_format_rules.md`. It is reloaded every scene. Without it, the script swells. The key rules:

1. **No camera directions.** None. Not even "we see." TV directors rotate — each brings their own visual language.
2. **No emotional parentheticals.** (angrily), (sadly), (quietly) — all forbidden. If the dialogue doesn't carry the emotion, rewrite the dialogue.
3. **No adverbs in dialogue tags.** "She says quietly" — forbidden.
4. **No interiority in action lines.** "Lena remembers the funeral" — forbidden. "Lena's hand stops on the photograph" — allowed.
5. **Dialogue is subtext, not statement.** Characters do not name their emotional states.
6. **Trust the actor.** The action line says what is happening. The dialogue says what is said. The actor and director find the rest.

## TV Format Discipline

### Cold Opens, Act Breaks, and Tags

TV has a unique structural vocabulary that film does not:

**The Cold Open** (2-3 pages, before the title sequence)
- Must hook the audience with a question, tension, or arresting image
- Must set the emotional register for the episode
- Must do real narrative work — not a "previously on" or montage
- Must end on a turn that creates forward momentum
- The cold open is the episode's most important 2-3 pages

**Act Breaks** (structural turning points)
- For network TV: mark where commercial breaks go
- For streaming: mark structural turning points even without commercials
- Every act break must compel — the last image before the break should demand resolution
- Act breaks escalate — each raises stakes higher than the last
- The mid-episode break is the pivot — where understanding shifts

**The Tag** (1-3 pages, after the main structure resolves)
- Provides closure for the A-story (or deliberate refusal to close)
- Advances the B or C story with a final beat
- Leaves the audience with a final image that lingers
- Should be short — if the tag is longer than 3 pages, it's too long

### Page Count Targets

| Format | Target Pages | Runtime | Notes |
|--------|-------------|---------|-------|
| Half-hour comedy (single-cam) | 28-34 pages | 22-28 min | More dialogue, less action |
| Half-hour comedy (multi-cam) | 40-45 pages | 22 min | Different format (wider margins) |
| One-hour drama (network) | 55-65 pages | 42-48 min | Act breaks for commercials |
| One-hour drama (cable/streaming) | 55-70 pages | 48-58 min | More flexibility |
| Limited series | 55-65 pages | 48-58 min | Same as one-hour drama |

The 1-page ≈ 1-minute rule applies to TV as it does to film.

## Episodic vs. Serialized Storytelling

### The Spectrum

| Pure Episodic | Hybrid | Pure Serialized |
|---------------|--------|-----------------|
| Each episode is self-contained | Each episode has a self-contained A-story and a serialized B/C-story | Each episode is a chapter in a continuous story |
| Case-of-the-week | A-story + serialized arc | Continuous narrative |
| Audience can miss episodes | Audience benefits from watching in order | Audience must watch in order |
| Character development is slow | Character development is moderate | Character development is rapid |

### The Rule

Most great TV shows are hybrids. The procedural engine (case-of-the-week) keeps the audience engaged week to week. The serialized mythology keeps them coming back. The best shows make the procedural engine illuminate the serialized arc — the case this week is a metaphor for what the protagonist is going through.

### Self-Containment Test

Even in a heavily serialized show, each episode must have a beginning, middle, and end. The "Previously On" Test: if there were no "previously on" recap, would the audience still understand this episode? If the answer is no, the episode is too dependent on prior episodes.

## A/B/C Story Structure

Every episode operates on multiple story threads simultaneously:

### A-Story (Primary)
- The main plot of the episode
- Active in every episode
- Typically the protagonist's journey
- Receives the most screen time (60-70% of the episode)

### B-Story (Secondary)
- A subplot that illuminates or complicates the A-story
- Can rest for one episode maximum
- Often involves secondary characters
- Receives 20-30% of screen time

### C-Story (Mythology/Serialized)
- The long-arc story that spans the season
- Can be seeded with a single detail in early episodes
- Must be present (even as a whisper) by mid-season
- Receives 5-15% of screen time

### Thread Collision

The best episodes are ones where A, B, and C stories collide — when the procedural case reveals something about the mythology, or when the B-story character becomes central to the A-story. Plan at least 2-3 collision episodes per season.

## Episode Engines

Different episodes serve different functions in the season:

| Engine | Description | When to Use |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| **Case-of-the-week** | A self-contained problem drives the episode | Procedural shows, hybrid shows |
| **Mythology episode** | The serialized arc is the A-story | Mid-season, finales |
| **Bottle episode** | Limited locations, limited cast, dialogue-heavy | Budget episodes, character development |
| **Flashback episode** | Reveals backstory through non-linear structure | After the audience cares about the characters |
| **Flash-forward episode** | Shows the future to create dramatic irony | Rare, high-impact |
| **Clip show** | Recap with minimal new footage | Budget episodes (avoid if possible) |
| **Crossover episode** | Characters from another show appear | Network synergy (use sparingly) |
| **Two-parter** | One story across two episodes | Season premieres, mid-season, finales |
| **Stand-alone** | No serialized arc advancement | Early season, palate cleanser |

### The Bottle Episode

A bottle episode is one of the most powerful tools in TV. Limited locations (often one), limited cast (often 2-4 characters), dialogue-heavy, minimal action. It forces the writing to do the work. It's also the cheapest episode to produce.

**When to use:** When you need deep character development, when the budget is tight, when the audience needs a breather between high-action episodes.

**When NOT to use:** Early in the season (before the audience cares about the characters), as a finale (audience expects spectacle), too often (once or twice per season maximum).

## Season Arc Architecture

### The Three-Act Season

The season has its own rhythm, analogous to a three-act structure:

- **Act One (Episodes 1-3):** Setup. The audience learns the world, meets the characters, understands the central question.
- **Act Two (Episodes 4-7):** Complication. The stakes escalate. The central question deepens. The mid-season twist (usually Episode 5 or 6) is the moment where understanding shifts irrevocably.
- **Act Three (Episodes 8-10):** Crisis and resolution. The central question is answered (or deliberately left open).

### The Mid-Season Twist

The mid-season twist is the season's midpoint — the moment where the protagonist's understanding of the world shifts irrevocably. It should be:
- Genuinely surprising
- Retrospectively inevitable
- A point of no return for the protagonist
- The moment the audience realizes the show is deeper than they thought

### The Finale

The season finale must:
- Resolve the A-story (or deliberately refuse to resolve it)
- Provide closure for B-stories
- Either resolve or deliberately leave open the C-story
- Leave the audience with a final image that lingers
- If there's a next season: create a new question

## Cross-Episode Callback System

Every seeded item must have a payoff. Every payoff must have a seed. The callback ledger tracks this across episodes.

### Types of TV Callbacks

| Type | Span | Example |
|------|------|---------|
| **Intra-episode** | Same episode | A detail in Scene 2 pays off in Scene 15 |
| **Cross-episode** | Same season | A line in Episode 1 pays off in Episode 8 |
| **Cross-season** | Multiple seasons | A character detail in Season 1 pays off in Season 3 |
| **Visual callback** | Any | An image from one episode recontextualizes in another |
| **Dialogue callback** | Any | A line repeated or echoed across episodes |

### The Rule

If you plant it, you must pay it off. If you pay it off, you must have planted it. No exceptions.

### TV-Specific Callback Techniques

1. **The Echo Line** — A character says the same words in a different context. Same words, different meaning.
2. **The Visual Rhyme** — An image from one episode mirrors an image from another. The audience feels the connection before understanding it.
3. **The Recontextualization** — A detail that seemed innocuous in Episode 2 becomes devastating in Episode 8 when new information changes its meaning.
4. **The Payoff Delay** — The longer the delay between seed and payoff, the more powerful the payoff — but only if the audience remembers the seed.

## TV Pacing

### Episode Pacing

Every episode has a rhythm — a build from the cold open to the final image:

1. **Cold open** — The episode's most arresting moment. Hook the audience.
2. **Act One** — Establish the world of the episode. The case is introduced. Characters are in their routine.
3. **Act Two** — Disrupt the routine. The case deepens. The B-story introduces tension.
4. **Act Three** — The crisis. The case reaches its most dangerous point. The B-story collides with the A-story.
5. **Act Four** — Resolution or refusal to resolve. The character is changed (or confirmed in resistance to change).
6. **Tag** — The final image lingers.

### Season Pacing

The season has its own pacing:
- Episodes 1-3: Build the world. Don't rush.
- Episode 4: First major complication.
- Episodes 5-6: Mid-season twist. The point of no return.
- Episodes 7-8: Escalation. Stakes are at their highest.
- Episodes 9-10: Crisis and resolution. Don't rush the ending.

### Dialogue Density for TV

TV has more dialogue than film. A one-hour drama may have 40-50 pages of dialogue-heavy content. This creates two risks:

1. **Over-writing:** Characters talk too much. Scenes become conversations instead of dramatic events.
2. **Under-writing:** Characters talk too little. Scenes become atmospheric without advancing.

The balance: every scene must have at least one line of dialogue that advances the plot, reveals character, or shifts the power dynamic.

## Character Development Across Episodes

### The Long Arc

In a film, a character changes over 120 pages. In a TV season, a character changes over 600+ pages across 10 episodes. This allows for:
- Slower, more believable character development
- Multiple false starts and setbacks
- Subtle shifts that accumulate over episodes
- The "recognition moment" — when the audience realizes the character has changed

### Voice Architecture in Television

The voice architecture produces distinct character voices:
- Each character has multiple "registers" — tonal modes that emerge under different conditions
- Registers can evolve over time — a character's defensive register may weaken as they open up
- Track these changes in `state/character_state_tracker.json`
- Voice register names must never appear in action lines

### The Richest Moments

The richest moments are when one register is active and another is leaking through — when a character gives a precise professional answer in a voice that is starting to crack. That crack is the scene.

## Voice Consistency Across Episodes and Writers

In a real TV writers' room, multiple writers write different episodes. The showrunner's job is to ensure the show sounds like one voice. In this AI-assisted system, the same challenge exists — voice drift across episodes is a real risk.

### Prevention

1. Reload character profiles before every scene
2. Use voice registers as anchors
3. Run the voice critic on every scene
4. Track dialogue patterns in the convention ledger
5. The "cover the names" test — can you tell who is speaking without character names?

### The Show Bible Voice Reference

The show bible establishes the voice of the show. Every episode must be consistent with:
- The show's tonal register (dark comedy, prestige drama, procedural, etc.)
- The show's dialogue style (naturalistic, heightened, poetic, etc.)
- The show's action line discipline (sparse, detailed, literary, etc.)

## Special Episode Formats

### Bottle Episodes
- Limited locations, limited cast
- Forces writing to do the work
- Cheapest to produce
- Once or twice per season maximum

### Flashback Episodes
- Reveals backstory through non-linear structure
- Use after the audience cares about the characters
- The flashback must illuminate the present, not just fill in the past

### Two-Parters
- One story across two episodes
- Use for season premieres, mid-season, and finales
- The break between parts must be the most compelling act break in the season

### Cold Open Without Title Sequence
- Some shows skip the title sequence for special episodes
- The cold open flows directly into Act One
- Use sparingly — the title sequence is part of the show's identity

### The Room's Ethics

1. The human creator has final authority. AI modes propose; the human decides.
2. Track what the AI produces. Every scene, every revision, every critic output is logged.
3. The goal is the best show possible. Not the fastest show. Not the cheapest show. The best show.

## The Naturalism Problem

AI-generated text has identifiable tells. Readers who perceive AI authorship prejudge the work regardless of quality. The Naturalism Critic addresses this by detecting and flagging:

1. **Em-dash overuse** — Target: ≤2 per page. The single most reliable AI tell.
2. **Triplet closing patterns** — Three short sentences ending passages. Acceptable once per scene; mechanical when repeated.
3. **Inhuman style consistency** — Uniform sentence lengths across scenes. Human writers have bad days.
4. **Sentence construction overuse** — Negation-action pairs, parallel adjectives, corrective fragments.

**Key principle:** The goal is reduction to human-normal frequency, not elimination.

---

## The Conditional Cutter

The cutter runs only when critics or editorial flag extraneous material. No target percentage. Cut only what was flagged.
- If a sentence doesn't earn its place, cut it
- If two sentences say the same thing, cut one
- If an adverb modifies a verb that already carries the meaning, cut the adverb
- White space is your friend. A single sentence alone on its line is often the right choice.
