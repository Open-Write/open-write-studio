## I. SERIES CONCEPT

> [A single sentence — 25-40 words — that captures the protagonist, the situation, the stakes, and the unique hook. A producer should be able to read this and know whether they want to read the pilot.]
>
> *Example: A disgraced forensic psychologist is recruited by the FBI's Behavioral Analysis Unit to consult on cases that defy conventional profiling — only to discover that the patterns she's seeing connect to the childhood trauma she's spent twenty years suppressing.*

> *Example: "Threshold" — read it however you need to: the threshold of a door, the threshold of perception, the threshold of tolerance. The series does not resolve which.*

### The Central Question

> [The single question the series is asking — the one the protagonist is asked, the one the audience is asked. This is the engine of the show, not the premise.]
>
> *Example: If you could see the patterns behind human cruelty — every motive, every trigger, every escalation — would you use that knowledge to save people, or would it destroy you first?*

### Central Themes

> [List 4-6 themes the series explores. These are not plot points — they are the ideas the show is in conversation with.]
>
> *Example:*
> - *The cost of seeing what others cannot — and whether that sight is gift or curse.*
> - *Whether trauma is a wound or a lens.*
> - *The specific cruelty of institutions that use people's pain as tools.*
> - *Whether understanding evil inoculates you against it or invites it in.*
> - *What we owe the dead — and whether the living deserve that debt.*
> - *The difference between empathy and identification.*

### Series Bible Summary

> [2-3 paragraphs that describe the series as if pitching it to a network executive. Include the world, the protagonist, the ongoing engine, and why this show needs to exist now.]
>
> *Example:*
>
> *THRESHOLD follows Dr. Lena Vasquez, a forensic psychologist whose career imploded after she correctly predicted a mass shooting — and was ignored. Three years later, living in self-imposed exile and teaching community college, she's recruited by the FBI's newly formed Anomalous Behavioral Analysis Unit. The unit handles cases that don't fit standard profiles: crimes with no discernible motive, patterns that shouldn't exist, perpetrators who describe experiences they couldn't have had.*
>
> *As Lena digs deeper, she discovers that the anomalies aren't random — they're connected to a neurological phenomenon she first experienced as a child, the same one that ended her academic career when she tried to publish it. The patterns are real. The question is whether they're evidence of something human — collective trauma expressing itself through violence — or something else entirely.*
>
> *The series is a procedural that becomes a conspiracy thriller that becomes a meditation on consciousness. Each episode is a case. Each season is a deeper layer. The show asks: what if the thing we call "evil" has a structure, and what if that structure is visible to anyone willing to look — and what if looking is the most dangerous thing you can do?*

### Season Count & Arc

> [How many seasons do you envision? What is the series arc? Is this a closed-ended story or an open-ended one?]
>
> *Example: 3 seasons, 10 episodes each. Season 1: Lena discovers the pattern. Season 2: Lena follows the pattern to its source. Season 3: Lena must decide whether to reveal the pattern to the world. Closed-ended — the story has a conclusion.*

### Episode Engine

> [What happens in a typical episode? What is the procedural engine? This is what keeps viewers coming back week to week.]
>
> *Example: Each episode, Lena and the ABU team investigate a case that appears to be conventional crime but has an anomalous element — a perpetrator who describes a pattern they couldn't know, a victim whose behavior before the crime matches a mathematical sequence, a crime scene that violates forensic expectations. The A-story is the case. The B-story is Lena's personal arc. The C-story is the season mythology, seeded in the background.*

> *For streaming shows, you may use no act breaks at all — the episode plays as a continuous piece. For network shows, each act break must be a hook that prevents the viewer from changing the channel.*

### Cold Open / Teaser

> [Does each episode have a cold open before the title sequence? What is its purpose?]
>
> *Example: Each episode opens with a cold open (2-3 pages) showing the crime or anomaly from an unexpected perspective — often the perpetrator's or the victim's, before we understand what we're seeing. The cold open ends on an image or line that recontextualizes once the episode is complete.*

### Title Sequence

> [Is there a title sequence? How long? When does it play?]
>
> *Example: 60-second title sequence plays after the cold open. The sequence is a montage of forensic diagrams that slowly resolve into patterns — neural networks, constellations, crime scene tape. No characters appear. The sequence is the same every episode (like True Detective Season 1) but the images become more recognizable as the season progresses.*

## III. WHAT MAKES THIS SHOW DIFFERENT

> [2-3 paragraphs on the unique selling proposition. What does this show do that no other show does? Why does it need to exist?]
>
> *Example:*
>
> *THRESHOLD is not a procedural that happens to have a mythology. It is a mythology that wears the clothing of a procedural. The case-of-the-week structure is real — each episode delivers a complete investigation with a resolution. But the resolutions are increasingly unsatisfying, because Lena is beginning to see that the cases aren't isolated incidents. They're symptoms. The show rewards attentive viewing: details that seem like production design in Episode 3 become plot-critical in Episode 8. The pattern is there from the first frame. The question is whether the audience sees it before Lena does.*
>
> *The show also does something rare in the thriller genre: it takes grief seriously. Lena's trauma is not backstory — it is the lens through which she sees the world. Her ability to profile is inseparable from her wound. The show does not heal her. It asks whether healing is the right goal, or whether the wound is the instrument.*
