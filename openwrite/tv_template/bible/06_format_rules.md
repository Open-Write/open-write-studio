# Format Rules — TV Script

*Adapted from screenplay format rules for episodic television. **THIS FILE MUST BE RELOADED FOR EVERY SCENE.** It is the discipline document. If the writer loses it, the script swells.*

*The core principles are identical to the screenplay format rules — no camera directions, no emotional parentheticals, trust the actor, subtext not statement. The TV-specific adaptations are marked with [TV].*

*See also: [`07_craft_feeling.md`](07_craft_feeling.md) for emotional execution standards, [`01_series_concept.md`](01_series_concept.md) for series concept.*

---

## I. THE DISCIPLINE

**This document is a bible. The script is not a bible.** Everything in the episode outline — every character profile, every emotional palette, every scene-level intent — is *understanding*. It is what the writer must hold internally before writing. It is not what the page should contain. A scene whose outline entry runs three hundred words may, on the page, run twelve lines of action and dialogue. The compression is not a loss. It is the entire point.

**This is a spec script, not a shooting script.** A spec script is written to be read by people who decide whether to make the show. A shooting script is a production document with scene numbers, camera directions, and technical specifications. Produce the first. Most failure modes in AI-written scripts come from accidentally producing the second.

---

## II. WHAT IS FORBIDDEN

### No Camera Directions

None. Not even "we see." The director chooses shots.

Forbidden:
- CUT TO, ANGLE ON, CLOSE-UP, PAN, PUSH IN, MATCH CUT, INSERT
- "We see," "the camera follows," "the camera reveals"
- POV shots described in action lines
- Any shot specification

Allowed:
- Standard slug lines: INT./EXT., location, time of day
- Rare structural devices: INTERCUT, SERIES OF SHOTS (sparingly)

**[TV] Note:** TV directors rotate. Unlike a film where one director may shoot the entire script, TV episodes are directed by different people each week. Camera directions are even less appropriate — the director of Episode 3 is not the director of Episode 7, and each brings their own visual language.

### No Emotional Parentheticals

A parenthetical (the small italicized direction beneath a character's name) should be used only when:
- (a) It disambiguates the addressee: *(to Theo, not Daniel)*
- (b) It specifies an action during dialogue: *(picking up the photograph)*
- (c) It specifies delivery method: *(into phone)*

Never for emotion:
- FORBIDDEN: (angrily), (sadly), (quietly), (nervously), (softly), (holding back tears)
- If the dialogue doesn't carry the emotion, rewrite the dialogue.

**Target:** Under 3 parentheticals per episode. Over 5 is a red flag.

### No Adverbs in Dialogue Tags

- FORBIDDEN: "She says quietly." "He asks nervously." "They whisper urgently."
- If the dialogue needs an adverb to convey its meaning, the dialogue is wrong.

### No Interiority in Action Lines

- FORBIDDEN: "Lena remembers the funeral." "Marcus thinks about his dead informant."
- ALLOWED: "Lena's hand stops on the photograph." "Marcus sets his coffee down. Doesn't pick it up."

The interiority is the actor's job. The page gives them only what the camera could see.

### No Emotional Palette Annotations on the Page

The outline's emotional palette annotations are the writer's understanding, not the script's content. They do not appear in the screenplay. If you find yourself writing *(palette: grief and awe)* under a slug line, you have confused the documents.

### No Voice Register Names in Action Lines

- FORBIDDEN: "The Analyst takes over." "Her frightened self surfaces."
- ALLOWED: "Her spine straightens. Her voice flattens." "Her hand shakes. She puts it in her pocket."

The voice architecture is the writer's understanding of the character. It produces distinct dialogue and behavior. It never appears on the page.

## III. WHAT IS REQUIRED

### Dialogue Is Subtext, Not Statement

Characters do not say what they mean. A character does not say "I am terrified." A character says "I'm fine. Pass the salt." The audience understands the terror from what the character doesn't say, from the pause before "fine," from the way they reach for the salt.

**[TV] Note:** TV has more dialogue than film. This makes subtext even more important — with more words, the temptation to let characters state their feelings increases. Resist. Every line of dialogue that names an emotional state directly is a line that needs to be rewritten.

### Trust the Actor

The action line says what is happening. The dialogue says what is said. The actor and director find the rest. Never direct a performance from the page.

### Action Lines: Short, Present Tense, Concrete

- Action blocks: no longer than 3-4 lines on the page
- Use white space. A single sentence alone on its line is often the right choice.
- Action describes what is visible and audible — never what a character is thinking or feeling.
- Present tense, always.

### Body Anchors

Every scene must have at least one physical grounding — hands, eyes, breath, spine, jaw, feet. Bodies communicate what dialogue cannot. Lead with bodies, not concepts.

### Silence

The most important moments are not dialogue. Resist the impulse to fill. A scene where two people sit in a car and don't speak can be the most powerful scene in the episode.

## IV. TV-SPECIFIC FORMAT RULES

### [TV] Slug Lines

Standard TV slug lines:

```
INT. ABU FIELD OFFICE - DAY
EXT. PARKING LOT - NIGHT
INT. LENA'S APARTMENT - LIVING ROOM - CONTINUOUS
```

- Always INT. or EXT.
- Location is specific (not "OFFICE" — "ABU FIELD OFFICE")
- Time of day: DAY, NIGHT, DAWN, DUSK, CONTINUOUS, LATER
- For split locations: INT./EXT. CAR - MOVING - NIGHT

### [TV] Cold Open / Teaser

The cold open comes before the title sequence. It is typically 2-3 pages and ends on a hook.

```
COLD OPEN

INT. HOLDING CELL - NIGHT

[Scene content]

FADE TO:

TITLE SEQUENCE
```

### [TV] Act Breaks

For network TV with commercial breaks, mark act breaks clearly:

```
END OF ACT ONE

[Commercial break would go here]

ACT TWO

INT. ABU FIELD OFFICE - DAY
```

For streaming shows with no commercial breaks, act breaks are optional. If used, they mark structural turning points, not commercial interruptions.

### [TV] Episode Title Page

```
Title: THRESHOLD
Episode: S01E01 - "Pattern Recognition"
Credit: Written by [Creator Name]
Draft date: [Date]
```

### [TV] Series vs. Episode Format

TV scripts are episodic — each episode is a self-contained document. Unlike a feature screenplay where scenes are numbered sequentially from 1 to ~60, TV episodes restart scene numbering at 1 for each episode. This is standard.

### [TV] Page Count Targets

| Format | Target Pages | Runtime | Notes |
|--------|-------------|---------|-------|
| Half-hour comedy (single-cam) | 28-34 pages | 22-28 min | More dialogue, less action |
| Half-hour comedy (multi-cam) | 40-45 pages | 22 min | Different format (wider margins) |
| One-hour drama (network) | 55-65 pages | 42-48 min | Act breaks for commercials |
| One-hour drama (cable/streaming) | 55-70 pages | 48-58 min | More flexibility |
| Limited series | 55-65 pages | 48-58 min | Same as one-hour drama |

**The 1-page ≈ 1-minute rule** applies to TV as it does to film. A 60-page script will produce approximately 60 minutes of content.

### [TV] Character Introductions

When a character appears for the first time in the series, their name should be in CAPS in the action line, followed by a brief physical description:

```
LENA VASQUEZ (34, compact, with the kind of stillness that makes
people think she's shorter than she is) sets her coffee on the desk.
```

In subsequent episodes, character names are no longer in CAPS (unless they appear for the first time in that episode).

### [TV] Series Regulars vs. Guest Stars

- **Series regulars** appear in most episodes. Their introductions in the pilot are detailed; subsequent episodes assume the audience knows them.
- **Recurring characters** appear in multiple episodes. Reintroduce briefly if they've been absent for 2+ episodes.
- **Guest stars** appear in one episode (or a short arc). Full introduction required.

## V. LENGTH & COMPRESSION

### [TV] Per-Episode Page Budget

Each episode has a target page count determined by the format (see Section IV). The writer must stay within ±5 pages of the target. Over-length episodes are a production problem — they cost money and require cuts that the writer didn't choose.

### Re-Read for Discipline

After completing a scene, re-read it for clarity and economy. If critics later flag extraneous material, the cutter will address it.

- If a sentence doesn't earn its place, cut it
- If two sentences say the same thing, cut one
- If an adverb modifies a verb that already carries the meaning, cut the adverb
- White space is your friend

### [TV] The Cold Open Is Not a Teaser in Disguise

The cold open should do real narrative work — introduce the case, establish the tone, hook the audience. It is not a "previously on" or a montage. It is a scene. Treat it with the same discipline as every other scene.

---

## VI. WHAT TO DO WHEN IN DOUBT

If you are uncertain whether to include a description, an adverb, a parenthetical, an action beat, a line of dialogue — the answer is almost always to cut it. The outline is rich. The page is sparse. The reader fills in the rest. The actors fill in the rest. The director fills in the rest. The writer's job is to have understood the most and put the least on the page.

---

## VII. THE SUCCESS METRIC

The success metric for the script is not whether it captures every detail of the outline. The success metric is whether a reader, opening it cold at page one, recognizes within ten pages that they are reading something good — and whether by page thirty they would not stop. This requires confidence on the page, which requires restraint, which requires trust in the reader.
