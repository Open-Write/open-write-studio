## VI. CRAFT GUIDANCE — ON FEELING

[This section defines how emotion is rendered in the novel. The premise is intellectually rich, but premise is not story. What separates a novel from a Wikipedia article is that every chapter must do emotional work.]

**Every chapter must aim to produce at least two distinct emotions in the reader, often in tension.** A chapter of pure grief is sentimental. A chapter of pure awe is tedious. A chapter where a mother weeps over her dead daughter while feeling, for the first time, faintly grateful that the loss has cracked her open enough to be reached by something — that is fiction.

**Lead with bodies, not concepts.** The reader experiences the story through the protagonist's hand on a doorknob, their breath catching, their eyes refusing to meet. The science/speculation/magic is not the feeling. The hand is the feeling. When in doubt, write the body — the breath held, the eyes that won't meet, the hand that almost reaches and doesn't.

**Trust silence.** The most important moments in a novel are not dialogue. Resist the impulse to fill. A single true sentence followed by white space does more work than a paragraph of explanation.

**No villain.** Every character — including antagonists — must be given the most articulate possible version of their position. The reader must be able to hear the antagonist's argument and feel its pull. The novel fails if anyone is a foil. The novel succeeds if every character is the protagonist of the story they are telling themselves.

**Use the voice architecture for every line.** When writing a character's line, ask: *which emotional register is speaking?* The richest moments are when one part is talking and another is leaking through — when the protagonist gives a precise scientific answer in a voice that is starting to crack. That crack is the scene.

**Grief/emotion is the engine, not the subject.** The novel is not *about* the loss. The novel is about the characters and the world. The loss is the wound through which the cosmic and the domestic become the same scale. Treat it with restraint. The less it is shown directly, the more the absence does the work.

**Pace by emotional rhythm, not by plot beat.** A novel like this earns its climaxes by patience. The scene where the protagonist holds their child must be allowed to breathe. Resist pacing that serves plot over feeling.

**The science/speculation must feel earned.** Every ability, every rule, every piece of world-building should be set up before it is used. Magic in a literary novel is a failure of structure.

**Last and most important.** The success of this novel is not measured by how clever the premise is. It is measured by whether, six weeks after reading it, a reader who has experienced the novel's central emotion still thinks about the protagonist, and whether a reader who has not yet faced that experience begins, quietly, to wonder about it. The premise is the scaffolding. The feeling is the building.
