## VII. ON WRITING THE SCRIPT — RESTRAINT, FORMAT, AND THE SUBMISSION STANDARD

A few honest words about what this document is and isn't, and what the writer is being asked to deliver.

**This document is a bible. The screenplay is not a bible.** Everything in this outline — every character profile, every scene-level intent — is *understanding*. It is what the writer must hold internally before writing. It is not what the page should contain. A scene whose bible entry runs three hundred words may, on the page, run twelve lines of action and dialogue. The compression is not a loss. It is the entire point.

**This is a spec script, not a shooting script.** This is the most important single instruction in this section. A spec script is a sales document — a demonstration of voice, structure, and craft, written to be read by people who decide whether to make the film. A shooting script is a production document, written after the script has been bought, populated with scene numbers, camera directions, and technical specifications. Most failure modes in AI-written screenplays come from accidentally producing the second.

**No camera directions. None.** No CUT TO, no ANGLE ON, no CLOSE-UP, no PAN, no PUSH IN, no MATCH CUT, no INSERT. The director will choose the shots. The writer suggests rhythm and emphasis through the action lines and through where the page break falls — never through camera language. The single exception is standard slug lines (INT./EXT., location, time of day) and rare structural devices like INTERCUT or SERIES OF SHOTS where the structure genuinely requires it. Use those sparingly. Otherwise: nothing.

**Trust the actor.** Never direct a performance from the page. Do not write *[Character] looks devastated.* Do not write *(softly, holding back tears)* under a character name. The action line says what is happening; the dialogue says what is said. The actor and director will find the rest. Adverbs in dialogue tags and emotional descriptors in action lines are the most common AI-screenplay tell. They must be cut almost entirely.

**Parentheticals: rare and functional only.** A parenthetical (the small italicized direction beneath a character's name, before their dialogue) should be used only when (a) it disambiguates the literal meaning of a line — *(to Theo, not Daniel)* when the addressee isn't obvious, or *(into phone)* — or (b) it specifies an action that happens inside the dialogue, like *(picking up the photograph)*. It is *never* used to direct emotion: *(angrily)*, *(sadly)*, *(quietly)*, *(nervously)*. If the dialogue does not communicate the emotion on its own, the dialogue is wrong; rewrite the dialogue. Across this entire screenplay, the total count of parentheticals should number under twenty.

**Action lines: short, present tense, concrete.** Action blocks should generally run no longer than three or four lines on the page. Long action paragraphs are a sign of a writer who hasn't done the work of compression. Use white space. A single sentence, alone on its line, is often the right choice for a moment of weight. Action describes what is visible and audible — never what a character is *thinking* or *feeling*.

**Dialogue: subtext, not statement.** The characters do not say what they mean. Subtext is not optional — it is the medium. Every time the writer finds itself writing dialogue in which a character names their own emotional state directly, the line is wrong. Rewrite until the character is talking about something else.

**No emotional palette annotations on the page.** The bible's per-scene palette annotations are the writer's understanding, not the script's content. They do not appear in the screenplay.

**Length: 110–120 pages, hard ceiling 125.** AI models reliably overwrite first drafts; the writer must build in a pass at the end specifically to cut. Anything over 125 pages will be flagged as undisciplined by readers before they have read a word.

**Re-read for discipline.** After completing a scene, re-read it for clarity and economy. If critics later flag extraneous material, the cutter will address it. Do not run a mandatory cut pass.

**Format: Fountain markup, professional spec layout.** The script should be written in Fountain and converted to industry-standard PDF using the tooling provided. Standard spec layout — Courier 12, 1.5" left margin, 1" right and bottom, character names centered, dialogue blocks roughly 3.5" wide. No scene numbers. No headers or footers beyond the page number in the upper right. Title page contains: title, *Original Screenplay by [author]*, contact information. That is all.

### Fountain Syntax Reference

```
Title: My Screenplay
Credit: Original Screenplay by
Author: Author Name
Draft date: YYYY-MM-DD

FADE IN:

INT. LOCATION - TIME OF DAY

Action line describing what happens.

CHARACTER NAME
(parenthetical if needed)
Dialogue line.

Another action line.

FADE OUT.
```

**Key Fountain rules:**
- Scene headings: Start with INT. or EXT. — always ALL CAPS
- Character names: ALL CAPS, on their own line, before dialogue
- Parentheticals: In parentheses, on their own line between character name and dialogue
- Transitions: ALL CAPS ending with TO: (e.g., FADE IN:, CUT TO:)
- Page breaks: `===` on its own line
- Centered text: `> text <`

**What to do when in doubt.** If uncertain whether to include a description, an adverb, a parenthetical, an action beat, a line of dialogue — the answer is almost always to cut it. The bible is rich. The page is sparse. The reader fills in the rest. The actors fill in the rest. The director fills in the rest. The writer's job, in a spec, is to have understood the most and put the least on the page.

**One last thing.** The success metric for the script is not whether it captures every detail of the bible. The success metric is whether a contest reader, opening it cold at page one, recognizes within ten pages that they are reading something good — and whether by page thirty they would not put it down. This requires confidence on the page, which requires restraint, which requires trust in the reader.
