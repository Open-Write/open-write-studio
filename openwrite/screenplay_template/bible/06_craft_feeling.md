## VI. CRAFT GUIDANCE — ON FEELING

This is the most important section. The premise is intellectually rich, but premise is not story. What separates this from a Wikipedia article is that every scene must do emotional work. Below is the standard.

**Every scene must aim to produce at least two distinct emotions in the audience, often in tension.** A scene of pure grief is sentimental. A scene of pure awe is tedious. A scene where [character] weeps while feeling, for the first time, faintly grateful that the loss has cracked them open enough to be reached by something — that is cinema.

**Lead with bodies, not concepts.** [The sci-fi/fantasy elements] are not the feeling. The hand is the feeling. When in doubt, write the body — the breath held, the eyes that won't meet, the hand that almost reaches and doesn't.

**Trust silence.** The most important moments are not dialogue. Resist the impulse to fill. Cinema is a medium of withholding.

**No villain.** Every character must be given the most articulate possible version of their position. The audience must be able to hear [antagonist]'s argument and feel its pull. The film fails if anyone is a foil. The film succeeds if every character is the protagonist of the story they are telling themselves.

**Use the voice architecture for every line.** When writing a character's line, ask: *which emotional register is speaking?* [Character A's Scientist] sounds entirely different from [Character A's Mother]. The richest moments are when one part is talking and another is leaking through — when [character] gives a precise scientific answer in a voice that is starting to crack. That crack is the scene.

**Grief is the engine, not the subject.** The film is not *about* [the loss]. The film is about [the larger story]. [The loss] is the wound through which the cosmic and the domestic become the same scale. Treat it with restraint.

**Pace by emotional rhythm, not by plot beat.** A film like this earns its climaxes by patience. Resist Hollywood-paced cuts. The audience is being asked to feel the scale of a loss that does not fit on a screen — they need time to find the size of it.

**The science must feel earned.** Every [world rule] and every [ability] should be set up before it is used. Magic in a science-fiction film is a failure of structure.

**Hold the misdirections.** [Describe the misdirection architecture. What does the audience believe in each act? When does the truth emerge?]

**Last and most important.** The success of this film is not measured by how clever the premise is. It is measured by whether, six weeks after seeing it, a viewer who has experienced [the central loss] still thinks about [the key image], and whether a viewer who has not yet faced their own [mortality/threshold/question] begins, quietly, to wonder about it. The premise is the scaffolding. The [emotion] is the building.
